MEC03 · Motion & forces

Projectile motion

In a physics lab, a spring gun fires one ball sideways at the same instant that a second ball is simply dropped. You hear one thud, not two. Sideways speed does not slow the fall by even a hair.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

Motion splits into two independent halves. Sideways, nothing changes the speed, so the ball covers equal distances in equal times. Downwards, gravity speeds it up exactly as it would a dropped ball. Put them together and you get a parabola, and for a ball thrown sideways from a height, the time in the air is set by that height alone, not by how hard it was thrown.

0 · The story

The apparatus is a classroom favourite. Two metal balls sit at the same height. A spring-loaded plunger hits one of them sideways at the very instant that it releases the other to fall straight down. The first ball sets off on a long curved arc. The second just drops.

You close your eyes and listen. Everyone expects two sounds: first the ball that simply falls, then the one that has to travel sideways as well. What you hear is a single clack. Both balls hit the floor at exactly the same moment.

It feels wrong. The thrown ball has so much further to go. Surely it should take longer. This chapter explains why it doesn’t, and why that tiny fact made modern ballistics, and later the idea of an orbit, possible.

1 · The illusion

“Throw something harder and it falls more slowly”

Everyday experience seems to confirm this. A stone thrown hard seems to “hang” in the air for longer than one dropped. For centuries scholars described a thrown object as moving in a straight line under its “impetus” until the impetus ran out, and then dropping vertically.

What this misses is that going sideways and going down are two separate things. Gravity pulls only downwards. It does nothing sideways, and the sideways motion cannot affect the downward motion in any way. The simulation lets you check.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 350 BCEAristotle describes thrown objects as carried by the air, then falling straight down when that push runs out.
  2. 1537Niccolò Tartaglia publishes Nova Scientia, the first mathematical study of cannon fire. He correctly finds that a cannon fires farthest when pointed at about 45°, but still draws shots as a straight line, a curve and then a straight drop.
  3. 1638Galileo, in Two New Sciences, shows that a projectile’s motion is two independent motions combined, steady sideways and accelerating downwards, and that the path is therefore a parabola.
  4. 1680sNewton extends the same idea to the Moon: an orbit is a projectile that is fast enough to keep missing the ground (see gravity as a field).
  5. 1900s–Ballistic firing tables, sports analytics and computer game engines all rely on separating the horizontal and vertical motions.

3 · The model

The teal ball is dropped from a 30 m cliff. The amber ball is fired sideways from the same height at the same instant, at a speed you choose. The white lines join the two balls every quarter second.

  4. Look at the spacing along the amber ball’s path: steady sideways, growing downward. That mismatch is what bends the path into a parabola.

4 · The reveal

Two motions that never talk to each other

Imagine you are looking down from directly above: you see only the sideways motion, which is steady, because nothing pushes sideways. Now imagine looking from the side with a very thin slit that only shows height: you see only the fall, which is exactly the fall of a dropped ball, because gravity doesn’t care how fast the ball is moving sideways. What you actually see is these two combined: a smooth curve.

That is the whole idea. The sideways speed and the vertical speed are components, as in the arrow chapter, and each follows its own simple rule. The flight time is the time to fall, and the distance is the sideways speed times that time. For a ball launched at an angle, you break its launch speed into a sideways part and an upwards part, then use the same two rules. The time in the air then depends on the upward part too: from level ground it is 2 × (upward speed) ÷ g, so a harder throw at a given angle stays up longer.

Throwing farther

To go farthest at a given speed, you want to spend as long as possible in the air and as much of the speed as possible moving sideways. Those two goals pull in opposite directions: a steep throw stays up long but goes mostly up, a flat one goes sideways but lands quickly. In a vacuum, launching and landing at the same height, the best compromise is exactly 45° (from a cliff the best angle is lower).

The surprise

For a sideways throw, throwing harder does not buy you more time in the air, only more distance: the time is fixed by the height. This is also why an orbit works: go sideways fast enough and the ground curves away as quickly as you fall.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now every video game with a thrown grenade or a jumping character is splitting motion into a sideways part and a vertical part, sixty times a second, using exactly these two rules. Tennis and cricket tracking systems like Hawk-Eye combine cameras with the same split (adding air drag and spin) to show the path of a ball. And the water arcing from a garden fountain is a bundle of tiny projectiles, each following a parabola.

  • SportFree throws, goal kicks, javelin and golf all depend on choosing the launch angle and speed.
  • Ballistics and artilleryFiring tables are projectile motion with a correction for air resistance.
  • Firefighting and irrigationWater jets follow parabolic paths, so nozzles are aimed with this in mind.
  • Computer games and animationPhysics engines use exactly this independence of components.
  • Space flightAn orbit is a projectile fast enough to keep missing the ground.
  • Volcanology and engineeringThe reach of thrown rocks (“volcanic bombs”) is estimated from projectile motion.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

There is no air in the simulation. Real balls and bullets are slowed by drag, which couples the sideways and downward motions together, so real paths are not exact parabolas and the 45° rule becomes a lower angle. The ground is flat and gravity is the same at every height, which is fine over a few hundred metres but not for artillery shells flying tens of kilometres, where the curvature of the Earth matters. The balls have no spin and are treated as points.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Sideways and down are separate. Each follows its own simple rule.
  2. Fall time depends on height only for a sideways throw. A harder throw goes farther, not longer.
  3. The path is a parabola. A steady sideways motion plus a steadily growing fall.

Glossary and sources

Projectile
An object moving under gravity alone after being launched.
Component
The part of a velocity along one direction (sideways or vertical).
Parabola
The curve y = ax² you get by combining steady motion and steady acceleration.
Range
The horizontal distance a projectile travels.
