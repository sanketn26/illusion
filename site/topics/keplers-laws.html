GRV05 · Gravity & orbits

Kepler’s laws

Johannes Kepler spent years trying to fit a circle to the orbit of Mars and was off by an amount so small you could barely see it. He refused to ignore it, and in doing so, reinvented astronomy.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Planets move on ellipses with the Sun at one focus. A planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so it moves fastest when closest to the Sun. And the square of a planet’s year is proportional to the cube of its distance: the same rule fits every planet.

0 · The story

When the Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe died in 1601, he left behind something no one had ever had: thirty years of planetary observations accurate to about one minute of arc, around a thirtieth of the width of the Moon. His young assistant Johannes Kepler inherited them, and set himself the task of working out the true orbit of Mars.

For years Kepler tried circles, with the Earth and Mars on shifted circular tracks. After a long and brutal set of calculations he found a circle that fitted nearly all of Tycho’s data, except for two places where it was off by eight minutes of arc. Eight minutes is a fraction of a degree: about a quarter of the width of the full Moon.

A less honest man would have called it a good fit. Kepler wrote later: “If I had believed that we could ignore these eight minutes, I would have patched up my hypothesis accordingly. But, since it was not permissible to ignore, those eight minutes pointed the road to a complete reformation of astronomy.” The orbit was not a circle at all.

1 · The illusion

“Planets move at a steady pace on perfect circles”

For two thousand years the heavens were thought to be made of perfect circles at constant speed. It was beautiful, simple, and what everyone expected. Even Copernicus kept the circles.

The planets don’t do this. They speed up as they near the Sun and slow as they move away, along squashed circles called ellipses. The simulation lets you stretch an orbit and watch what happens to the speed.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1576–1601Tycho Brahe builds an observatory on the island of Hven and records the positions of the planets with unprecedented accuracy.
  2. 1609Kepler publishes his first two laws in Astronomia Nova: orbits are ellipses, and a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
  3. 1619In Harmonices Mundi, Kepler announces the third law: the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the orbit’s size.
  4. 1687Newton shows that all three laws follow from an inverse-square pull. The second law is conservation of angular momentum.
  5. 1758Halley’s comet returns on schedule, as predicted from Newton’s laws sixteen years after Halley’s death.

3 · The model

A planet moves around the Sun. You can stretch the orbit (the eccentricity, e) and choose which planet. The coloured wedges are twelve equal slices of time. The speed readout uses the real motion of the chosen planet. The table at the right lists the real data for the six classical planets.

1) ellipse, Sun at a focus 2) equal areas in equal times 3) T² = a³ (T in years, a in AU)The second law means the planet moves fastest at its closest point (perihelion). The ratio of the fastest to slowest speeds is (1 + e)/(1 − e).

Try this

  1. Set the stretch to 0. The orbit is a circle, the wedges are identical, and the planet’s speed never changes.
  2. Increase the stretch to about 0.6. The wedges near the Sun are short and wide, the ones far away long and thin, yet all have the same area.
  3. Watch the speed readout. The planet moves several times faster at its nearest point than its farthest.
  4. Look at the table’s last column: year² ÷ a³ is 1.00 for every planet, from Mercury to Saturn. That is the third law.

4 · The reveal

Three laws, one cause

Kepler found the laws from data, without knowing why they held. Newton supplied the why. If the Sun pulls with a force that falls as the inverse square of distance (the previous chapter), then every one of Kepler’s laws follows: ellipses, equal areas, and the 2-to-3 power relationship.

The second law is the most surprising. “Equal areas in equal times” is the same statement as conservation of angular momentum, the rule you saw with the spinning skater: as a planet moves nearer the Sun its “radius” shrinks, so it must speed up, just as the skater speeds up by pulling in their arms. The force of the Sun points along the line to the planet, so it exerts no twist, and the angular momentum cannot change.

One constant for the whole family

The third law says something remarkable: the ratio T²/a³ is the same for every planet. Planets that differ in size, mass and distance are all instances of one rule, with one constant set by the Sun’s mass. Measure the year and orbit of any moon or planet around any star, and you can weigh the star. Astronomers use this every day to find the masses of stars and distant planets.

The surprise

A small, stubborn discrepancy, eight minutes of arc, was enough to overturn two thousand years of circles. Real physics is often hiding in the part of the data that doesn’t quite fit.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now NASA’s Parker Solar Probe is flying round the Sun on an orbit that takes just 88 days, and at its closest approach it is moving at about 190 kilometres per second, the fastest humans have ever sent anything. That is Kepler’s second law at work. Earth itself is also fastest in early January, when it is nearest the Sun, at about 30.3 km/s against 29.3 km/s in July.

  • Space missionsMission planners use the laws to schedule launches and gravity assists.
  • Weighing stars and planetsThe third law turns an orbit’s size and period into the mass of what it circles.
  • ExoplanetsThe period of a planet’s transits tells astronomers how far it is from its star.
  • Comets and asteroidsHalley’s comet, with a period of about 76 years, is predicted by the same laws; it is next due in 2061.
  • SatellitesEvery satellite obeys the same three laws around the Earth.
  • Calendars and seasonsEarth’s changing speed makes the seasons slightly unequal in length.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The planet moves on an exact ellipse around a fixed Sun, with no other planets pulling on it. Real orbits are slightly disturbed by neighbours, and the ellipses slowly turn. Real planets have very small eccentricities (Earth’s is 0.017, and Mercury’s 0.21), so the large values of the slider are what-if cases, not real planets. The animation runs at an easy speed, one lap per 12 seconds, for every planet, so it does not show their real relative years; those are in the table. The twelve wedges are exact slices of the true motion.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Ellipses, not circles. The Sun sits at one focus.
  2. Equal areas in equal times. A planet speeds up as it nears the Sun.
  3. T² ∝ a³. One rule for every planet, set by the central mass.

Glossary and sources

Ellipse
A squashed circle; the path of a planet.
Eccentricity (e)
How squashed an ellipse is: 0 for a circle, close to 1 for a long thin one.
Perihelion / aphelion
The closest and farthest points of an orbit around the Sun.
AU
The astronomical unit: the average Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million km.
