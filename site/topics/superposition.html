OSC09 · Oscillations & waves

Superposition

An orchestra of eighty musicians pushes on the same air. At your seat, the air can only do one thing at a time. Yet you can pick out the oboe. The explanation is one of the most useful ideas in all of wave physics.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–9 min

The 30-second version

When two waves meet, the medium just adds their heights. Where they overlap you get a bigger bump, a smaller bump, or none at all, and then each carries on exactly as before, as if the other had never been there. That is why one patch of air can hold a whole orchestra, and a single antenna a whole radio band.

0 · The story

An orchestra has eighty musicians. Every violin, flute, trombone and drum is pushing on the same air. At the seat where you sit, the air is only a few millimetres of motion, one pattern of back-and-forth squeezing against your eardrum. Yet from that single wiggle your brain can separate out the oboe from the cellos, and even follow a single voice in a choir.

How can one motion hold so much? Because the motions add. The sound of the oboe is a small wiggle, the cellos another, the drum another, and the air does their sum. And your ear and brain are good at taking a sum apart.

The same applies to the ripples on a pond. Two stones thrown in make two sets of ripples. They cross, and each continues unchanged, as if the other did not exist.

1 · The illusion

“When waves collide, something has to give”

Two cars meeting head-on bounce off or crumple. Two balls collide and fly apart. It seems that anything meeting head-on should be altered. So when two waves cross, we expect a muddle, or a clash that changes them both.

Waves do not behave that way. They meet, add, and pass on, unchanged. The simulation lets you send two pulses along a string and watch.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1753Daniel Bernoulli states the “principle of the coexistence of small oscillations”: a vibrating string can carry many vibrations at once, and the total is their sum.
  2. 1801Thomas Young uses the principle to explain the stripes made by light passing through two slits.
  3. 1822Joseph Fourier shows that any repeating wave can be written as a sum of simple sine waves.
  4. 1834John Scott Russell watches a solitary mound of water travel along a canal for miles without changing shape, a wave of a different kind that does not simply add, and a reminder that superposition has limits.
  5. 1863Helmholtz argues that the ear works by breaking the sum of many sounds into its separate pitches.

3 · The model

Two pulses run towards each other along a string. The teal one comes from the left; the amber one from the right. The upper picture shows each by itself. The lower picture shows what the string actually does: the sum. You choose the size and sign of the second pulse. The red bead sits in the middle of the string.

height of the string at a point = (height of wave 1) + (height of wave 2)This is the whole rule. It works because the equation for small waves is “linear”: add two solutions and you get another solution.

Try this

  1. With the second pulse at +100%, watch the middle: when they meet, the string rises to twice the height of either pulse.
  2. Set it to −100% (upside down). At the moment they meet, the string is flat. The bead does not move, and yet the pulses continue afterwards.
  3. Try +50%. The middle reaches 1.5 times the height of the big one: the pulses simply add.
  4. Look at the pulses after they have passed. Each has the same shape, size and speed as before: nothing was lost.

4 · The reveal

Adding, not colliding

The reason is in the equation of small waves. It is “linear”: if one wave is a solution and another wave is a solution, then their sum is a solution too. Each piece of the string is pulled by its neighbours, and the pulls from two waves simply add up. Nothing about one wave changes the way the string responds to the other. So the string can carry any number of waves at once, each travelling on without regard to the others.

Cancellation is not destruction. When the pulses at −100% meet, the string is flat for an instant, yet each still has its energy: it is held as the motion of the string, which is moving fastest where the string is flat. A moment later, the pulses reappear exactly as they were.

Take a sum apart

Because waves add, you can go the other way: split a complicated wave into simple pieces. Fourier’s great discovery was that any repeating wave is a sum of simple sine waves of different frequencies. A radio receiver does exactly this, tuning in one sine wave and ignoring the others. So does your ear, which sorts a sound into pitches before your brain ever sees it.

The surprise

Waves do not interact. They pass through each other as if the other were a ghost, and the world they travel through is just the sum of all of them. That is what makes it possible for a single patch of air to carry a whole concert.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the air in the room around you is carrying dozens of radio and Wi-Fi signals at once: FM stations, mobile phone calls, Bluetooth, television, all crossing the same space without disturbing each other. At sea, groups of swells are overlapping, and where several crests happen to line up, they add into the rare, towering waves that sailors call freak waves. And in a pair of noise-cancelling headphones, a speaker is producing a wave that is the exact opposite of the engine noise, so that the sum at your ear is nearly silence.

  • Radio and telecommunicationsThousands of signals share the same air, each recovered by tuning.
  • Hearing and musicYour ear separates the sum of many sounds into individual pitches.
  • Noise cancellationA wave that is the opposite of the noise adds to it to give almost nothing.
  • Ocean wavesSwells from different storms add; freak waves come from lucky alignments.
  • Fourier analysisImage, sound and video compression all work by writing signals as sums of simple waves.
  • Light and quantum physicsInterference patterns are superposition in action.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The pulses are idealised humps on an endless, loss-free string, small enough that the equations are exactly linear. In real media, large waves do interact: water waves that are steep, loud shock waves, and light in some materials all break the rule. Real strings lose a little energy, and the pulses would slowly spread out. The bead is not a real object but a marker. The picture of the sum is drawn at the same scale as the separate pulses so you can see the addition.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Waves add. Where they overlap, the heights simply add together.
  2. Then each carries on unchanged. Waves pass through each other without damage.
  3. Sums can be taken apart. Any wave is a sum of simple waves, which is how radios and ears work.

Superposition

Linear

Constructive / destructive
Adding to a bigger / smaller disturbance.
