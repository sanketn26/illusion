Damping: how a suspension smooths bumps

Push down on a corner of a parked car. If it bobs up and down more than once or twice, the shock absorbers are worn out. They are not springs and they do not hold the car up. Their whole job is to stop the bouncing.

Reading time ≈ 11 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Anything that bounces will keep bouncing until something removes its energy. That something is damping: a drag that grows with speed. Too little and the car bounces for ever. Just enough, critical damping, and it settles fastest without overshooting. Too much and it is slow to recover and jolts you harder. Good suspensions sit somewhere in between.

0 · The story

Mechanics have a simple test for a car’s shock absorbers. They lean on one corner and push the body down hard, then let go. A healthy car comes back up once and settles. A car with worn shock absorbers bounces up, down, up again, and keeps going. Passengers in such a car feel it on every bump, and the tyres can lose contact with the road as they bounce.

The springs hold the car up. They also store energy when the car hits a bump, and give it back, which is exactly why the car bounces. Something has to take that energy out of the motion before the next bump arrives. That is the shock absorber, which is really a “damper”, a piston forcing oil through a small hole. It pushes back more strongly the faster the car moves up and down, and turns the energy of the bounce into heat.

Skyscrapers have the same problem. Taipei 101, once the tallest building in the world, sways in typhoons. High up inside it hangs a 660-tonne steel ball, a giant pendulum, that swings against the sway and soaks it up.

1 · The illusion

“A stiffer shock absorber is always better”

If bouncing is bad, more damping should be better. Make the shock absorber as stiff as you can and the car will never bounce. That is how many people think of it.

The trouble is that a very stiff damper is also a very stiff link to the road. When the wheel hits a bump, the damper drags the body upward immediately, and you feel the full jolt. The simulation lets you turn the damper from too soft to too stiff and feel the trade-off.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1850s–80sInstrument makers learn that sensitive meters, like galvanometers, need “critical” damping to settle on a reading quickly instead of swinging for minutes.
  2. 1908Maurice Houdaille patents a hydraulic shock absorber, using fluid forced through small passages.
  3. 1909Hermann Frahm patents the “dynamic vibration absorber”, a spring-mounted weight that soaks up a structure’s vibration, the ancestor of the tuned mass damper.
  4. 1928–34J. P. Den Hartog works out how to tune such absorbers, and writes the standard textbook, Mechanical Vibrations.
  5. 1977The Citicorp Center in New York becomes one of the first buildings to carry a tuned mass damper.
  6. 2004Taipei 101 opens with a 660-tonne steel ball damper visible to visitors.

3 · The model

This is a “quarter car”: one wheel, one spring, one damper, and a quarter of the car’s weight (300 kg) on top. The spring is fixed, so the body bounces at about 1.3 times a second. You choose the damper’s strength, from 5% to 200% of what is called critical damping, the amount that gives the fastest return without overshooting. Then press Hit a bump, or Washboard road for a series of small bumps.

damping ratio ζ = c / (2 √(k m)) ζ < 1: bounces ζ = 1: critical ζ > 1: over-dampedc is the damper’s strength, k the spring’s stiffness, m the mass. Each bounce is smaller than the last by the factor exp(−2πζ / √(1 − ζ²)). Cars use ζ ≈ 0.2 to 0.4.

Try this

  1. Set the damper to 10% and hit a bump. The body bounces many times, each bounce about half the last. That is a worn shock absorber.
  2. Set it to 30%, typical for a car. It rebounds once and settles in about a second and a half.
  3. Set it to 100%, critical. It returns without a single overshoot, but note the biggest jolt figure: it is much larger than before.
  4. Set it to 200%. The body is dragged by the wheel, so the jolt is the worst of all. Press Washboard road to see how each setting copes with repeated bumps.

4 · The reveal

Three regimes

With light damping, the energy of each bounce is only slowly taken away, so the body keeps oscillating, each swing a bit smaller. With critical damping, the energy is removed just fast enough that the body returns to rest without overshooting: it is the fastest way to settle. With heavy damping, the damper resists motion so strongly that the body crawls back slowly, and, more importantly, passes the shock of the wheel almost directly to the passengers.

That is why cars are tuned for a damping ratio of 0.2 to 0.4 rather than 1. At that setting the car settles in about one and a half bounces and, crucially, passes only a mild jolt on the first one. Racing cars use stiffer damping to keep the tyres in contact, giving up comfort. The “best” setting depends on what you are trying to protect.

Soaking up a building’s sway

For a skyscraper, a big damper in the foundation would do nothing: the sway is at the top. So engineers hang a heavy weight near the top on a pendulum tuned to the building’s own sway rate, so that the weight swings out of step with the building and absorbs its energy through a shock-absorbing link. In a strong typhoon the ball in Taipei 101 has been reported to swing by roughly a metre, and the tower’s own sway is cut by about a third.

The surprise

The thing that makes a car comfortable is not what holds it up, it’s the part that does nothing when the car is still. A damper only works while things are moving, and a strong damper can be just as uncomfortable as no damper at all.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now every car on the road is carrying four shock absorbers working exactly this way, tuned by engineers to a damping ratio of around 0.3. In Taipei, a 660-tonne steel sphere hangs from cables inside the 101-storey tower, tuned to its sway; it was designed to swing like a pendulum in typhoon winds. And in your home, the door closer on the fire door, the slow-closing lid on a toilet and the soft landing of a drawer are all dampers, turning the energy of motion into heat.

  • Cars and trainsDampers tuned to about ζ = 0.3 trade off comfort and road grip.
  • Skyscrapers and bridgesTuned mass dampers cut wind-driven sway.
  • Earthquake protectionDampers and isolators in buildings take energy out of ground shaking.
  • InstrumentsMeters and balances use near-critical damping to read quickly without overshoot.
  • Everyday objectsDoor closers, soft-close hinges and gas struts on car boots all damp motion.
  • Sports and wearablesRunning-shoe midsoles and bike forks damp the shock of each footfall or bump.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is a “quarter-car”: one wheel and a quarter of the body. It ignores the tyre’s own springiness and the wheel’s weight, which matter in real suspensions. The damper here is perfectly linear: the force is proportional to speed. Real shock absorbers use valves, and typically resist stretching more than compressing. The bump is a single smooth hill, and the jolt figure is a rough measure of the body’s peak acceleration, not a comfort rating. The settling times are estimates, based on the usual 2% rule.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Damping takes energy out. Without it, a bounce would carry on for ever.
  2. Critical is the fastest settle. No overshoot, but not the gentlest ride.
  3. Too stiff is harsh. A strong damper passes the shock of the road straight to you.

Glossary and sources

Damping
A drag that turns the energy of oscillation into heat, growing with speed.
Damping ratio (ζ)
The actual damping divided by the critical damping.
Critical damping
The amount that returns a system to rest fastest without overshooting.
Tuned mass damper
A weight on a spring or pendulum tuned to a structure’s sway rate to cancel it.
