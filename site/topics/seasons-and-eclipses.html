GRV10 · Gravity & orbits

Seasons, moon phases and eclipses

In early January the Earth is closer to the Sun than at any other time of year, and the northern half is in the depths of winter. The distance does not cause the seasons. A tilt does.

Reading time ≈ 11 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

The seasons come from the Earth’s tilt, not its distance from the Sun: tilting a hemisphere towards the Sun gives it higher, stronger sunlight and longer days. Moon phases come from seeing different parts of the Moon’s lit half as it goes round. Eclipses are rare because the Moon’s orbit is tilted too, so the three bodies seldom line up.

0 · The story

On the first days of January, Earth reaches its closest point to the Sun: about 147 million kilometres. Six months later, in early July, it is farthest, about 152 million. The difference, about 3%, is real, but it has almost no effect on our weather. In the north, January is cold. In Australia and Argentina, it is the height of summer.

If distance made the seasons, the whole planet would be warm at the same time and cold at the same time. It isn’t: when it is summer in Sydney, it is winter in Stockholm, and the Earth is the same distance from the Sun for both.

Something else must be doing it, and the same “something else”, a tilt, will help explain why the Moon changes shape and why we see an eclipse only now and then.

1 · The illusion

“Seasons come from the distance to the Sun”

It feels natural: closer to a fire is hotter. A great many people, including many who teach it, think summer is when Earth is nearer the Sun. But the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time, which settles it.

What changes is how directly the sunlight strikes the ground, and for how long. The simulation lets you tilt the Earth, or not, and watch what happens.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 270 BCEAristarchus uses the shape of the Moon at first quarter to estimate how much farther the Sun is than the Moon. His geometry is right, but his measurement too rough: he finds about 20 times, and the real figure is about 400.
  2. c. 230 BCEEratosthenes measures the tilt of the Earth’s axis at about 23.9°, remarkably close to today’s 23.4°.
  3. 1715Edmond Halley predicts the path and timing of a total solar eclipse over England to within a few minutes, a triumph for the new Newtonian astronomy.
  4. 1919Arthur Eddington photographs stars near the eclipsed Sun and measures their positions, a test of Einstein’s theory of gravity.
  5. 1993Jacques Laskar shows that the Moon helps keep the Earth’s tilt steady; without it the tilt could wander wildly.

3 · The model

The Earth moves around the Sun, with its axis always pointing in the same direction in space. When the northern end leans towards the Sun (left of the picture), the north has summer; half a year later it leans away, and the north has winter. The graphs show, for the latitude you choose, the height of the midday Sun and the length of the day through the year. Axial tilt is adjustable, even down to zero.

  2. At 45° N, compare the midday Sun in June (about 68° high) with December (about 22°). The day is about 15.4 hours long in June and 8.6 hours in December.
  3. Set the tilt to 0. The graphs go flat: no change in the Sun’s height, no change in day length: no tilt-driven seasons. (The Earth–Sun distance still varies a few percent, giving about 7% more sunlight in January than July, the same worldwide, a weak effect compared with tilt.)
  4. Set the tilt to 45° and the latitude to 0 and then 80: large tilt means extreme seasons, and near the pole the Sun sets for months.

4 · The reveal

Tilt, sunlight and day length

Imagine holding a torch above a table: shine it straight down and you get a small bright circle; tilt it and the same light spreads over a larger, dimmer oval, like the inverse-square spreading from the first chapter. The same happens with sunlight. When your hemisphere leans towards the Sun the light comes in steeply and concentrated, and the day is long, so the ground and air get more energy. When it leans away, the light is slanted and spread out, and the day short.

At the equator, the Sun is always high and the days are always about 12 hours: no big seasons. Near the poles the tilt dominates, with six months of day and six of night. Mid-latitudes get the clearest swing, as the graphs show.

Moon phases and eclipses

Half of the Moon is always lit by the Sun, as is half of the Earth. As the Moon moves around us, we see more or less of its lit half (the cycle of phases takes 29.5 days, a little longer than the 27.3 days the Moon takes to orbit the Earth, because the Earth has moved on round the Sun meanwhile): a thin crescent when it’s nearly in front of the Sun, a full circle when it is behind us. The phases are not caused by the Earth’s shadow: that only happens in a lunar eclipse.

If the Moon’s orbit were in the same plane as the Earth’s, we would have a solar eclipse at every new Moon and a lunar eclipse at every full Moon. But the orbit is tilted by about 5°, so most months the Moon passes just above or below the Sun and its shadow misses the Earth. Eclipses can occur only twice a year or so, during “eclipse seasons”, when the Moon is crossing the plane. And total solar eclipses are possible only because of a lucky coincidence: the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so they look the same size.

The surprise

Three of the sky’s most familiar patterns, seasons, phases and eclipses, come from just tilts and alignments. And the total solar eclipse is a cosmic accident: as the Moon drifts outward, it will one day be too small to cover the Sun.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now, at mid-latitudes, the length of daylight is changing at its fastest around the equinoxes, by up to about three minutes every day, and hardly at all near the solstices. Somewhere on Earth there are at least two solar eclipses and as many as five in a year. And at this moment the Moon is moving away from us by a few centimetres a year, bringing the day, perhaps hundreds of millions of years from now, when it will no longer hide the Sun completely.

  • Agriculture and calendarsSeasons set when to plant and harvest; calendars track the Sun’s tilt.
  • Solar energyPanels are tilted to catch the noon Sun best at their latitude.
  • Climate scienceChanges in Earth’s tilt and orbit shape the ice ages (the Milankovitch cycles).
  • NavigationThe noon height of the Sun, with the date, gives latitude.
  • AstronomyEclipses have been used to measure the Sun’s corona and to test relativity.
  • Other planetsMars (25° tilt) has seasons; Uranus, tilted 98°, has extreme ones; Mercury, almost untilted, has none.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The orbit is drawn as a circle seen in perspective, though it is slightly oval, and the Earth is closest to the Sun in January. The declination follows a simple sine curve and the noon-height formula ignores the bending of light by air, so real values differ by a fraction of a degree. The tilt slider goes beyond any real value, up to 45°, to show the effect. Only the northern hemisphere is shown; the southern has opposite seasons. The Moon-phase and eclipse diagrams are not to scale and exaggerate the tilt.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Tilt makes seasons. Not distance: the hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time.
  2. Phases are views of a lit half. Not the Earth’s shadow.
  3. Eclipses need alignment. The Moon’s tilted orbit keeps them rare.

Glossary and sources

Axial tilt (obliquity)
The angle between the Earth’s axis and the perpendicular to its orbit: about 23.4°.
Solstice / equinox
The days of longest and shortest daylight / the days when day and night are equal.
Node
A point where the Moon’s orbit crosses the plane of the Earth’s orbit.
Saros
The 18-year, 11-day cycle after which eclipses repeat in a similar pattern.
