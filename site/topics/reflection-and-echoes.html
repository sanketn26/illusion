OSC18 · Oscillations & waves

Reflection, transmission and echoes

In 1895 a young Harvard physicist was handed an odd job: fix a lecture hall in which every word seemed to dissolve into mush. To do it, he spent his nights carrying hundreds of seat cushions into the room and timing how long a single organ note took to die away.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

When a wave meets a boundary, some of it bounces back and some goes through. The split depends on the mismatch between the two sides: a large mismatch, like air to water, sends nearly everything back; a perfect match sends nothing. Reflected pulses can come back upside down, and a smooth slope can reflect almost nothing. Echoes, mirrors, sonar and ultrasound all use this rule.

0 · The story

The Fogg Art Museum at Harvard opened its new lecture hall in 1895, and the room was a disaster. A speaker’s words came back as a blur. The university’s president asked a junior physics professor, Wallace Sabine, who had never studied acoustics, to find out why.

Sabine, working with his assistants, borrowed hundreds of seat cushions from the nearby Sanders Theatre, whose acoustics were known to be good. Late at night, to escape traffic noise, they carried the cushions in and out, blew an organ pipe, and timed with a stopwatch how long the sound lingered. In the empty hall it rang on for more than five seconds. Each added cushion shortened it. Every bounce off the walls was robbing the sound of a small share of its energy, and the fewer bounces needed to use it all up, the clearer the speech.

From these nights came the first formula in architectural acoustics, and a few years later, the design of Boston’s Symphony Hall, opened in 1900. All of it came from the simple question of what happens to a wave when it hits something.

1 · The illusion

“A wall either bounces a wave or swallows it”

We talk as if a surface had two settings: a mirror that bounces light and a curtain that soaks up sound. But most surfaces do both, at once, partly. A window shows your reflection and lets you see through it. A shout hitting water sends almost everything back, yet something goes in, enough for a diver to hear the world above.

The split depends on how different the two sides are. The simulation lets you change one side of a rope and measure the split.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1886Lord Rayleigh notices that old, slightly tarnished glass lets through more light than new, clean glass. Reducing reflection, it seems, can be a surface problem.
  2. 1895–1900Wallace Sabine works out how the decay time of a room’s echoes depends on its volume and its absorbing surfaces. Boston Symphony Hall, opened in 1900, is the first hall designed using his formula.
  3. 1909Andrija Mohorovičić studies a Croatian earthquake and finds two sets of waves arriving, one direct and one deflected by a boundary deep underground: the boundary between Earth’s crust and mantle.
  4. 1912–1918Weeks after the Titanic sinks, Lewis Fry Richardson patents the idea of finding icebergs by echo; in 1914 Reginald Fessenden’s oscillator detects one about two miles away. Paul Langevin builds a quartz ultrasonic echo-sounder in 1917–18.
  5. 1935Alexander Smakula at Zeiss develops the first practical anti-reflection coating for lenses, a thin layer chosen so that reflections from its two surfaces cancel.
  6. 1958Ian Donald and Tom Brown, in Glasgow, publish the first ultrasound scans of the body, including of the womb.

3 · The model

Try this

  1. Start with 4× heavier. The pulse that comes back is upside down and a third the height, and the pulse that goes on is two-thirds the height.
  2. Slide to identical. There is no change in the rope, so there is no reflection at all: the whole pulse goes straight through.
  3. Go to 4× lighter, then free end. Now the reflected pulse comes back the right way up. With a free end it comes back as large as it went in.
  4. Try fixed wall: all of the pulse returns, upside down, and nothing gets through. Compare it with the chapter on wave speed, where the pulse hit the wall at the end.

4 · The reveal

It is all about the mismatch

Imagine the pulse reaching a place where the rope gets heavier. The heavier rope is harder to move, so it pushes back on the incoming pulse. That push-back is the reflection. The heavier the new rope compared to the old, the stronger the push-back. If the new rope is lighter, it is too easy to move, and the pulse comes back the other way up. If the new rope is identical, there is nothing to push back and no reflection.

For sound the number that matters is the density times the speed of sound. Air is about 413 in the usual units; water is about 1.5 million. That is a mismatch of about 3,600 to one: when sound in air reaches water, 99.9% of the energy is reflected. That is why a swimmer cannot hear a shout from the poolside, and why a doctor smears gel on your skin before an ultrasound scan: a tiny film of air would reflect nearly everything and the scanner would see nothing.

Making the mismatch smaller on purpose

A coating on camera glass has a refractive index in between air and glass and a thickness of a quarter of a wavelength, so the light reflected from its front and back cancel: 4% reflection per surface falls to about 1%. A trumpet’s bell and a loudspeaker horn change shape gradually, from the narrow mouthpiece to the open air, so the sound is not reflected back at a sudden edge. Echo-free rooms use spikes of absorbing foam for the same reason: a gradual change has nowhere to bounce from.

The surprise

Few boundaries only reflect or only absorb. Between two media, a wave usually splits, and the split depends on how different the two sides are (with real materials absorbing some too). A match makes a wall invisible to a wave.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now an ultrasound probe somewhere is sending pulses into a patient and timing the echoes from boundaries between tissues. A phone’s lens has a stack of thin coatings so light passes through instead of bouncing back at the camera. And a cable-TV or internet line is ending in a matched connector so the signal does not reflect back along the wire. Bats are using the same echo timing, but in their own heads.

  • Ultrasound scanningEchoes from tissue boundaries are turned into an image; gel removes the air mismatch.
  • Sonar and radarEcho delay gives distance; strength and shape of echo give size and type of target.
  • Seismology and oil explorationReflections of waves from rock layers reveal what is below, and showed Earth has an inner core (Inge Lehmann, 1936).
  • Lenses and solar cellsAnti-reflection coatings stop light being wasted by bouncing.
  • Cables and circuitsMatched “terminators” and connectors stop reflections of the signal that cause ghosting and errors.
  • Concert halls and studiosDesigners choose shapes and surfaces so the right amount of sound is reflected for the right time.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The simulation uses a rope in one dimension with a pulse small enough that the equations are exactly linear. Both ends are set to let waves pass out without bouncing, so the rope behaves as if it were very long on both sides. The join is sudden, which gives the largest reflection; a smooth change would give less. A real echo in air or on a cliff involves waves spreading in all directions and many surfaces, and part of the energy is lost as heat in each bounce, which is the subject of absorption and not shown here. The measured numbers are good to about two percent, because the computer solves the rope in small steps.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. A boundary between two media splits a wave. Some is reflected, some transmitted. In this lossless one-dimensional rope model, reflection is zero only if the two sides match and transmission is zero only for a perfect wall or free end. (Other waves have extra cases: light at Brewster’s angle can have no reflection, and total internal reflection can have no transmission.)
  2. The mismatch sets the split. A big difference, like air to water, reflects nearly all; a match reflects none.
  3. Echo delay is a ruler. Distance = speed × delay ÷ 2: sonar, radar and ultrasound all use it.

Glossary and sources

Reflection
The return of part of a wave from a boundary.
Transmission
The passing of part of a wave through a boundary.
Impedance (Z)
A measure of how strongly a medium resists being set into wave motion. For sound in a fluid, Z = density × speed of sound.
Echo
A reflected sound heard separately from the original, usually if delayed by more than about a tenth of a second.
Reverberation
The slow fading of sound in a room as it bounces many times.
