OSC08 · Oscillations & waves

What sets a wave’s speed

At a ballpark, you see the bat hit the ball, and then, a moment later, hear the crack. A louder crack doesn’t arrive any sooner. What decides how fast a wave goes is not how hard you made it, but what it travels through.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–9 min

The 30-second version

A wave’s speed is set by the medium, not by the source. On a rope it is √(tension ÷ weight per metre): pull the rope tighter and the pulse runs faster; use a heavier rope and it runs slower. Flick it harder and you make a bigger pulse, not a faster one. Sound in air goes at about 343 m/s, whatever the pitch or loudness.

0 · The story

Sit in the outfield seats of a baseball stadium, a hundred metres from home plate. You watch the batter swing, see the ball leap off the bat, and then, about a third of a second later, you hear the crack. Sound takes time to cross the stadium. A hundred metres at 343 metres a second is nearly 0.3 seconds.

Early scientists wondered whether a louder noise would outrun a quieter one, or a high note a low one. In 1635, the French philosopher Pierre Gassendi tested it by timing the delay between the flash and the bang of a gun, and of a cannon, from a distance. A loud cannon and a small musket were no different. Sound had a speed of its own.

The same is true of a pulse on a rope. If you flick one end of a long taut rope, a hump travels along it. Flick harder and you get a bigger hump. But does it get there faster?

1 · The illusion

“A harder push makes a faster wave”

If you throw a ball harder, it goes faster. It seems natural that a harder flick should send a pulse faster, and a louder shout should carry faster. Waves seem like things we throw.

But a wave is not a thing that is thrown. It is a disturbance passed from neighbour to neighbour along the medium, and the rate of that passing depends on the neighbours: how tightly they are tied and how heavy they are. The simulation lets you test it.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1635–36Gassendi and Marin Mersenne time the delay of gunshots over a distance and find that loud and quiet sounds travel at the same speed, though their numbers are rough.
  2. 1687Newton calculates the speed of sound from the properties of air and gets about 298 m/s, some 15% too low.
  3. 1713Brook Taylor works out the speed of a wave on a stretched string from Newton’s laws.
  4. 1816Pierre-Simon Laplace spots the missing ingredient: sound compresses air so fast that it heats it slightly, stiffening it. The correction brings Newton’s answer to about 350 m/s.

3 · The model

A rope is stretched between two walls. Flick gently or flick hard to send a hump along it. You set the rope’s tension and its heaviness (mass per metre). The simulation solves the rope’s motion in small steps, then times the hump between two marks to measure its speed, and compares it with the formula.

wave speed on a rope v = √( T / μ ) in air: v ≈ 331 + 0.6 × (temperature in °C) m/sT = tension, μ = mass per metre. More tension: faster. More weight: slower. Neither the height of the pulse nor how hard you flicked appears in the formula.

Try this

  1. Press Flick gently and note the measured speed. Then press Flick hard. The pulse is three times taller, but the measured speed is exactly the same.
  2. Double the tension from 40 N to 80 N. The speed rises by a factor of √2, about 40%, not 100%.
  3. Make the rope heavier. The pulse slows down. Heavier neighbours are harder to move, so the disturbance is passed more slowly.
  4. Watch what happens when the pulse hits the wall: it comes back upside down, still at the same speed.

4 · The reveal

The medium sets the rules

Think of a line of people passing a message by tapping the next person’s shoulder. The speed of the message depends on how quickly each person reacts and taps, not on how hard you tapped the first one. In a rope, every little piece is pulled by its neighbours with a force set by the tension, and it has to be accelerated by that force: the heavier the piece, the slower it responds. The ratio of the two, tension to weight, sets the speed.

Sound works the same way, with stiffness in place of tension and density in place of weight. Air is light but easily compressed, so sound travels at 343 m/s. Water is far stiffer, and sound goes 4 times faster, at 1,480 m/s. Steel is stiffer still, at about 5,000 m/s.

Pitch, loudness and wavelength

Once the medium sets the speed, the rest follows. The pitch (frequency) is chosen by the source, and the wavelength comes out as speed ÷ frequency. A louder sound is a bigger wave, but the speed is unchanged. That is why a band playing in a stadium sounds in tune from the far seats: all the notes, high and low, loud and quiet, arrive at the same speed. If higher notes travelled faster than lower ones, music from a distance would sound smeared.

The surprise

For ordinary small waves in a given medium, the speed is a property of the medium, like the speed limit on a road. The source decides what kind of wave it makes, but not how fast it runs. (Two exceptions come later: in some media the speed depends on the wavelength, see dispersion, and very large waves can travel at speeds that depend on their height.)

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now a thunderstorm somewhere is giving the usual count: each three seconds between flash and thunder means about a kilometre of distance. Seismometers around the world are timing the gap between two kinds of earthquake waves, fast P waves at about 6 to 8 km/s and slower S waves at about 3.5 to 4.5 km/s, and using the gap to find how far away a quake was. And in a hospital, an ultrasound scanner is timing echoes using the speed of sound in tissue, about 1,540 m/s, to turn delays into depths.

  • Musical instrumentsTightening a guitar string raises its wave speed, and with it the pitch.
  • Ultrasound and sonarEcho delays become distances, using the known speed of sound.
  • SeismologyWave speeds at different depths reveal the structure of the Earth.
  • Weather and lightningThe thunder count uses the speed of sound in air.
  • Telephony and engineeringSignals in cables travel at a speed set by the cable’s properties.
  • ArchitectureEchoes in a hall depend on the speed of sound and the distances.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The rope is ideal: perfectly flexible, with no stiffness and no loss of energy, and the pulse is small enough that the motion is exactly linear. The simulation solves the wave equation approximately in small steps, so the measured speed is within about half a percent of the formula. The two ends are held fixed. Real ropes are heavy, stiff and damp the pulse as it travels. The animation runs at 15% of real speed, which is why the pulse is easy to follow.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. The medium sets the speed. On a rope, v = √(T/μ); in air, about 343 m/s.
  2. Harder flick, bigger pulse. Not a faster one.
  3. Pitch and loudness do not matter. High and low, loud and quiet notes all travel at the same speed.
