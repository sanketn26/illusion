MEC07 · Motion & forces

Conservation of momentum

In 1920 the New York Times mocked a scientist for claiming that a rocket could work in empty space, because there would be “nothing to push against.” The paper printed a correction in 1969, the day after Apollo 11 left for the Moon.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

Momentum, mass times velocity (with direction), is never created or destroyed; it only moves from one object to another. Two skaters pushing apart gain equal and opposite momentum, so the lighter one moves faster. A rocket works the same way: it throws gas backwards and gains the same momentum forwards, with nothing to push against.

0 · The story

On 13 January 1920 the New York Times ran an editorial about a report from a Massachusetts physics professor named Robert Goddard. Goddard had suggested that a rocket could, in principle, reach the Moon. The editors were scornful. A rocket, they explained, needs something to push against. Once it leaves the air, “its flight would be neither accelerated nor maintained by the explosion of the charges.” To think otherwise, they said, was to deny a fundamental law of physics. Professor Goddard, they implied, had forgotten the basics taught in high school.

Goddard had not forgotten. The editors had. A rocket doesn’t push against the air. It pushes against its own exhaust.

On 17 July 1969, the day after Apollo 11 launched, the paper ran a short correction. It said that investigation and experiment had confirmed the findings of Isaac Newton in the 17th century and that a rocket could function in a vacuum. “The Times regrets the error.”

1 · The illusion

“To move, you must push against something else”

Walking, you push against the ground. Swimming, against the water. A boat pushes against the water with an oar. It seems obvious that pushing needs something to push against, and empty space has nothing.

What this misses is that you can push against part of yourself. Throw something away, hard, in one direction, and you recoil in the other. That is all a rocket does, and the rule that governs it is one of the great conservation laws.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1644René Descartes proposes that a “quantity of motion” (mass times speed) is conserved in the universe. He is right about the quantity but misses that direction matters.
  2. 1668–69The Royal Society invites John Wallis, Christopher Wren and Christiaan Huygens to study colliding bodies. They find that what is conserved is mass times velocity with direction: momentum.
  3. 1687Newton’s third law (equal and opposite forces) is the same fact seen from the side of force. Pushes come in pairs, so momentum gained by one is lost by the other.
  4. 1918Emmy Noether proves that conservation of momentum follows from a symmetry of nature: the laws of physics are the same everywhere in space.
  5. 1926Goddard launches the first liquid-fuelled rocket, in a cabbage field in Auburn, Massachusetts.

3 · The model

Two skaters stand together on ice, with no friction. A has a mass of 60 kg. You choose the mass of B. When you press Push apart, they shove each other away with a fixed amount of push (an “impulse” of 240 newton-seconds). The bars show each skater’s momentum.

momentum p = m · v before = after: m_A·v_A + m_B·v_B = 0 (if they start at rest)The two skaters get equal and opposite momentum, so the total remains zero. The lighter skater must have the greater speed. The centre of mass does not move at all.

Try this

  1. Leave B at 60 kg and press Push apart. Both move at the same speed, in opposite directions.
  2. Press Reset, then set B to 20 kg and push. B goes three times as fast as A, but the momentum bars have the same length.
  3. Try 120 kg. Now A is the fast one. The heavier skater barely moves.
  4. Watch the white diamond (the centre of mass). It does not move, whatever the masses. Internal pushes cannot move it.

4 · The reveal

Pushes come in pairs

When skater A pushes on B, B pushes on A with an equal and opposite force for exactly the same time, so each receives equal and opposite momentum. Before the push, the total momentum was zero. After the push, one skater has +240 and the other −240, so the total is still zero. That is conservation: momentum can be passed around but not made or lost.

Because momentum is mass times velocity, a light object that carries the same momentum as a heavy one must be going faster, in just the same proportion. A skater who is half as heavy moves twice as fast. That is why a rifle recoils far more slowly than the bullet flies, and why the Earth is not knocked into space every time you jump.

Rockets

A rocket is the skaters again, with exhaust gas as B. The engine throws a huge amount of hot gas backwards at high speed. The gas carries away momentum backward; the rocket has to gain exactly as much forward. No air is needed, and in fact a rocket works better outside the atmosphere because the gas doesn’t have to push any air out of the way.

The surprise

You don’t need something to push against. You only need something to push away. And when you push, the thing you throw pushes back, always, by exactly the same amount.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now spacecraft all over the solar system are changing course by throwing a little gas the other way. NASA’s Dawn probe used ion engines that fire xenon ions out at tens of kilometres per second, and gained speed gram by gram. And every time you take a step, the Earth recoils backwards by an amount far too small to measure. Jumping at 3 m/s, a 70 kg person gives the Earth about 3.5 × 10⁻²³ m/s the other way.

  • Rockets and spacecraftEvery manoeuvre in space is momentum conservation applied to exhaust.
  • Jet engines and propellersThey throw air backwards, and get thrust forwards.
  • Guns and recoilThe bullet’s momentum forward equals the gun’s backward, so the gun moves slowly because it is heavy.
  • Squids and jellyfishThey jet water out to move through it.
  • Crash investigationMomentum before and after a collision is used to work out vehicle speeds.
  • Particle physicsMissing momentum in a collision is how physicists detect invisible particles like neutrinos.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The ice here is frictionless and the push is instantaneous. Real skaters also spin, and momentum alone doesn’t say anything about spin (angular momentum is a separate law). The skaters are discs moving along one line, and when they reach the edge of the rink the picture just stops them, which would in reality involve a wall and another push. Rocket exhaust, in reality, is a stream of many particles pushed out gradually, not a single shove.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Momentum is conserved. For a system with no net outside push, it can move between objects but is neither created nor destroyed.
  2. Pushes come in equal and opposite pairs. So gained momentum equals lost momentum.
  3. Light moves faster. For the same momentum, less mass means more speed.

Glossary and sources

Momentum
Mass times velocity, a vector.
Impulse
Force multiplied by the time it acts; equal to the change in momentum.
Centre of mass
The average position of a system’s mass, which accelerates only if a net outside force acts.
Isolated system
A system with no outside forces acting on it.
