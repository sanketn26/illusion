FND03 · Foundations & method

Orders of magnitude

A million seconds is about eleven and a half days. A billion seconds is about thirty-two years. A trillion is about thirty-two thousand. We say “million, billion, trillion” as if they were neighbours. They are in different worlds.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–9 min

The 30-second version

To compare things that differ by a factor of a thousand or a billion, count in powers of ten. Each step up is ten times bigger. Across the whole of known physics, from a proton to the observable universe, there are only about 42 steps. Thinking this way lets you estimate almost anything and spot nonsense at a glance.

0 · The story

Suppose someone offered to give you a million seconds of free time, or a billion. Which would you rather have? Most people feel that a billion is “more” but not how much more. Do the arithmetic. A million seconds is a fortnight’s holiday. A billion seconds is a whole working life, thirty-two years.

Now a trillion. A trillion seconds ago, which is about thirty-two thousand years, humans were painting on cave walls in the Ice Age, and farming had not yet begun.

Politicians and newspapers say “million,” “billion” and “trillion” in the same breath. The words sound alike, but they hide factors of a thousand each. The human mind simply was not built to feel the difference. Physics, which routinely deals with sizes from the proton to the cosmos, needed a better way.

1 · The illusion

“Big numbers are just bigger”

We handle ordinary quantities, the number of people in a room, the length of a table, with an instinctive sense of scale. But that sense runs out quickly. Ask people to place a million and a billion on a line from zero to a trillion and many put the billion near the middle.

The fix is to measure not by how much, but by how many times ten.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 216 BCEIn The Sand Reckoner, Archimedes invents a system for naming enormous numbers, to show that even the number of grains of sand needed to fill the universe could be counted.
  2. 1945Enrico Fermi, watching the Trinity test, drops scraps of paper and estimates the blast’s strength from how far the air pushes them. He is within a factor of two. “Fermi estimates” become a way of thinking.
  3. 1957Dutch educator Kees Boeke publishes Cosmic View, a picture book zooming out by powers of ten.
  4. 1968–77Charles and Ray Eames make the film Powers of Ten: it zooms out by a factor of ten every ten seconds to 10²⁵ metres, then plunges in to 10⁻¹⁶ metres, covering 41 powers of ten in about nine minutes.

4 · The reveal

Counting in tens

An order of magnitude is a factor of ten. If one thing is two orders of magnitude bigger than another, it is a hundred times bigger. Writing a number as a power of ten, like 10⁶ for a million, 10⁹ for a billion, turns multiplication into addition: the exponents simply add. Suddenly a million (6) and a billion (9) are three steps apart, and a trillion (12) is three steps beyond that.

Seen this way, the whole known universe fits on one short ladder. The proton is about 10⁻¹⁵ metres across. You are about 10⁰. The Earth is about 10⁷. The observable universe is about 10²⁷. That is only 42 steps from the size of a proton to the width of the observable universe, and each step is an idea you can hold in your head.

Estimating like Fermi

The great practical use of this is the quick estimate: to find an answer to within a factor of ten, break a question into pieces you can guess to the nearest power of ten, and multiply. Physicists use it to check whether a result is even plausible before doing the detail. How many piano tuners in Chicago? How much oil does a country use in a day? Any answer within a factor of a few is already far better than a guess.

The surprise

Our everyday sense of number is linear, but the world is multiplicative. Learning to count in tens turns the impossible-to-imagine into a short list of steps, and lets you spot, at a glance, whether a claim is off by a factor of a thousand.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now your body contains roughly 30 to 40 trillion cells, and every cell that has a nucleus holds about two metres of DNA (red blood cells have none), so that all the DNA laid end to end would reach to the Sun and back dozens of times. The processor in your phone is switching billions of times a second. And at this moment, light that left the Sun eight minutes ago is falling on the Earth, light that left the nearest star over four years ago is only now arriving, and light from the farthest galaxies has been on its way for over thirteen billion years.

  • Scientific notationEvery science uses powers of ten to handle sizes from atoms to galaxies.
  • Estimation and sanity checksEngineers and scientists check answers to within a factor of ten before trusting them.
  • ComputingMemory and speed are measured in powers of ten: kilo-, mega-, giga-, tera-.
  • Finance and policyUnderstanding “million” versus “billion” is basic financial literacy.
  • Medicine and biologyCells, bacteria and viruses span about four orders of magnitude, which dictates which instrument sees them.
  • CosmologyThe universe’s scales are only meaningful as ratios of tens.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The sizes are rounded: “the Earth” is its diameter, “a proton” is an approximate charge radius doubled, and “the observable universe” is the diameter of the region we can see, a convention rather than an edge. Each bar is drawn on an ordinary linear scale against the view; objects are shown as bars, not shapes. Very small things are drawn as the thinnest line, which isn’t their real size. The zoom runs continuously, but there is nothing special about powers of ten except that we count in tens.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Count in tens. One order of magnitude is a factor of ten.
  2. A million, a billion and a trillion are far apart. Each is a thousand times the last: 12 days, 32 years, 32,000 years of seconds.
  3. 42 steps cover everything. From a proton to the observable universe.

Glossary and sources

Order of magnitude
A factor of ten. “Two orders of magnitude bigger” means a hundred times.
Scientific notation
Writing a number as a decimal times a power of ten, like 3 × 10⁸.
Logarithmic scale
A scale where each equal step multiplies the value by a fixed factor, such as ten.
Fermi estimate
A quick estimate that breaks a problem into parts guessed to the nearest power of ten.
