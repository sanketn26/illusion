GRV02 · Gravity & orbits

The inverse-square law

Stand twice as far from a lamp and it looks a quarter as bright, not half. Gravity, electricity, sound and radio all obey the same rule, and the reason is a single fact about the shape of space.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

A source that spreads out in all directions has to spread its effect over a sphere, and a sphere’s area grows as the square of its radius. Twice as far means four times the area, so a quarter as strong at each spot. Gravity and electricity follow the same rule for the same reason, and it works because space has exactly three dimensions.

0 · The story

In January 1684, in a London coffee house, three of the cleverest men in England were having an argument. Robert Hooke, Christopher Wren and the young astronomer Edmond Halley all suspected that the planets are held in orbit by a pull from the Sun that gets weaker with distance in a particular way: as the square of the distance. But could they prove that such a pull would make planets move in the ellipses that Kepler had found?

Hooke said he had a proof but wouldn’t show it. Wren, who doubted him, offered a prize, a book worth forty shillings, to anyone who could produce one within two months. Nobody did.

That August, Halley travelled to Cambridge and put the question to a reclusive professor named Isaac Newton. “What curve would a planet follow under such a force?” “An ellipse,” said Newton at once. Halley asked how he knew. “Why,” replied Newton, “I have calculated it.” He couldn’t find the paper. But Halley pressed him, and three years later, the result became the Principia.

1 · The illusion

“Twice as far, half as strong”

If something weakens with distance, the simplest guess is that it weakens in proportion: twice as far, half as strong. That is how a rope tension or a leaky pipe might behave. But light, gravity and sound do something sharper: double the distance and the effect drops to a quarter.

The reason is not about gravity or light in particular. It is geometry, and you can watch it happen in the simulation.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1604Johannes Kepler, studying optics, argues that light must weaken with the square of the distance because the same light spreads over the surface of ever-larger spheres.
  2. 1645Ismaël Boulliau suggests that a force from the Sun might weaken as the inverse square of distance, but then rejects the idea.
  3. 1679–84Hooke, Wren and Halley each suspect that an inverse-square pull explains the planets, but none can prove it.
  4. 1684–87Newton proves that an inverse-square pull gives elliptical orbits, and publishes the full theory in the Principia.
  5. 1785Charles-Augustin de Coulomb shows that electric charges attract and repel by the same inverse-square rule.
  6. 1917Paul Ehrenfest argues that, with forces spreading as in three dimensions, stable orbits and atoms are not possible in more than three.

3 · The model

A lamp sends out a fixed number of flashes in all directions. A screen sits at the distance you choose. The picture on the right shows the screen face-on: each dot is one flash, and the amber square is a fixed one-unit patch. Count how many land inside it.

strength at distance r ∝ 1 / r² area of a sphere of radius r = 4π r²The same amount of light, force or sound must cover a sphere. Twice the radius, four times the area, so one quarter of the strength per unit area.

Try this

  1. At distance 1 the screen is one square, and every flash lands in the amber one: 100%.
  2. Move to 2. The same light now spreads over four squares, so about 25% of it lands in the amber one.
  3. Try 3 and 5. The measured percentage keeps following 1/9 (11%) and 1/25 (4%). The counts are random, but the average is steady.
  4. Wait a few seconds. The more flashes counted, the closer the measured value gets to the expected one.

4 · The reveal

It is geometry, not a law of light

Picture the light leaving the lamp as a swarm of tiny particles, each flying straight out. A moment later the swarm lies on the surface of a sphere. A moment after that, the same swarm lies on a bigger sphere. No particles have been lost, but the area they must cover has grown as the square of the radius. So the number per square metre has dropped by that same factor.

Anything that spreads out evenly from a point in three-dimensional space must obey this: light, sound in open air, radio, the field of an electric charge and the pull of a mass. They don’t share a physical cause. They share a geometry. In a flat two-dimensional world, the rule would be 1/r. In a four-dimensional one, 1/r³.

Why three dimensions matter

The 1/r² rule is special in another way. Of all the force laws one could invent, only the inverse square (and a spring-like force that grows in proportion to distance) make every bound orbit close up and repeat exactly, a result proved by Joseph Bertrand in 1873. With other force laws, bound orbits can still exist, but they generally precess: each loop comes back rotated a little, tracing a rosette. (Mercury’s orbit precesses very slowly, mostly from the pull of the other planets and a little from Einstein’s gravity.) Ehrenfest argued in 1917 that, for forces that spread out the way they do in three dimensions, stable orbits and stable atoms could not exist in more than three. The argument depends on the assumed force law and model, and other dimensions are less clear-cut: in two dimensions, a force falling as 1/r also has stable circular orbits.

The surprise

A fact about the shape of space, that a sphere’s area grows as r², controls how light, gravity and electricity fade. Ehrenfest argued that with forces that spread out as they do in three dimensions, more dimensions would mean no stable orbits.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the sunlight reaching a Mars rover is only about 43% as strong as on Earth, so its panels must be larger than their Earth equivalents. At Jupiter it is about 4%, which is why NASA’s Juno spacecraft, the first solar-powered probe to work out there, carries three enormous solar arrays, each about nine metres long. And at this moment your Wi-Fi router’s signal is a tiny fraction of its strength a few metres away, falling as the same law.

  • Space missionsSpacecraft power, communication links and thermal design all start from the inverse-square law.
  • Radiation safetyDoubling your distance from a small source cuts the dose rate to a quarter.
  • Photography and lightingFlash and lamp brightness fall off quickly, which is why a flash can’t light up a stadium.
  • AstronomyAstronomers measure the distance to a star of known brightness from how faint it looks.
  • Sound and radioIn open air, each doubling of distance drops sound by about 6 decibels.
  • Gravity and electricityThe same law governs the pull of the Sun and the force between two charges.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The lamp is a point that sends light equally in every direction, in empty space. Real air, fog and dust absorb and scatter light, so real brightness falls even faster. Real screens are not always face-on (a tilted screen gets less light by another factor). The photons are modelled as random dots spread evenly over the screen, and the cone of light is a neat square pyramid. For gravity there are no particles at all: the law matches the geometry of spreading, but the reason gravity behaves this way is deeper (general relativity).

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Spreading over a sphere. Area grows as r², so strength falls as 1/r².
  2. Twice as far, a quarter as strong. Three times as far, a ninth.
  3. It depends on three dimensions. In a different number of dimensions the exponent would change.

Glossary and sources

Inverse-square law
A quantity that falls as 1 divided by the square of the distance.
Intensity
Power per unit area, such as watts per square metre of sunlight.
Solar constant
The sunlight intensity at Earth’s distance, about 1,361 W/m².
Bertrand’s theorem
Only inverse-square and spring-like forces give closed orbits.
