THM13 · Heat, entropy & randomness

Entropy in a two-chamber box

Nothing in the laws of motion stops all the air in your room from rushing into one corner. It just never happens. A box, a divider and some counting explain why, and what “disorder” really means.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

There are vastly more ways for particles to be spread out than to be bunched up. Nothing forces them to spread. They just wander, and the spread-out arrangements are so overwhelmingly numerous that the wandering almost always ends up there. Entropy is simply a count of those arrangements.

0 · The story

In 1907 two physicists, Paul and Tatyana Ehrenfest, imagined a pair of dogs sharing a population of fleas. At each tick, one flea, chosen at random, hops to the other dog. Start with every flea on Dog A. What will you see over time?

Soon the fleas are shared roughly equally. Nobody planned it. No flea was told to even things out. Each flea simply hopped when picked. And yet the evenness is not a coincidence: it is the most likely outcome of any sequence of random hops. Wait a little longer, and you will see small random swings around an even split, but you will never see all the fleas return to Dog A, unless there are very few of them.

Replace fleas with gas molecules and dogs with the two halves of a box, and you have the central puzzle of thermodynamics.

1 · The illusion

“Gas spreads out because something pushes it to”

When you open a bottle of perfume, it feels as if the scent is pushed outward, as if spreading out were something the molecules were trying to do. They aren’t. Each molecule is flying about aimlessly, bumping into things. Nothing is seeking an even distribution.

So why doesn’t the scent ever fly back into the bottle? It could. Every individual motion involved would be perfectly legal. The reason is the subject of this chapter, and the simulation lets you watch it appear.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1850–54Rudolf Clausius states the second law of thermodynamics: heat does not flow by itself from cold to hot. In 1865 he coins the word entropy for the quantity that always increases.
  2. 1867James Clerk Maxwell imagines a tiny being that could sort fast and slow molecules and so beat the second law, a puzzle that takes decades to resolve (see Maxwell’s demon, THM19).
  3. 1872–77Ludwig Boltzmann explains entropy by counting: a state with more arrangements is more likely, and entropy measures the number of arrangements. His formula, S = k log W, is engraved on his tombstone in Vienna.
  4. 1876Josef Loschmidt objects that if the laws of motion run equally well backwards, any spreading-out should have a matching gathering-in. This objection leads directly to the next chapter, the arrow of time.
  5. 1907Paul and Tatyana Ehrenfest propose the dogs-and-fleas model, a toy that makes the statistics visible and exact.

3 · The model

Particles bounce around a box that is split by a divider. At the start, all of them are on the left. The line chart records the share still on the left. They never collide with one another. Each one simply flies in a straight line and bounces off the walls, so nothing in the rules prefers either side.

number of arrangements for N particles = 2NEach particle is independently on the left or right. “All on the left” is exactly one of these. “Half and half” is the largest group by far.

Try this

  1. Leave the divider in and watch the left side. Nothing changes: the particles are trapped.
  2. Press Remove divider. Watch the count and the line: it falls fast and then hovers near 50%.
  3. Now drag Particles down to about 6 and reset. Remove the divider. The line wobbles all over the place and sometimes the particles all go back to one side.
  4. Move it back to 300. The wobble nearly vanishes. The line sits on 50% as if it were pinned there.

4 · The reveal

Disorder is a count, not a mess

The key move is to separate two things. A microstate is the exact position of every particle. A macrostate is the coarse thing we can notice: how many are on the left. Each macrostate can be made by many microstates, and the numbers are wildly lopsided. All particles on the left: one way. Half-and-half: a huge number of ways.

The particles are not heading towards half-and-half. They are wandering through microstates with no preference. But almost all of the microstates look like half-and-half, so that is where a wandering system finds itself, almost always. That is all the second law is: not a force, but a statement about numbers.

The second lesson is in the slider. With six particles you can see the gas go back to one side, because the odds are only 1 in 32. With a hundred, those odds are about 1 in 1030. Even if the arrangement were reshuffled a trillion times each second, you would wait roughly three times the age of the universe to see it once. And a room of air has about 1026 molecules.

The surprise

The second law of thermodynamics is not like the law of gravity. It is a statement about large numbers. For a handful of particles, it is only a tendency. For a roomful, it is as close to certainty as physics ever gets.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the air in the room around you is making tiny random swings in how many molecules are in each half. They are millions of times too small to detect, because there are so many molecules. If your room had a few hundred molecules, you could feel the pressure flicker.

  • Engines and power plantsEntropy sets the best possible efficiency of any heat engine, which determines how much of a fuel’s energy can become useful work.
  • Refrigerators and air-conditioningThey pump heat “uphill” by paying for it with an increase in entropy elsewhere.
  • ChemistryWhether a reaction runs on its own depends on the balance between energy and entropy.
  • Information and AIShannon’s entropy uses the same counting idea to measure information. Training language models minimises a closely related quantity, cross-entropy.
  • MaterialsMixing, dissolving, melting and evaporation are all entropy-driven.
  • CosmologyWhy is the past different from the future? Part of the answer is that the early universe started in an unusually low-entropy state.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The particles don’t collide with each other, so there is no way for them to exchange energy and speed. Real gas molecules constantly collide, and that exchange is what spreads energy as well as position. The simulation counts only position (left or right), while real entropy also counts speeds. The box is 2D, and the number of particles is tiny compared with real gas. The model illustrates the counting argument, not the full thermodynamics.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. More ways wins. Spread-out arrangements outnumber bunched-up ones, so wandering particles end up spread out.
  2. It is a law of large numbers. With few particles you see fluctuations; with many, the odds of a big fluctuation vanish.
  3. No force required. Particles do not “want” to spread. Spreading is just overwhelmingly likely.

Glossary and sources

Microstate
The exact arrangement of every particle.
Macrostate
A coarse description, such as “60 on the left, 40 on the right”.
Entropy
A measure of how many microstates match a macrostate (S = k log W).
Second law
In an isolated system, entropy almost never decreases.
  • R. Clausius, “On the moving force of heat…” (1850) and later papers introducing entropy (1865).
  • L. Boltzmann, “Further studies on the thermal equilibrium of gas molecules” (1872) and “On the relation between the second law and probability theory” (1877).
  • P. and T. Ehrenfest, “Über zwei bekannte Einwände gegen das Boltzmannsche H-Theorem,” Physikalische Zeitschrift (1907).
