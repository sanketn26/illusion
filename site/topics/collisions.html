MEC08 · Motion & forces

Elastic and inelastic collisions

In a billiards game the cue ball can hit another ball head-on and stop dead, as if the motion had been handed over. Two lumps of clay thrown together just stick and sag. Something is conserved in both, and something is not.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

In a collision between objects with no outside push during the impact, momentum is conserved. Movement energy is conserved only if the objects bounce perfectly (an elastic collision). Otherwise some of it turns into heat, sound and denting. The more the objects stick, the more is lost, up to a maximum when they move off together.

0 · The story

Watch a good pool player. The cue ball rolls forward, strikes the red ball square on, and stops dead. The red ball takes off with the cue ball’s motion, as if a baton had been passed. Pool players call it a “stop shot.”

Now picture throwing a lump of wet clay at another lump of clay on a table. They meet with a dull smack, stick together, and slide forward slowly as one. No baton-passing here. The pair crawls away at half the speed of the thrown lump.

Same kind of event, two very different outcomes. A rule must govern both. Finding the rule, and finding what is conserved and what is not, took physicists most of the seventeenth century.

1 · The illusion

“If energy is conserved, collisions should always bounce”

We are told energy can never be destroyed, so it seems that two objects should always leave a collision with the same movement energy they came with. Momentum is conserved, we know. But clay doesn’t bounce, and its movement energy clearly dropped.

The answer is that energy was not destroyed. It changed form: the clay got slightly hotter, a little sound was made, and the lumps squashed. Only the movement energy fell. The simulation shows both books being balanced side by side.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1668The Royal Society in London asks John Wallis, Christopher Wren and Christiaan Huygens to explain how colliding bodies behave. All three reach the right rule for momentum.
  2. 1669Huygens publishes his rules for perfectly bouncy collisions, including that the total of mass × speed² is also unchanged.
  3. 1673–76Edme Mariotte demonstrates collisions with a row of hanging balls, the ancestor of the desk toy “Newton’s cradle”.
  4. 1687Newton measures collisions between pendulum balls of different materials and finds that the speed of separation is a fixed fraction of the speed of approach for each material, a number we now call the coefficient of restitution.
  5. 1843–50James Joule shows that the “lost” movement energy reappears as heat, in a precise ratio.
  6. 1967The commercial Newton’s cradle appears; its chrome balls turn a seventeenth-century experiment into an office ornament.

3 · The model

Cart A (1 unit of mass) moves at 4 m/s towards cart B, which is at rest. You choose how bouncy they are and how heavy B is. The two bar charts show momentum and movement energy before and after.

after: v_A = (1 − e·M) · 4 / (1 + M) v_B = (1 + e) · 4 / (1 + M) (M = B’s mass ÷ A’s mass, e = bounciness)e = 1: perfectly elastic. e = 0: they stick together. Momentum, mass × velocity added up, is the same before and after for any e.

Try this

  1. Start with perfectly bouncy and equal masses. A stops dead, B moves off at 4 m/s. The energy bars are equal.
  2. Slide Bounciness down to 0 (“sticks together”). The carts move off together at 2 m/s, the momentum bars are still equal, and exactly half the energy is gone.
  3. Make B 4 × heavier with perfect bounce. Now A bounces backwards, and B creeps forward. The energy is conserved but shared differently.
  4. Make B heavy and sticky: now 80% of the energy is lost. The heavier the target, the more energy is wasted when they stick.

4 · The reveal

Two books, one balanced

Momentum is conserved whatever happens, because the two carts push on each other with equal and opposite forces for the same time. That never fails. Energy of motion is another matter. When the carts bounce, springs of material compress and rebound, giving the energy back. When they stick or dent, that stored energy is not given back: it ends up as the random jiggling of atoms, as heat.

The lost energy is exactly accounted for. For any head-on collision, the amount lost is ½ × (reduced mass) × (approach speed)² × (1 − e²). It is zero for e = 1 and largest for e = 0, which is why a sticky collision wastes the most.

The special cases worth remembering

Equal masses, perfectly bouncy: the two simply swap velocities (the pool stop shot, and Newton’s cradle). Heavy hits light: the light one flies off at nearly double the heavy one’s speed, like a golf ball struck by a club. Light hits heavy: the light one bounces back at nearly its original speed, like a ball off a wall.

The surprise

Energy is never lost, but useful energy can be. Elastic collisions keep it in a form that can do work again; inelastic ones scatter it into heat. That is why you can bounce a ball for a long time, and why you can’t un-squash clay.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now golf’s rule-makers limit the “bounciness” of driver faces to a coefficient of restitution of 0.83, because a springier face would send the ball farther than the sport intends. In car crash labs, designers are building crumple zones that are deliberately inelastic so that a crash’s energy goes into bending metal and not into bouncing the passengers back. And inside nuclear reactors, water and graphite “moderators” slow down neutrons by letting them bounce off light nuclei, a hit on a hydrogen nucleus (nearly equal mass) takes about half the neutron’s energy on average, while a hit on a much heavier carbon nucleus takes at most about 28% and about 14% on average, so graphite needs more collisions.

  • Sports equipmentBats, rackets, clubs and balls are regulated and designed by their coefficient of restitution.
  • Vehicle safetyCrumple zones turn movement energy into deformation instead of rebound.
  • Nuclear reactorsModerators slow neutrons by elastic collisions with light nuclei.
  • Ballistic pendulumA bullet embedded in a hanging block (a perfectly sticky collision) lets you measure its speed from how high the block swings.
  • Particle physicsMany collisions at accelerators are very inelastic: movement energy becomes new particles.
  • Games and animationPhysics engines use the coefficient of restitution to decide how bouncy things feel.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Everything happens along a single line, with no friction. The carts are idealised: no spin, no sound, no sticking friction, and the “bounciness” is a single constant. Real objects have a bounciness that depends on speed, temperature and how they hit (a ball dropped from 10 cm rebounds more than one dropped from a house). The carts collide instantly. When they reach the edge of the track they simply stop, with no walls to bounce off.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Momentum is conserved. For the colliding objects together, if nothing outside pushes them during the impact, whether bouncy or sticky.
  2. Movement energy is conserved only if it bounces perfectly. Otherwise it becomes heat, sound and dents.
  3. Sticky loses most. Objects that move off together have lost as much movement energy as possible.

Glossary and sources

Elastic collision
One in which total movement energy is the same before and after.
Inelastic collision
One in which some movement energy becomes heat, sound or deformation.
Coefficient of restitution (e)
Speed of separation divided by speed of approach: 1 for perfectly bouncy, 0 for stuck together.
Reduced mass
μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂), the effective mass that sets the energy lost in a head-on collision.
