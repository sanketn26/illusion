OSC19 · Oscillations & waves

Diffraction: waves bend round corners

In 1818 the French Academy ran a contest on light. One judge, Siméon Poisson, worked out a prediction from a rival’s theory and thought it absurd: a bright spot at the very centre of a circular shadow. A colleague tried it. The spot was there.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A wave that passes through an opening or round an edge spreads out. How much depends on the opening compared with the wavelength: when the opening is about the wavelength or smaller, the wave spreads widely; when the opening is much bigger, the wave goes on almost straight. A voice has a wavelength of about a metre, so it bends round a doorway. Light’s wavelength is half a millionth of a metre, so it hardly bends at all, but it does, and that limits every lens ever made.

0 · The story

You are in a courtyard. Behind a stone wall your friend calls your name, and you hear every word. You walk up and down looking for them. You cannot see anyone at all, and will not until you get round the corner. Sound reaches round the corner; light does not.

It seems the end of the matter: sound bends, light travels in straight lines. In the 1660s the Jesuit Francesco Maria Grimaldi saw otherwise. Letting a thin beam of sunlight into a dark room, he saw that the shadow of a rod was wider than it should be, with faint coloured bands along its edge. Light bends too, a tiny amount, and he called this diffractio, a “breaking up”. In 1818 the question became a famous contest. Augustin Fresnel entered a wave theory of light that predicted every band. Poisson, a believer in the particle view, spotted a consequence that seemed ridiculous: if light is a wave, a small round disc should have a bright spot at the centre of its shadow. François Arago made the experiment, and there it was. Fresnel won the prize.

1 · The illusion

“Sound bends round corners; light goes straight”

Everyday experience seems to split waves into two kinds: sound and water waves that wrap round obstacles, and light that casts sharp shadows. It looks like they follow different rules.

They do not. Every wave spreads as it goes through a gap. What differs is the size of the gap measured in wavelengths. The simulation lets you change both and see.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1665Grimaldi’s book, published after his death, describes bands of light around shadows and names the effect diffraction.
  2. 1690Christiaan Huygens publishes his Treatise on Light (written 1678), proposing that every point on a wavefront is a source of new waves.
  3. 1801–1803Thomas Young shows with two slits that light can interfere with itself, firm evidence for a wave theory.
  4. 1818–1819Fresnel’s wave theory of diffraction wins the Academy’s prize after Arago finds the bright spot at the centre of a shadow predicted by Poisson.
  5. 1873–1879Ernst Abbe (microscopes, 1873) and Lord Rayleigh (telescopes, 1879) show that diffraction sets a smallest detail that any lens can show.
  6. 1912–1952Max von Laue’s group sees X-rays diffract off a crystal (1912). In 1952 Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling record “Photo 51”, an X-ray diffraction pattern of DNA that led to the double helix.

3 · The model

Waves come in from the left and meet a wall with an opening in it. On the right, the picture shows the wave’s height at each point, light for a crest and dark for a trough. The simulation treats each point of the opening as a tiny source (Huygens’ idea) and adds up all their waves at every point to the right. Change the wavelength and the opening width. The red dashed lines mark the first directions in which the waves cancel.

first dark direction: sin θ = wavelength ÷ opening width (for a round opening: 1.22 × wavelength ÷ diameter)If the opening is narrower than the wavelength, the formula has no solution because the wave spreads in every direction. Halve the wavelength or double the opening and the spread roughly halves.

Try this

  1. Press Voice at a doorway. The opening is only about one and a half wavelengths wide, and the waves spread out widely, filling the space behind the wall.
  2. Press Light at a doorway. The opening is many wavelengths wide, and the waves keep going nearly straight, with a narrow fan and a sharper “shadow”.
  3. Keep the opening fixed and slide the wavelength up and down. The fan opens and closes, but the opening does not change.
  4. Make the opening narrower than the wavelength. The waves come out as nearly circular ripples from a point.

4 · The reveal

Every gap is a new source

Huygens’ idea is that a wavefront is made of tiny sources side by side, each sending out its own little circular wave. In open space their waves line up in the forward direction and cancel in the others, so a wave seems to travel straight on. Block most of the front with a wall and only a few sources are left. Now there is less cancelling and the wave can spread sideways.

When the opening is many wavelengths wide, the sources across it cancel each other everywhere except close to straight ahead, so the beam stays narrow. When the opening is about one wavelength, there is hardly anyone to cancel with, and the wave leaves in all directions.

Why your friend sounds muffled

A person’s voice has wavelengths from about 3.4 metres (100 Hz) down to about 7 centimetres (5 kHz). Through a doorway 90 cm wide, a 1 kHz wave (34 cm) spreads to about ±22° before its first dark direction. A 100 Hz wave is wider than the door, so it goes out in every direction. The high frequencies, which carry the crisp consonants, are the ones that spread least. That is why a voice behind a wall sounds low and muffled. Light at half a micrometre going through the same door is spread by only about 0.00003 degrees.

The surprise

Light does bend round corners. It is only that the bend is a million times smaller than for sound. The same fact limits what a telescope or microscope can see.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now a chip factory is using extreme ultraviolet light of wavelength 13.5 nm to print circuit patterns, because diffraction limits how fine a pattern longer-wavelength light can draw. The Hubble Space Telescope, 2.4 m wide, is seeing detail as small as about 0.06 arcseconds, limited by diffraction. And a Wi-Fi signal with a wavelength of 12 cm is bending round a door frame to reach the next room.

  • Telescopes and microscopesDiffraction sets the smallest detail they can resolve: about 1.22 × wavelength ÷ lens width.
  • Chip makingShorter and shorter light (now 13.5 nm) is needed to print smaller circuits.
  • CDs, DVDs and Blu-raySmaller dots need a smaller wavelength: infrared for CDs, red for DVDs, blue-violet (405 nm) for Blu-ray.
  • X-ray crystallographyThe pattern of diffracted X-rays reveals the arrangement of atoms in crystals and in DNA.
  • Radio and mobile coverageLong waves, such as AM radio, bend over hills; short ones, like Wi-Fi, are blocked more easily.
  • Noise barriers and harboursLow-pitched sound leaks over and round a barrier; ocean swell bends in through a harbour mouth.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The simulation is two-dimensional and uses a simple scalar rule: each point of the opening sends out a circular wave, with a small factor that fades sideways, and the waves are added up. The wall is thin and perfectly absorbing, nothing is reflected back on the left, and the wave to the left of the wall is drawn as a plain plane wave. The picture is computed on blocks four pixels wide, so very fine detail is smoothed. The two presets are not real ratios: a real voice and a doorway are two or three wavelengths, and light and a doorway are about two million. Real sound also reflects off walls, floors and ceilings, which adds to what you hear round a corner.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Everything spreads through a gap. The size of the spread depends on the opening in wavelengths.
  2. Small opening, big spread. sin θ = wavelength ÷ opening: half the opening means about twice the spread.
  3. Light bends too. Only a millionth as much as sound at a door, which is why shadows look sharp, and why lenses can never be perfect.

Glossary and sources

Diffraction
The spreading of waves when they pass through an opening or round an edge.
Huygens’ principle
Every point on a wavefront can be treated as a source of small circular waves; their sum gives the next wavefront.
Resolution limit
The smallest detail an optical instrument can distinguish, set by diffraction.
Arago (Poisson) spot
The bright dot at the centre of the shadow of a small round disc.
