THM14 · Heat, entropy & randomness

The arrow of time

Run a film of a glass shattering backwards and you laugh, because it looks impossible. But every collision in that film would be perfectly legal in reverse. So what exactly is impossible about it?

Reading time ≈ 11 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 10–14 min

The 30-second version

The laws of motion look the same forwards and backwards. If you could flip every molecule’s velocity at once, a spread-out gas would rush back into its corner. But the reversal has to be perfect. A single tiny error is amplified by each collision until the film falls apart, and no real reversal is ever that precise.

0 · The story

Someone drops a glass. It hits the floor and explodes into a hundred pieces, scattering across the tiles. Now picture the film run backwards: the shards slide together, leap upward, and settle into your hand as a whole glass.

You know instantly which direction is real. But try explaining why. Newton’s laws don’t care. Every shard obeys the same equations in the film and in its reverse. There is no line in the rulebook that says “glasses may break but may not un-break.”

So where does the one-way street come from? That question sat in physics for over a century, and the simulation below puts it right in front of you.

1 · The illusion

“Time runs one way because the laws say so”

It is natural to think the direction of time is built into physics: the past is fixed, the future open, and the laws push events from one to the other. But the equations of motion of falling stones, colliding billiard balls, orbiting planets, and bouncing gas molecules are all time-symmetric. Play the motion backwards and you get another perfectly valid motion.

The one-way look must come from somewhere else. Here is where.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1872Ludwig Boltzmann derives that a gas will spread out and settle to an even state, and defines a quantity that can only decrease. It looks like a proof that time has a direction.
  2. 1876Josef Loschmidt objects: if the motions of molecules are reversible, then for every spreading-out there is a legal gathering-in, so no proof of one-way behaviour can come from the motions alone. A well-known, possibly apocryphal reply from Boltzmann: “Then go ahead and reverse them.”
  3. 1890 – 1896Henri Poincaré proves that a confined system will, given enough time, return arbitrarily close to its starting state. Ernst Zermelo uses this to press the same objection. The resolution turns on how long “enough” is.
  4. 1927Arthur Eddington names the problem the “arrow of time” in a lecture series published the next year.
  5. 1950Erwin Hahn discovers the spin echo: nuclear spins that have drifted out of step can be made to retrace their motion and come back into step. A real, laboratory reversal of dephasing.
  6. TodayMany physicists put the root of the arrow in the beginning of the universe: it started in a state of extraordinarily low entropy, and everything since has been the slow unwinding of that.

3 · The model

Seventy particles (hard-ish discs that gently push each other away when they touch) start bunched in the left third of the box. The motion uses a time-reversible method, so flipping every velocity is a genuine reversal. The slider sets how big an error creeps in at the moment of the flip.

reverse the film: (position, velocity) → (position, −velocity)If the laws of motion are time-symmetric, the particles retrace their path. In practice, any imprecision in the flip is magnified by every collision.

Try this

  1. Leave Error in reversal at none. Let the gas spread for about six seconds, then press Reverse time. The gas rushes back into the left third and the percentage returns to 100%.
  2. Press Restart, raise the error slider to 1e-7 px (a ten-millionth of a pixel!), wait the same time, and reverse. The gas no longer fully returns.
  3. Try bigger errors. Notice that once the error is above about a hundredth of a pixel, the gas just keeps spreading, as if the “reversal” had not happened.
  4. Wait much longer (30+ seconds) before reversing with none. Even then it fails: the computer’s own rounding is an error too.

4 · The reveal

Reversal is legal, and fragile

The first trial shows the point of the whole chapter: there is nothing in the laws that forbids the gas from gathering itself back. Flip the velocities exactly, and it does.

The second trial shows why we never see it in life. Each collision amplifies any small error, because a tiny difference in where two particles meet changes the angle they bounce away at, and that bigger difference is magnified at the next collision, and so on. Errors grow exponentially with the number of collisions. After a few hundred collisions an error smaller than an atom has become the size of the box. Reversing a real gas would need every one of 1026 molecules turned around with essentially infinite precision.

So why is there a direction at all?

If the laws don’t choose a direction, something else must. The answer is the starting condition. Our universe began in a very special state, one with extraordinarily low entropy. From such a start, almost every way of evolving leads to higher entropy. Time’s arrow points away from that special beginning. The shattered glass and the spreading gas are not obeying a law of one-way motion. They are simply doing what overwhelmingly most possible futures do, starting from an unusually tidy past.

The surprise

The arrow of time is not in the laws of motion. It comes from two things together: a very special starting state, and the enormous number of ways to be disordered.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now a hospital MRI scanner is using the idea from this chapter. Spin-echo sequences deliberately flip the phase of atomic nuclei so that signals that have drifted apart come back together and can be measured. It is a real, working “reverse the film” trick. It works because the nuclei barely interact with one another; a gas would be far less forgiving.

  • MRI spin echoesThe nearest laboratory cousin of Loschmidt’s reversal: dephased nuclear spins are made to retrace their steps.
  • Weather forecastingThe same sensitivity to tiny errors limits forecasts to about ten days or so, however good the computer.
  • Cryptography and random numbersSystems whose future depends exquisitely on tiny details are used to make unpredictable keys.
  • Simulation and renderingSymplectic, time-reversible integrators such as the velocity Verlet method used here are chosen for physics engines because, for a suitable step size and well-behaved dynamics, their energy error stays small and bounded over long runs. Energy is not exactly conserved, and reversibility alone does not guarantee this.
  • CosmologyThe question of why the early universe had low entropy is still open.
  • Everyday explanationsEvery time you see “you can’t unscramble an egg,” it is this argument in miniature.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is 70 soft discs in 2D, not a real gas. They repel gently rather than colliding as hard spheres, which keeps the motion exactly reversible but is not how atoms behave. The walls are also soft. The tiny “error” sizes are in screen pixels, not real distances, and the particular numbers depend on the box, the number of particles, and the time you wait. The qualitative point, that tiny errors grow exponentially, does not depend on those choices.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. The laws don’t care about direction. Flip every velocity and the film runs backwards, legally.
  2. The reversal must be perfect. Collisions amplify tiny errors exponentially, so real reversal is out of reach.
  3. The arrow comes from the past. We live downstream of a very low-entropy beginning.

Glossary and sources

Time-reversal symmetry
The property that if a motion is allowed, the same motion run backwards is also allowed.
Loschmidt reversal
Flipping all velocities at once to run a system back along its path.
Lyapunov exponent
The rate at which tiny differences grow in a chaotic system.
Poincaré recurrence
The eventual return of a confined system close to its starting state, after a very long time.
