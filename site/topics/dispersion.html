OSC17 · Oscillations & waves

Dispersion: sorting by speed

Surfers on Hawaii’s north shore watch the sea a day or two before a big swell. First comes a slow, long, almost lazy heave about twenty seconds from crest to crest. Over the following days the waves get closer together and quicker. That sorting happened over thousands of kilometres of open ocean, and it is the key to the whole chapter.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

In deep water, long waves travel faster than short ones. A storm makes a jumble of wavelengths, but over a long trip they sort themselves: the longest arrive first. This dispersion happens whenever the speed of a wave depends on its wavelength or colour. It splits white light in a prism, tells oceanographers where a storm was and spreads pulses in fibre-optic cables. In shallow water, and for sound in air, it is almost absent.

0 · The story

A storm rages near Antarctica. It stirs the sea into waves of every length at once, short and choppy and long and heaving, all jumbled in one place. Eight thousand kilometres later, on a beach in Hawaii, they arrive in order.

In 1963 the oceanographers Walter Munk, Frank Snodgrass and their colleagues from the Scripps Institution in California put this to the test. They set up sensitive wave recorders along a line stretching from New Zealand through the Pacific islands to Alaska, and followed swell from storms in the far Southern Ocean as it crossed the Pacific. At each station the waves arrived sorted by period, the longest first. By how far the sorting had gone, they could work out how far away the storm was and when it had happened, even for a storm no one had seen.

Why would a sea sort itself? Because waves of different lengths do not all travel at the same speed. The simulation drops a splash into the water and lets you watch it happen.

1 · The illusion

“Everything travels at one speed”

The last few chapters gave each wave one speed, set by its medium. For a rope or for sound in air, that is nearly true: a bass note and a piccolo reach the back row together, which is why an orchestra sounds in tune from a distance.

But a wave’s speed can also depend on its wavelength. When it does, a pulse made of many wavelengths falls apart as it travels: this is dispersion. The question is when it happens, and what it looks like.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1666Isaac Newton passes sunlight through a glass prism and shows white light is a mix of colours, each bent by a different amount. (We now know why: each colour has a different speed in glass.)
  2. 1781Joseph-Louis Lagrange shows that long waves in shallow water travel at √(g × depth), whatever their length.
  3. 1815–16The Paris Academy sets a prize problem on waves from a disturbance on deep water. Augustin Cauchy’s winning memoir (1816) treats exactly the “splash” in the simulation, as did Siméon Poisson’s.
  4. 1919–1953Heinrich Barkhausen hears eerie falling whistles on telephone lines in the First World War (1919). Llewellyn Storey explains them in 1953: radio waves from lightning spread out as they travel through space plasma.
  5. 1966Snodgrass, Munk, Miller and colleagues publish their record of ocean swell crossing the Pacific, showing the longest waves arriving first, as the theory says.
  6. 2004The Indian Ocean tsunami, whose waves are so long that even the deep ocean is “shallow” to them, reaches Sri Lanka in about two hours and East Africa in about seven.

4 · The reveal

Why deep water sorts, and shallow water does not

A water wave’s restoring force is gravity, and the water has to be moved around to make it. A long wave is felt deeper below the surface, so there is more water to move, but the pull of gravity on it is also stronger. The balance works out so that, in deep water, longer waves go faster: the speed grows as the square root of the wavelength. (Do not confuse this with ripples smaller than about two centimetres, where surface tension takes over and the opposite happens.) In shallow water the bottom limits how deep the motion can go, and then the depth alone sets the speed: all long waves go at √(g × depth).

What counts is the wavelength compared with the depth. A tsunami in the open ocean is 4 km deep but has a wavelength of 100 or 200 km, so to it the ocean is shallow. It races at √(9.8 × 4,000) ≈ 200 m/s, about 700 km/h, without sorting itself.

The same plot in other waves

The ocean is one example. Glass slows blue light more than red, so a prism fans out the colours. In optical fibre, pulses spread by about 17 picoseconds per nanometre of colour per kilometre of fibre in the common type. In the thin plasma around Earth, low radio frequencies travel more slowly than high ones, so a lightning crack arrives as a descending whistle, hearable on a radio. Earthquake surface waves sort too, and seismologists use that to map the Earth’s crust.

The surprise

A pulse is a bundle of wavelengths held together only because they move together. If they move at different speeds, the bundle falls apart in order, and the order tells you how far it travelled.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now swell from a storm in the far Southern Ocean is arriving on the coast of California or Alaska, longest waves first, and surf forecasters are reading the wave period from ocean buoys to predict when it will peak. A fibre-optic cable under the sea is carrying a stream of light pulses and the receiver is correcting for how far each colour has got ahead. And a radio listening to the upper atmosphere is picking up whistlers, falling tones from lightning flashes thousands of kilometres away.

  • Surf and ship forecastingThe period of arriving swell tells how far away the storm was.
  • Prisms, rainbows and lensesDifferent colours bend by different amounts because glass and water slow them differently.
  • Fibre-optic communicationPulses spread as they travel; engineers cancel or compensate the dispersion.
  • Tsunami warningTsunami waves are non-dispersive in deep water, so arrival times can be computed from the depth map.
  • SeismologySurface waves of different periods sample different depths, so their sorting maps the crust.
  • Space radio scienceWhistlers reveal the density of plasma along Earth’s magnetic field lines.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The splash is drawn as a line through the water, so it does not fade with distance the way a real round ripple does; the vertical scale is enlarged as the splash weakens, which the readout shows. The waves are small (linear), so large steep waves that break or change speed with height are left out. Surface tension and viscosity are ignored, and the depth is the same everywhere. A real swell is a mix of directions. The simulation sums 200 pure waves, so the ripples are exact for this model, but the picture runs at four times real speed. A 3 metre splash on a one metre depth is a pond-sized event, and a real tsunami is a thousand times longer.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Dispersion means speed depends on wavelength. A pulse made of many wavelengths then falls apart as it travels.
  2. Deep water sorts, shallow water does not. In deep water long waves lead. In shallow water all long waves share one speed, √(g × depth).
  3. Sorting is a clock and a ruler. The order of arrival tells you how far and how long ago the wave started.

Glossary and sources

Dispersion
The dependence of a wave’s speed on its wavelength or frequency.
Swell
Long-period waves that have travelled out of the storm that made them.
Period
The time between successive crests at one place.
Deep and shallow water (for waves)
Deep: depth more than about half a wavelength. Shallow: depth less than about a twentieth of a wavelength.
Whistler
A falling-tone radio signal made when a lightning pulse is dispersed in near-Earth plasma.
