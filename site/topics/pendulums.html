OSC02 · Oscillations & waves

Pendulums and simple harmonic motion

A grandfather clock keeps good time as its swing slowly shrinks, as the weight on its pendulum could be anything, and as the clock is moved from place to place. A pendulum is a better timekeeper than it has any right to be.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

A pendulum’s swing time depends on one thing you can control: its length. Not its weight, and, for small swings, not the size of the swing. A pendulum a bit under a metre long takes two seconds to go back and forth. A 60° swing takes only about 7% longer than a tiny one.

0 · The story

Legend has it that in 1583, a nineteen-year-old student named Galileo Galilei sat in the cathedral of Pisa, bored by the service, and watched a bronze lamp swinging from the ceiling on its long chain. Using his own pulse as a clock, he noticed something strange. As the lamp’s swings got smaller and smaller, each swing took about the same number of heartbeats.

It may never have happened exactly like that. But Galileo did study the pendulum, and about 1602 he wrote that the time of a swing seems not to depend on how wide it is. The idea took half a century to be put to use. In 1656, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens built the first pendulum clock, and the accuracy of timekeeping improved at once, from around fifteen minutes a day to about fifteen seconds.

Why does swinging in a wide arc take the same time as swinging in a narrow one? And why doesn’t the weight at the end matter at all?

1 · The illusion

“A bigger swing takes longer”

The wider swing has more distance to cover on each pass, so it ought to take longer. A heavy weight ought to pull harder, so it ought to swing faster. Our instinct says both.

The first is almost wrong, and the second is exactly wrong. The simulation shows two pendulums side by side: one swinging wide, one barely moving.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1583The legend: Galileo, in Pisa cathedral, times a swinging lamp with his pulse. (The story comes from a biography written long after his death.)
  2. c. 1602Galileo writes that a pendulum’s swing time does not depend on the width of the swing, and notes that it depends on the length only.
  3. 1656Christiaan Huygens builds the first pendulum clock. Timekeeping accuracy leaps forward.
  4. 1673In Horologium Oscillatorium, Huygens gives the formula T = 2π√(L/g), and shows that it is only approximately true for wide swings.
  5. 1851Léon Foucault hangs a 28 kg weight on a 67 m wire in the Panthéon in Paris and shows that its swing slowly turns, proof that the Earth spins.

3 · The model

Two pendulums of the same length hang side by side. The amber one swings through the “big swing” angle you choose. The teal one swings through only 5°. They start together, and each prints its measured swing time.

T = 2π √(L / g) (small swings) T ≈ 1.07 × that at a 60° swingL = length of the pendulum, g = 9.81 m/s². The mass of the bob does not appear. For a 1 m pendulum, T = 2.006 s.

Try this

  1. Set the big swing to 10°. The two pendulums stay in step: their times agree to 0.2%.
  2. Raise it to 60°. The amber pendulum falls slowly behind: it takes about 7% longer per swing.
  3. Shorten the string to 0.25 m. The swing gets twice as quick. Quarter the length and you halve the time.
  4. Notice the note at the bottom: there is no setting for the weight. It does not matter.

4 · The reveal

Why the weight cancels

A heavier bob is pulled down harder by gravity. But it is also harder to move, in exactly the same ratio. The two cancel, just as they do for a falling stone (free fall). That is why a pendulum takes the same time with a lead weight or a wooden one, and why it can be used to measure gravity itself: measure the swing time and the length, and you can work out g.

The size of the swing matters only a little because the pull towards the middle is nearly proportional to how far the bob is displaced when the swing is small: the same straight-line behaviour as a spring. Make the swing wide and the pull falls a little short of proportional, so the swing takes a bit longer. A pendulum is an almost perfect oscillator for small swings, and a good one for medium ones.

The pendulum that turns

A pendulum is also a very stable swinging arrow. Its plane of swing turns slowly, as seen from the floor. Foucault showed this in 1851: in Paris, a long pendulum turns its plane by about eleven degrees an hour, the Earth’s turning (15° an hour) times the sine of Paris’s latitude. Only at the North Pole is the picture simple: the plane stays fixed relative to the stars while the Earth turns beneath it, a full circle in a day. At other latitudes the local vertical itself is turning, so the plane precesses more slowly, and at the equator not at all.

The surprise

Galileo’s conclusion, a swing time that depends on length alone, is nearly exact for small swings. It gave the world its first accurate clocks. But it is not perfectly exact: a 60° swing takes 7% longer, a detail that haunted clockmakers for decades.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now a pendulum hangs in the Panthéon in Paris, the same building where Foucault first showed that the Earth rotates, its swing slowly turning around the floor, about eleven degrees every hour. A similar one swings in the entrance hall of the United Nations in New York. And somewhere a metronome on a piano is using the same law: a metronome’s sliding weight changes the beat in a related way: it is a compound pendulum, a rod with a movable weight, and moving the weight up slows the beat (the period depends on how the mass is spread, not just a string length).

  • ClocksPendulum clocks were the most accurate timekeepers from 1656 to the 1930s.
  • Measuring gravitySwing time and length give g, used by geologists to survey the Earth’s interior.
  • MetronomesA weight sliding on a rod changes how the mass is spread and so sets the beat (a compound pendulum).
  • Foucault pendulumsFound in museums, they demonstrate the Earth’s rotation.
  • Playground swingsA swing is a pendulum; its timing depends on the chain length, not on the child’s weight.
  • EngineeringA pendulum is the model for everything that swings: cranes, a person walking, a hanging lamp.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The pendulum here is a point weight on a massless, perfectly stiff string, swinging without any air resistance or friction. Real pendulums are not points: the string has mass, the bob has size, and these shift the swing time slightly. The amber and teal pendulums never lose energy, unlike a real clock’s pendulum that needs a push each swing. The formula for the swing time ignores the slight dependence on the swing’s size; the simulation includes it, which is why the two pendulums drift apart.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Length sets the time. T = 2π√(L/g).
  2. The weight does not matter. Gravity pulls harder on a heavier bob, but it is harder to move.
  3. The size of the swing hardly matters. Only 7% longer at 60°.

Glossary and sources

Period
The time for one full swing back and forth.
Simple harmonic motion
Motion in which the pull back to the middle is proportional to the distance from it.
Isochronism
The property that the swing time does not depend on the size of the swing.
Foucault pendulum
A long pendulum whose swing plane turns slowly because the Earth rotates.
