THM09 · Heat, entropy & randomness

Heat diffusion on a grid

A hot spoon cools, a cold room warms, and nothing in between is giving orders. Heat spreads because every spot compares itself with its neighbours, nothing more.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–12 min

The 30-second version

Every cell nudges its temperature toward the average of its neighbours. Sharp hot-cold contrasts get smoothed first and fastest; the final state is an even temperature. A purely local rule produces a global, one-way smoothing, which is the heat equation.

0 · The story

You stir a mug of tea with a metal spoon and leave it there. A few seconds later, the handle, which was nowhere near the tea, is warm.

Nobody sent the heat up the handle. No molecule in the spoon knew about the tea. Each one only did something very small: it jostled its neighbours, and shared a little of its jiggle with whichever was calmer. Repeat that between neighbours enough times and a whole spoon warms.

The people who first wrote this down thought it needed deep mathematics, and it did. But the core idea fits in one picture: every spot compares itself with the spots next to it.

1 · The illusion

Heat seems to “flow” like a fluid with a purpose

Put a hot pan on a counter and the warmth seems to go somewhere, as if heat were a substance pouring from hot to cold. That is a good picture, and for a long time it was the working one (the “caloric” theory). But it hides the surprising part: nobody directs it. There is no pump, no plan, and no memory of where the heat started.

So how does a lump of heat in one corner end up as an even warmth, always in that direction and never the reverse?

2 · Where the idea came from

Fourier and the problem that needed new mathematics

  1. 1701Newton’s law of cooling: a hot object cools at a rate proportional to its temperature excess over its surroundings, a statement about how fast, not how it spreads inside.
  2. 1807 · 1822Joseph Fourier presents his work on heat conduction to the Paris Academy (1807) and publishes Théorie analytique de la chaleur (1822). He writes the rule for temperature varying in space and time, now the heat equation, and solves it by splitting any temperature profile into simple sine waves, which created Fourier series.
  3. 1855Adolf Fick adapts Fourier’s law to the spreading of dissolved substances: diffusion. Salt in water and heat in metal obey the same mathematics.
  4. 1860sWilliam Thomson (Kelvin) uses the heat equation to estimate how long the Earth has been cooling, arriving at tens of millions of years. The method was correct; the answer was wrong because it missed radioactive heating and mantle convection. A model can be right and its conclusion still miss reality.
  5. 1905Einstein links diffusion to random molecular motion, connecting this chapter to random walks: heat spreading is the average of many tiny random hops.

The idea in one line

The rate at which a spot warms is proportional to how much hotter its surroundings are than it is: curvature in the temperature profile is what drives change.

4 · The reveal

The averaging rule already has a direction

The averaging rule is already a one-way rule: run it backwards and it would have to un-average, sharpening a smooth grid into a hot patch, which is a different and unstable rule. Real heat is made of atoms whose individual motions could in principle run either way, so the one-way behaviour comes from counting: there are overwhelmingly more ways to be evenly warm than concentrated, and mixing is what happens by default. This is the first hint of the arrow of time (THM14).

Two more things become visible:

  • Sharp is fragile. A feature half as wide smooths about four times faster, because heat only needs to travel half the distance in each direction. Time to smooth scales as distance squared, the same square-root law as the random walk, read backwards.
  • Total heat is conserved. The patch fades, but the amount of heat in the grid doesn’t change. It only gets spread out.

The surprise

Local, simple rules can generate global, irreversible behaviour. The averaging is symmetric in space but still smooths in one time direction only; any reversibility lives in the atoms underneath. No coordinator is needed.

5 · Where it shows up

Anywhere something spreads or smooths

Happening right now

Right now the processor in your phone or laptop is deciding whether to slow itself down. Chip designers run heat-diffusion calculations like this one, in three dimensions, to place cooling and decide when to throttle (most processors start slowing at around 100 °C). And every time you blur a photo, the software is literally letting brightness diffuse between neighbouring pixels.

  • Chips and batteriesThermal engineers solve this exact equation to place heat sinks and keep processors and cells within safe temperatures.
  • Blurring a photoA Gaussian blur is heat diffusion run for a short time on an image’s brightness. “Sharpness” is a hot-cold edge being smoothed.
  • Cooking and food safetyThe time for the centre of a roast to warm grows with the square of its thickness. Cutting a roast in half more than halves the wait.
  • GeologyThe cooling of lava, the thermal history of the crust, and Kelvin’s Earth-age estimate all use conduction.
  • Drug and dye spreadingFick’s diffusion in tissue and gels is the same maths with concentration instead of temperature.
  • Machine learningDiffusion models for image generation add noise in a diffusion-like process and learn to reverse it, a nod to heat’s one-way smoothing.

6 · Where the simulation lies

What the grid leaves out

Honest limits

This is conduction only: no moving fluid (convection), no radiation, and the material is the same everywhere with a fixed diffusion rate. Real materials conduct heat at different rates, and conductivity can change with temperature. The edges of the grid are insulated, so heat can’t escape, and the colours show relative warmth, not real degrees. The grid itself is a coarse approximation of continuous space.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Local rule, global result. Averaging with neighbours is enough to smooth any temperature landscape.
  2. Small features vanish first. Smoothing time grows with distance squared.
  3. One-way by counting. The smoothing rule runs in one direction only. Underneath, atoms’ motions could run either way; the direction comes from there being far more even states than lumpy ones.

Conduction
Heat transfer through direct contact of neighbouring material.
Heat equation

Diffusivity (α)
How quickly a material smooths out temperature differences.
