THM02 · Heat, entropy & randomness

Brownian motion in a box

Look through a microscope at a drop of water and a speck of dust, and the speck will never keep still. For eighty years nobody could say why. The answer ended an argument about whether atoms are real.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A visible grain in water is bombarded by billions of invisible molecules every second. The hits from different sides almost, but never exactly, cancel, so the grain staggers around. How far it staggers depends on how many molecules there are and how fast they move, and that is how Jean Perrin counted atoms in 1908.

0 · The story

It is 1827, and a Scottish botanist named Robert Brown is looking down a microscope at pollen grains floating in water. He expects to see grains. What he sees is motion: tiny particles jittering, swerving, never resting.

Brown does the sensible thing: he asks whether the pollen is alive. Perhaps it is some kind of microscopic swimmer. So he tries pollen that has been dead for ages. It still dances. He tries ground-up glass. It dances. He tries powdered granite. It dances too.

Nothing living is pushing these specks. So what is? The question will sit unanswered, like a stone in a shoe, for nearly eighty years.

1 · The illusion

“If it moves, something living must be moving it”

Our instinct says that a thing which jerks about on its own must have an engine, a will, or at least a current to carry it. A speck of rock has none of these. And yet it moves, forever, with no energy supply and no rest.

Look at the simulation below first. You will see the answer sitting in plain view, and then you can hide it and watch the mystery that Brown faced.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 60 BCEThe Roman poet Lucretius describes dust motes dancing in a sunbeam and argues that unseen atoms must be shoving them about. A remarkable guess, but with no way to test it.
  2. 1785Jan Ingenhousz notices coal dust jittering on the surface of alcohol. The observation is forgotten.
  3. 1827Robert Brown watches pollen grains in water, then rules out life by testing non-living powders. He cannot explain the motion, and the name “Brownian motion” sticks.
  4. 1905Albert Einstein, not yet sure that the motion he is predicting is the one Brown saw, works out what should happen if water is made of molecules in constant thermal motion. A grain’s average squared distance from its start must grow in proportion to time, at a rate linked to the number of molecules.
  5. 1906Marian Smoluchowski reaches the same result by a different route.
  6. 1908–09Jean Perrin photographs and measures the positions of tiny grains at regular intervals, confirms Einstein’s prediction, and extracts a value for Avogadro’s number. Atoms are no longer a hypothesis. He wins the 1926 Nobel Prize in Physics.

3 · The model

The simulation is a box of invisible particles (teal) and one large amber grain, about forty times heavier than each particle. Particles bounce off the walls and off the grain, never off each other, so the only thing you are watching is many small pushes acting on one big thing.

average (distance)² ≈ 4 · D · tThis is for motion on a flat surface like the simulation’s box: 2Dt along each direction, so 4Dt in the plane (6Dt in three dimensions). It holds once the grain has forgotten its starting motion and before it reaches the walls. D, the diffusion constant, is bigger when the surrounding particles are faster or fewer obstacles are in the way. Doubling the time doubles the squared distance, not the distance.

4 · The reveal

Why a still grain can’t stay still

At every instant, molecules hit the grain from every direction. If the hits were perfectly balanced the grain would sit still. But a molecule is a random thing: a few more arrive from the left than the right in this instant, a few more from above than below in the next. The imbalance is small and short-lived, but it is never zero. That leftover is the jerk you see.

Two features are worth sitting with.

The path has no steady speed

Early observers tried to measure how fast Brownian particles move, and got answers that were far too small. They had been fooled: they watched at a coarse time-scale, joining dots with straight lines. Look more closely, and each straight line is itself a jagged zig-zag, and each zig-zag is more zig-zags. The picture below shows the same path seen at two time resolutions. The measured “speed” keeps rising as you look finer, because you keep finding more wiggle. Einstein’s trick was to stop asking about speed and ask about how far it gets. (A perfect mathematical random path really has no speed at all. A real grain has mass and so does have a velocity, and in 2010 researchers measured it for a tiny bead in air. But that only shows up over microseconds or less, far finer than anyone can see by eye, so at the scales of the simulation the picture of a path without a steady speed is the right one.)

Many hits blur into a shiver

Move the particle slider up. More particles means more hits per second, and the individual jerks blur into a busier, finer shiver. A real grain is struck billions of times per second, which is why real Brownian motion looks like a restless tremble, and why you needed a microscope to see it at all.

The surprise

The jitter is how we know atoms exist. If matter were smooth, there would be nothing to make the imbalance: the grain would sit still. The motion is evidence of the graininess of the world.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now a lab somewhere is firing a laser through a vial of nanoparticles and measuring how the scattered light flickers. The flicker rate tells them how fast the particles are jittering, and therefore how big they are. This technique, dynamic light scattering, is a routine way to size proteins, vaccine components and paint pigments.

  • Counting atomsPerrin’s measurement turned the jitter of visible grains into a value for Avogadro’s number.
  • Sizing nanoparticlesDynamic light scattering and particle-tracking instruments infer a particle’s size from how fast it wanders.
  • FinanceModels used to price options treat prices as a continuous cousin of this motion, a mathematical Brownian process.
  • Cells and medicineMolecules reach their targets inside a cell mainly by diffusing, which is the same random wandering.
  • Tiny machinesMolecular motors and nanoscale sensors work in a storm of thermal jostling that engineers must design around.
  • Computer graphics and AIRandom-walk and diffusion processes generate natural-looking noise and underpin today’s image-generating diffusion models.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Real particles are not points that ignore each other: the particles here never collide with one another, and everything lives in 2D rather than 3D. The grain is only forty times heavier than a “molecule” here; a real pollen grain is trillions of times heavier than a water molecule, so each real hit is vastly weaker and there are vastly more of them. The walls bounce everything perfectly, and the simulation shows each collision as a clean event, where in reality the motion is continuous on any scale you can see. It is a faithful picture of the mechanism, not of the numbers.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Many small pushes don’t cancel. Their leftover is what makes the grain move.
  2. Squared distance grows with time. Double the time, and you get double the squared spread, not double the distance.
  3. Jitter is proof of atoms. A smooth world would have no jitter to see.

Glossary and sources

Brownian motion
The irregular motion of a small particle suspended in a fluid, caused by molecular collisions.
Diffusion constant (D)
How quickly spreading occurs; higher for faster or smaller particles.
Avogadro’s number
The number of molecules in one mole, about 6 × 10²³.
Mean squared displacement
The average of (distance from start)² over many particles or many runs.
