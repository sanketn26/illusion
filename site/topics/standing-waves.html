OSC11 · Oscillations & waves

Standing waves

A guitar string could in principle vibrate in any shape at all. It doesn’t. It picks out a short list of patterns and ignores the rest, and that short list is the reason music exists.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

Waves bounce off the fixed ends of a string and overlap with themselves. Left to itself, the string can only keep vibrating in patterns that fit a whole number of half-waves between the ends; these are its allowed notes. Boundaries turn a continuous world into a list of allowed notes.

0 · The story

Try this with a guitar, if you have one. Pluck the open string. Now rest a finger on the string directly above the 12th fret — don’t press it down, just touch — and pluck again. Lift your finger away.

A bright, bell-like note rings out, exactly an octave above the open string. Your finger did nothing but touch. You didn’t shorten the string, as pressing would. So how did lightly touching one point change the note?

You did something subtler. You made one point on the string unable to move. And that single constraint threw away every pattern of vibration that wanted to move that point, leaving only the ones that didn’t.

1 · The illusion

“A string vibrates as one smooth arc”

Pluck a string and slow-motion footage shows an elegant arc swinging back and forth. It looks like a single motion. In fact the string is doing many things at once, and the one you remember is only the loudest.

The second thing we assume is that any frequency is available: you could vibrate the string at any rate if you pushed it that way. A fixed string rejects almost every rate. The simulation shows which survive, and why.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 500 BCETradition credits Pythagoras with discovering that strings whose lengths are in simple ratios (2:1, 3:2) sound pleasant together. The experiment is a one-string instrument called the monochord.
  2. 1636Marin Mersenne establishes how a string’s pitch depends on its length, tension and thickness: shorter, tighter, thinner strings sing higher.
  3. 1713Brook Taylor calculates the fundamental frequency of a string from Newton’s laws.
  4. 1747–53D’Alembert writes the wave equation; Daniel Bernoulli argues that a plucked string vibrates in many modes at once, added together.
  5. 1787Ernst Chladni sprinkles sand on vibrating plates and shows the nodal patterns of standing waves in two dimensions.
  6. 1926Erwin Schrödinger describes an electron in an atom as a standing wave around the nucleus. The same “only patterns that fit” rule gives atoms their discrete energy levels.

3 · The model

Both ends of the string are clamped, so those points cannot move. A wave running along the string reflects from each end, flipped upside down. The reflected wave runs back, reflects again, and so on. Left and right movers keep overlapping. Only a wavelength that fits the length can form a steady pattern.

allowed wavelengths: λₙ = 2L / n allowed frequencies: fₙ = n · v / (2L) n = 1, 2, 3 …L = length of the string, v = wave speed on it. The lowest note (n = 1) is the fundamental; the rest are overtones, or harmonics.

Try this

  1. Slide the harmonic from 1 to 8. Count the red dots: there are always n + 1 of them.
  2. Press Show the two travelling waves. The amber wave moves right, the coral wave moves left. Neither is standing, but their sum is.
  3. Look for the moments when the amber and coral curves are mirror images. The teal string goes flat then. When they line up it swings widest.
  4. Notice the nodes: wherever amber and coral are always opposite, the string never moves.

4 · The reveal

The ends choose the notes

The key is that a string end that cannot move forces a node there. A wave that fits its length (half a wave, or one, or one and a half) lines up with its own reflection at every pass and keeps the same shape. Any other wavelength would need the ends to move, so it cannot be a free vibration of this string. Nothing is “erased”: any free motion of an ideal string is simply a mix of the allowed patterns. If you drive the string at some other frequency it does respond, but weakly, following your driver, and the strong ringing appears only when you drive near an allowed frequency (that is resonance, the next-but-one chapters). Friction eventually takes energy out of every pattern.

The same logic explains your guitar trick. Touching the 12th fret forces a node in the middle of the string, which rules out every pattern that wants to move there. The lowest pattern left is the one with two half-waves, an octave higher. At the 7th fret (a third of the way along) you get three half-waves, and at the 5th fret (a quarter), four.

Why a violin doesn’t sound like a flute

A real note is not a single mode. A pluck or bow excites many at once, in different proportions, and each instrument has its own recipe. That mixture is its timbre. (It also means the “lowest note” is only the most prominent part of what you hear.)

The surprise

Boundaries turn a continuous range into a list. Wherever a wave is trapped, in a string, a pipe, a drum, a laser, or an atom, only certain patterns survive. That “only patterns that fit” rule is the seed of quantum mechanics.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now if there is a microwave oven nearby, the waves inside it form a standing pattern with hot and cold spots about six centimetres apart, which is exactly why food has a turntable. People do a classic experiment: remove the plate, microwave a slab of chocolate, measure the distance between melted spots, and multiply by two and by the oven’s frequency (2.45 GHz) to measure the speed of light.

  • Musical instrumentsStrings, pipes, drums and bells are all machines for selecting standing waves.
  • Microwave ovensThe cavity forms standing waves, and uneven heating follows the pattern of nodes.
  • LasersMirrors at each end of a laser form a cavity that selects a particular standing light wave.
  • Watches and phonesA quartz crystal rings in a standing mode at 32,768 cycles per second. That is the “tick”.
  • EarthquakesAfter a very large quake the whole Earth rings like a bell for weeks, with a lowest “note” that has a period of about 54 minutes.
  • AtomsAn electron in an atom is a standing wave, which is why atoms have only certain energy levels.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

An ideal string is perfectly flexible, perfectly fixed, and loses no energy, so this one rings forever. A real string is slightly stiff, which makes higher harmonics a little sharp, and it loses energy to the air and the instrument body (which is how you hear it at all). The simulation slows higher harmonics so you can see them, and a real 440 Hz string moves about seven hundred times faster than your eye can follow. Only the vertical motion is shown; real strings also vibrate sideways and twist.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Ends choose the notes. Only wavelengths that fit a whole number of half-waves survive.
  2. Standing = two travelling. A standing wave is a left-mover and a right-mover added together.
  3. Nodes are constraints. Pin a point and every pattern that moves it is thrown out.

Glossary and sources

Standing wave
A wave pattern that stays in place, with fixed nodes and swinging antinodes.
Node / antinode
A point that never moves / a point that swings the most.
Fundamental and harmonics
The lowest allowed frequency, and whole-number multiples of it.
Timbre
The tone quality of a sound, set by the mix of harmonics.
