OSC07 · Oscillations & waves

Sine wave basics

A stadium wave circles the arena while every spectator stays in their seat. That small paradox is the whole idea of a wave: the thing that travels is not the thing that moves.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–9 min

The 30-second version

A wave is a pattern moving through stuff that mostly stays put. The simplest pattern is a smooth up-and-down called a sine wave. Three numbers describe it: how far apart the crests are (wavelength), how often something wiggles (frequency), and how fast the pattern travels (speed = wavelength × frequency).

0 · The story

In 2002, three physicists, Illés Farkas, Dirk Helbing and Tamás Vicsek, did something delightfully unusual: they filmed stadium crowds doing the Mexican wave and measured it. They found that the wave rolled round the stands at roughly twelve metres per second, about twenty seats a second, usually clockwise, and that it was only six to twelve metres wide.

Twelve metres per second is faster than a sprinter. Yet nobody in that crowd ran anywhere. Each person did one thing: stand, raise arms, sit. The wave was not a person. It was a pattern of who was standing when, and it travelled around the stadium while the people stayed put.

Pause on that. Something with a speed, a width, and a direction, that is not made of any one thing. That is what a wave is.

1 · The illusion

“Something has to travel for a wave to travel”

When a ripple crosses a pond, it feels as if the water itself is moving outward. Throw a leaf in, and watch: the leaf bobs up and down and stays put. The same is true for sound (the air molecules shiver in place, by about ten nanometres for ordinary speech) and for a wave on a rope.

What moves is energy and pattern, passed from neighbour to neighbour. This chapter takes the simplest pattern and gets every term right.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1636Marin Mersenne publishes work on musical instruments, including an early estimate of a string’s vibration frequency: a first step to counting waves.
  2. c. 1700Joseph Sauveur coins the words node, harmonic and fundamental while studying vibrating strings.
  3. 1747Jean le Rond d’Alembert writes the first wave equation, for a vibrating string. Euler and Daniel Bernoulli then argue for years about what shapes it allows.
  4. 1822Joseph Fourier shows that any repeating shape can be built by adding sine waves. The sine wave becomes the alphabet of all waves.
  5. 1860sJames Clerk Maxwell shows that light itself is a travelling wave of electric and magnetic fields.

4 · The reveal

Three numbers, one rule

Wavelength is a length: crest to crest. Frequency is a rate: how many wiggles per second at a single spot. Speed is how quickly a crest travels. They are tied together by a simple counting argument. If a crest passes a point f times per second, and neighbouring crests are λ apart, then a crest must travel λ × f every second.

This rule is universal. It works for a stadium wave, a ripple, a note, radio, or light. It is also the reason a high note and a low note travel at the same speed in the air: a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength, with the product fixed by the air.

Why the sine?

A point that goes round a circle at a steady rate has a height that, plotted against time, is exactly a sine wave. So the sine is what you get from the simplest steady motion. It is also the shape of motion near any stable resting point, which is why springs, pendulums, atoms, and strings all produce it. Fourier’s discovery in 1822 then makes it the alphabet: every other wave shape is a stack of sines of different sizes and wavelengths.

The surprise

A wave carries energy and information without carrying stuff. That is why a wave can cross an ocean while the water stays, and why a radio station can reach you without sending you anything you can touch.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the electricity in your wall socket is a sine wave, rising and falling smoothly 50 or 60 times a second. Your Wi-Fi router is also using sine waves, at 2.4 or 5 billion cycles per second, and varying them slightly to carry your messages. Your watch or phone is counting the ticks of a quartz crystal vibrating 32,768 times per second.

  • Sound and musicA pure tone is a sine wave. Every real sound is a stack of them.
  • Radio, Wi-Fi and phonesInformation rides on sine-wave carriers whose amplitude, frequency or phase is varied.
  • Mains electricityPower stations produce sine waves because rotating coils naturally do.
  • TimekeepingQuartz oscillators and atomic clocks count the cycles of an extremely steady sine wave.
  • MedicineUltrasound uses megahertz sound waves; MRI uses radio-frequency waves tuned to atoms.
  • SeismologyEarthquake waves are analysed by splitting them into frequencies to learn what the Earth is made of.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is a perfectly regular, single-frequency wave in a medium with no friction and no limit. Real wave speeds can depend on the wavelength (this is called dispersion, as in water waves or light in glass), and real waves lose energy. A stadium wave, by contrast, is not a sine at all: it is a triggered, crowd-driven pulse. The pattern on screen also shows only one dimension and does not show how energy is actually passed from one dot to the next.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. A wave is a pattern. The medium wiggles in place; the pattern travels.
  2. Speed = wavelength × frequency. The one equation that works for every wave.
  3. Sines are the alphabet. Any wave shape can be built by adding sine waves.

Glossary and sources

Amplitude
The size of the swing from the middle to a crest.
Wavelength (λ)
The distance between two neighbouring crests.
Frequency (f)
How many full wiggles pass a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz).
Transverse / longitudinal
Whether the medium moves across (rope) or along (sound) the direction the wave travels.
