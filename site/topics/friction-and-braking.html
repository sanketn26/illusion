MEC05 · Motion & forces

Friction and braking distance

A police officer measures a skid mark 40 metres long on a dry road and, with a pencil and one formula, tells the court how fast the car was going. The same formula explains why speed is more dangerous than it feels.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Brakes work by turning movement energy into heat through friction. Movement energy goes as speed squared, so a car going twice as fast needs four times the braking distance. Add the distance covered while the driver is still reacting, which grows with speed, and a small increase in speed makes a big difference to whether you stop in time.

0 · The story

It is a dry afternoon on a straight road. A car has skidded to a stop, leaving a black mark on the asphalt, forty metres long. The driver, sure that they were “only doing the limit,” disputes the ticket. An accident investigator walks the length of the mark with a measuring wheel, then pulls out a pocket calculator.

He multiplies the length by the road’s grip, a number he has measured for exactly this kind of surface, by a constant, and takes the square root. The answer is on the screen before the driver has finished arguing: about 84 kilometres per hour. The mark was longer than the driver had admitted. The formula does not lie. It comes straight from the laws of friction.

Behind it is a fact most of us never quite believe. When you double your speed, you do not double the distance you need to stop. You quadruple it.

1 · The illusion

“Twice as fast, twice the stopping distance”

It sounds right. Go twice as fast, take twice as long to stop. And it is right for one part of the journey: the distance you travel before your foot even reaches the brake. But the part where the brakes are working is different. It grows much faster.

The simulation breaks the total into those two pieces so that you can see which one is responsible for the explosion in distance at high speed.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 1493Leonardo da Vinci experiments with sliding blocks and finds that friction doesn’t depend on how large the contact area is. His notes lie unpublished.
  2. 1699Guillaume Amontons rediscovers the rules: friction is proportional to the load pressing the surfaces together, and independent of the apparent area.
  3. 1785Charles-Augustin de Coulomb confirms them and separates friction when sliding from friction when stuck.
  4. 1950Frank Bowden and David Tabor explain why: surfaces touch only at microscopic bumps, and the real contact area is a tiny fraction of the apparent one.
  5. 1978The first mass-produced anti-lock braking system (ABS) arrives on a Mercedes-Benz S-Class, keeping wheels at the edge of skidding where grip is greatest.

3 · The model

Try this

  1. At 50 km/h on a dry road (grip 0.70) the car stops about 28 m after the driver sees the hazard, well short of it.
  2. Raise the speed to 100 km/h. Twice the speed, but the stopping distance is about three times larger, and the car hits the hazard at about 50 km/h.
  3. Go back to 50 km/h and reduce the grip to 0.40 (wet). The braking part grows by about three-quarters (0.70 ÷ 0.40 = 1.75). Now try 0.10 (ice).
  4. Find the fastest speed at which the car just stops in time on a dry road. A few km/h more makes it hit.

4 · The reveal

The square, and the cancellation

Your moving car carries movement energy, ½mv². To stop, the brakes must turn all of it into heat. Friction removes energy at a steady rate per metre: the force μmg times the distance. Set the two equal and the distance comes out as v²/(2μg), proportional to the square of the speed. Double v and the distance goes up by four. Treble it and by nine.

Notice what is missing from the formula: the mass. A heavy lorry has more energy to get rid of, but its tyres press harder on the road and so grip proportionally harder. The two cancel, just as weight and inertia cancelled in free fall. (In real life a heavy vehicle does take longer, but because brakes, tyres and heat limits are designed for lighter loads, not because of the physics here.)

Why speed limits aren’t arbitrary

Going from 50 to 60 km/h feels trivial. But the braking distance rises by 44% (from 14 to 20 m on a dry road), and the speed at which you would hit an obstacle that only just was avoided at 50 is not 10 km/h more but more than 30 km/h. Small changes in speed produce big changes in outcome because the physics is squared.

The surprise

Intuition treats speed as a straight line, but the energy a car must lose is a curve. A pedestrian struck at 50 km/h faces roughly four times the movement energy of one struck at 25 km/h, and that, not the speed itself, is what injures.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now cars on the road are braking in a way this chapter predicts. Anti-lock systems pulse the brakes several times a second to keep the tyres at the edge of grip, and automatic emergency braking, which is required on new cars in the EU, uses radar or cameras to apply them before a driver has finished reacting. Road designers compute “stopping sight distance” from exactly these two terms, reaction plus braking, to decide how far ahead a driver must be able to see.

  • Accident reconstructionInvestigators estimate speed from skid-mark length, v ≈ √(254·μ·d) in km/h with d in metres.
  • Road and junction designSight distances and speed limits are set from stopping distances.
  • Anti-lock brakes and traction controlKeep the wheels at peak friction instead of sliding.
  • Tyres and road surfacesCompounds and textures are tuned to raise μ, especially when wet.
  • Aircraft landingRunway lengths are chosen from landing-roll distances computed in the same way.
  • Automatic emergency brakingSystems decide when braking must start, using the formula in reverse.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Real friction is not a single number: it varies with speed, tyre temperature, load and surface, and the best braking occurs just before the wheels lock. Here the grip is constant, the road is flat, and the brakes apply their full force instantly. Reaction time is fixed at one second (real values range from about 0.7 to 1.5 s or more), and the hazard is a fixed 70 m away. The model also ignores the weight shifting forward onto the front wheels under braking, and air drag, which helps slightly at high speed.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Braking distance grows with speed squared. Twice as fast needs four times the distance.
  2. Reaction distance grows with speed. Both parts matter, and the second one is invisible to the driver.
  3. Grip sets the scale. Halve it and the braking distance doubles.

Glossary and sources

Coefficient of friction (μ)
The ratio of friction force to the force pressing surfaces together.
Reaction distance
The distance travelled between seeing a hazard and applying the brakes.
Braking distance
The distance travelled while the brakes slow the vehicle.
ABS
Anti-lock braking system, which prevents wheels locking.
