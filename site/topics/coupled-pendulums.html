Coupled pendulums

In 1665, a physicist confined to his room by illness noticed something odd about two clocks on the wall. However he started them, they ended up swinging in perfect opposition. He called it “an odd kind of sympathy.”

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Join two oscillators with even a weak link and their motion is no longer separate. They have two shared patterns of swinging, “normal modes”, each with its own rate. Start one pendulum swinging and you excite both patterns at once, which drift in and out of step. The energy slowly slides across from one pendulum to the other and back, again and again.

0 · The story

In February 1665, the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens was ill and confined to his room for several days. To pass the time he watched the two pendulum clocks he had hung side by side from the same wooden beam. He noticed something he had never seen before. The two pendulums were swinging in exact opposition, one left as the other went right, and in perfect step.

He tried to spoil it. He started them in step, and in other ways. Within about half an hour they had drifted into the same opposite pattern again. He moved the clocks apart and the effect vanished. He wrote to the Royal Society about “an odd kind of sympathy.” He suspected that tiny, invisible movements of the shared beam were passing little nudges from one clock to the other.

He had discovered one of the most important ideas about oscillators: when two of them are linked, they cannot go on swinging independently. They influence each other, and their joint behaviour is richer than either alone.

1 · The illusion

“Each pendulum just swings on its own”

If you join two identical swings with a loose rope and start one of them, it seems that the first keeps swinging while the second jiggles a little. The rope seems like a minor detail.

What actually happens is stranger and more beautiful. The simulation shows it directly: start one pendulum and watch.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1665Huygens observes two pendulum clocks on a shared beam synchronise in opposite phase, and writes of their “sympathy”.
  2. 1733–34Daniel Bernoulli analyses the swinging of a hanging chain and finds its separate “modes” of vibration, the first calculation of normal modes.
  3. 1800sNormal modes become central to the study of sound, bridges, molecules and circuits.
  4. 2002James Pantaleone shows that a row of metronomes on a shared, movable board fall into step, a classroom classic ever since.

4 · The reveal

Two notes, one slow handover

The coupled pair has two simple ways to swing. In step, the spring between them is never stretched, so they swing at the same rate as a single pendulum. In opposition, the spring is squeezed and stretched, giving an extra restoring force, so they swing slightly faster. Each of these patterns is a “normal mode” with its own rate. Once started, a normal mode keeps its shape: nothing moves from one pendulum to the other.

Starting only the left pendulum is the same as starting both modes at once, half-and-half. The two modes swing at slightly different rates, so they drift out of step with each other. At the start the left pendulum’s motion is the sum of the two, which add. A little later the modes are exactly opposite and cancel on the left but add on the right: the left has stopped and the right is swinging. That is the handover. The same arithmetic produces beats when you add two sounds of nearly the same pitch.

An idea that shows up everywhere

Coupling changes the way a system vibrates. A molecule such as carbon dioxide is three atoms joined by springs, with its own set of normal modes, and the one that bends the molecule is why it absorbs heat radiation. Two nearby guitar strings pass vibration to one another through the bridge. The cells that keep your heart beating influence each other and fall into step.

The surprise

A weak link between two oscillators makes them take turns. The energy doesn’t leak away. It passes back and forth, like a ball being tossed between two players, and the rate of passing is set by how strong the link is.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now carbon dioxide molecules in the air are absorbing heat radiation at the frequencies of their normal modes: the bending mode absorbs infrared at about 15 micrometres, one of the main reasons CO₂ warms the planet. In a wireless charging pad, two coils, one in the pad and one in your phone, are coupled so that energy passes between them. And inside your chest, thousands of pacemaker cells are nudging each other into step, a biological cousin of Huygens’ clocks.

  • Molecules and the greenhouse effectNormal modes decide which infrared frequencies a molecule absorbs.
  • Wireless charging and radio filtersCoupled resonant circuits pass energy between them.
  • Musical instrumentsStrings and the body of a guitar or piano are coupled oscillators.
  • SynchronisationClocks, metronomes, fireflies and heart cells fall into step through weak coupling.
  • Buildings and bridgesEach floor of a building is coupled to the next; engineers compute its normal modes.
  • Quantum physicsTwo coupled quantum states trade energy in the same way, the root of many atomic effects.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The pendulums are assumed to swing through small angles, where the equations are exactly the coupled-spring kind. They are identical, have no friction, and the spring has no weight and always pulls horizontally. Real coupled clocks synchronise because of friction and the shared support, which this model leaves out. The graph shows each pendulum’s share of the energy while ignoring the small energy stored in the spring. The simulation runs at double speed.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Linked oscillators share patterns. Two coupled pendulums have two normal modes, each with its own rate.
  2. Energy slides across. Starting one pendulum excites both modes, which drift apart and hand the energy over.
  3. Stronger link, faster handover. The time depends on the gap between the two rates.

Glossary and sources

Normal mode
A pattern in which all parts of a system oscillate at the same rate and keep their relative sizes.
Coupling
A link that lets two oscillators influence each other.
In phase / anti-phase
Swinging in step / in exactly opposite directions.
Synchronisation
The tendency of coupled oscillators to fall into a common rhythm.
