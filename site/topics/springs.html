OSC01 · Oscillations & waves

Springs and Hooke’s law

In 1676 the physicist Robert Hooke published a puzzle: fourteen scrambled letters. They hid one of the most useful laws in engineering, and he kept the key to himself for two years so that nobody else could claim it.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

A spring pulls back with a force proportional to how far it is stretched: twice the stretch, twice the pull. That one straight-line rule has a surprising consequence: a mass on a spring bounces at a rate set only by the mass and the stiffness, not by how far you pull it. A big bounce takes exactly as long as a small one.

0 · The story

In the seventeenth century, no one had a patent office, and scientists protected their discoveries in a strange way: they published them in code. If you had found something important but weren’t ready to explain it, you could publish a scrambled sentence, an anagram. When someone else announced the same discovery, you could unscramble yours and prove you were first.

In 1676, at the end of a book about telescopes, Robert Hooke printed the following: ceiiinosssttuv. It means nothing. He gave no hint. Two years later, in a lecture on springs, he revealed the answer: ut tensio, sic vis, “as the extension, so the force.”

Hooke had a rival, the Dutch physicist Christiaan Huygens, and the two were racing to build accurate watches. A watch needs something that swings back and forth at a steady rate, and Hooke had worked out that a spring does exactly that. His law is still how we design nearly everything that bounces.

1 · The illusion

“A bigger bounce takes longer”

Picture a spring with a weight hanging on it. Pull the weight down a little and let go, and it bobs up and down. Pull it down much further and let go: it has a much longer way to travel on each bob. It seems obvious that the big bounce must take longer.

It doesn’t. And the reason is Hooke’s law. You can test it in the simulation: pull the weight down by different amounts and time the bounces.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1660Hooke discovers the law of springs, but does not publish it.
  2. 1675Huygens patents a spiral spring to regulate a watch. Hooke protests that he had thought of it first, an argument that lasts years.
  3. 1676Hooke publishes the anagram ceiiinosssttuv to establish priority without revealing the law.
  4. 1678In De Potentia Restitutiva he reveals the answer: ut tensio, sic vis.
  5. 1807Thomas Young introduces a number to describe the stiffness of a material itself, now called Young’s modulus.

3 · The model

A weight hangs from a spring. You choose its mass and the stiffness of the spring. It starts at rest at the position where the pull of the spring balances its weight. Press Pull down and let go to make it bounce. The graph shows the spring’s pull against its stretch, and the white dot follows the bouncing weight.

F = k · x bounce time: T = 2π √(m / k)k is the stiffness (newtons per metre), x the stretch. The bounce time depends on the mass m and stiffness k only. It does not contain the size of the stretch, and it does not contain gravity.

Try this

  1. Look at the graph: it is a straight line. Double the weight and the stretch at rest doubles.
  2. Press Pull down and let go. The weight bounces with a time of 1.26 s, as the formula says. Press it again while the weight is moving, to pull it further down.
  3. The size of the bounce changes, but its timing does not. A big bounce takes the same time as a small one.
  4. Raise the mass: the bounce gets slower. Raise the stiffness: it gets faster. Four times the mass doubles the time.

4 · The reveal

Why the size doesn’t matter

Pull the weight twice as far and two things happen. The distance it has to go on each bob doubles, which would take longer. But the spring’s pull also doubles, because of Hooke’s law, so the weight accelerates twice as hard and reaches speeds twice as high. The extra speed exactly pays for the extra distance. The straight-line law makes the two cancel perfectly, so the time is the same however far you pull.

That is a very special property. It is why springs make good timekeepers: a watch’s balance spring beats at the same rate whether the watch is fully wound (large swings) or nearly run down (small ones).

What sets the time, and what does not

A heavier weight is harder to speed up and so bounces more slowly. A stiffer spring pulls harder for the same stretch and so bounces faster. Gravity doesn’t appear at all: it only shifts where the weight sits at rest. The same weight on the same spring bounces at the same rate on the Moon, or in the free fall of a space station. Astronauts use springs to “weigh” themselves: they measure the oscillation time to find their mass.

The surprise

A straight-line law makes the time of a bounce independent of its size. That one fact, called isochronism, is what turned springs, and later pendulums, into the heart of every mechanical clock.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the accelerometer in your phone is a microscopic version of this chapter: a tiny block of silicon hanging on springs thinner than a hair. When the phone moves, the block lags behind, stretching a spring, and the chip measures the stretch to work out the acceleration. In a mechanical watch on someone’s wrist, a spiral balance spring is pulling a wheel back and forth about four times a second, 28,800 beats an hour, driven by exactly the law Hooke hid in an anagram.

  • Watches and clocksA balance spring oscillates at a steady rate, whatever the size of the swing.
  • Vehicle suspensionSprings carry the car’s weight; their stiffness sets the natural bounce rate.
  • Scales and force gaugesThe stretch of a spring is a direct reading of the force.
  • Phones and sensorsTiny spring-mass systems in silicon detect motion and rotation.
  • MaterialsThe bonds between atoms behave like tiny springs, which is why solids are elastic.
  • Buildings and bridgesEngineers calculate how stiffly a structure bends so that they can predict how it will vibrate.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The spring here is perfectly “linear”: it obeys Hooke’s law at every stretch. Real springs obey it only approximately, up to the proportional limit, and if you stretch them beyond the elastic limit they stay permanently stretched. The spring itself has no mass, and there is a little built-in friction so that the bouncing dies out slowly, as it does in life. The weight only moves up and down: real hanging weights also sway and twist. The scale shows a range of stretch of up to 1.4 m, much longer than ordinary springs.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Twice the stretch, twice the pull. Hooke’s law is a straight line.
  2. Bounce time ignores the size of the bounce. Only mass and stiffness matter: T = 2π√(m/k).
  3. Gravity just moves the middle. It decides where the weight rests, not how fast it bounces.

Glossary and sources

Hooke’s law
The pull of a spring is proportional to its stretch: F = kx.
Stiffness (k)
How hard the spring pulls for each metre of stretch, in newtons per metre.
Elastic limit
The stretch beyond which a material no longer springs back fully.
Period
The time for one full bounce, back to where it started.
