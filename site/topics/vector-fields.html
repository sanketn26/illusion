FND10 · Foundations & method

Vector fields: arrows everywhere

A temperature map gives you a number at every place. A wind map gives you something richer: a direction and a strength at every place. That small upgrade, a map of arrows, is how physics describes gravity, electricity, magnetism, fluids and weather.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

A vector field assigns an arrow to every point in space. To use one, ask what an object placed at that point would feel or do. Follow the arrows and you trace how a leaf drifts, how heat flows, or how a charge is pushed. Every force field you will meet in this project is one of these.

0 · The story

In the 1840s, a US Navy officer named Matthew Fontaine Maury sat among shelves of old ship’s logs, dull records of winds and currents that sailors had jotted down on thousands of voyages. Nobody had ever combined them. Maury did. He sorted them by place and season and drew, ocean by ocean, what the wind and water usually did at each spot.

His charts were not maps of land, nor of depth. They were maps of arrows: here the wind usually blows this way, strongly; there it is weak, and turns. Captains who followed them found faster routes, because the sea has patterns, and the patterns can be written down as an arrow at every point.

That is all a vector field is. The idea is old and simple, and it is the key to almost everything else in physics.

1 · The illusion

“A map assigns a single number to each place”

Most maps we meet give one number per place: temperature, height, rainfall. But many things in physics need more. To know what the wind will do to a kite, a number is not enough. You need to know which way it is blowing and how hard.

The same is true for the pull of gravity, the push on a charge, and the flow of water in a river. In each case, space itself carries an arrow at every point.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1847Matthew Maury publishes his Wind and Current Chart of the North Atlantic, built from thousands of ship’s logs, a map of arrows over the ocean.
  2. 1830s–60sMichael Faraday and James Clerk Maxwell describe magnetism and electricity as fields: arrows filling space around magnets and charges.
  3. 1904Vilhelm Bjerknes proposes that weather can be forecast by solving physics equations for the atmosphere’s wind, pressure and temperature fields.
  4. 1922Lewis Fry Richardson tries it by hand, taking weeks of hand calculation for a single six-hour forecast. The result is wildly wrong, but the method is right.
  5. 1950The first computer weather forecast is run on the ENIAC. Today’s forecasts track billions of arrows on supercomputers.

4 · The reveal

Reading a field

You can learn a lot from the arrows without any equations. Where arrows point out from a spot, something is being created there: a source. Where they point in, it is being drained: a sink. Where they swirl round, there is rotation. Where they come in along one direction and leave along another, there is a saddle, a place where things split apart.

The same four shapes appear in electric fields (a charge is a source or a sink of the field), in the flow of heat, and in the pattern of winds around a storm. The picture tells you what kind of thing is happening before you do any calculation.

Following the arrows

A field is a rule, not a path. But from a rule you can make paths: put something at a point, move it a little in the direction of the arrow, look up the new arrow, and repeat. That is exactly what the leaves do, and how a computer traces the flow of air over a wing. (For a field of force, such as an electric field, the arrow tells you the push, not the path: a charge’s motion also depends on its mass and its speed so far.)

The surprise

A single idea covers a surprising range. The same kind of map, an arrow at every point, describes the wind, the pull of the Earth, the push on an electron, and how heat flows through a spoon. Learn to read it once and you can read them all.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the weather forecast on your phone comes from supercomputers holding a vector field for the whole atmosphere, wind arrows on a grid with billions of points, and stepping it forward in time. Airline flight planners are using the jet stream’s arrows to route flights, which is why a flight from North America to Europe is usually quicker than the way back. And somewhere a robot is planning its way across a room by following arrows that point away from obstacles and towards its goal.

  • Weather and climateForecast models step the global wind field forward in time.
  • Flight planningRoutes are chosen to ride tail winds and avoid head winds in the jet stream.
  • Gravity and electric fieldsThe next chapters describe them as exactly this: an arrow at each point.
  • Aircraft and car designEngineers compute the flow field around a wing or a car body to see where air accelerates or swirls.
  • MedicineBlood-flow imaging and MRI show the flow field inside arteries and the heart.
  • Robotics and gamesCharacters and robots steer by following arrows that point towards goals and away from obstacles.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

These fields are fixed in time, drawn in 2D, and made up to show the basic patterns. Real winds change from moment to moment and move in three dimensions. The leaves are idealised: they have no weight or momentum and simply take the local arrow as their velocity (real leaves lag behind a changing wind). And the colour scale and arrow lengths are arbitrary, so you cannot read real speeds from the picture.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. A field is an arrow at every point. Direction and size, for every place.
  2. Ask what would happen there. The arrow tells you what an object placed at that point would do.
  3. Four shapes cover a lot. Source, sink, whirlpool and saddle show up everywhere.

Glossary and sources

Vector field
A rule that assigns an arrow to every point in space.
Scalar field
A rule that assigns a single number to every point, like temperature.
Streamline
The path a massless leaf would follow in a steady field.
Source / sink
A place where arrows point out from / into.
