OSC16 · Oscillations & waves

Wave packets and group velocity

Drop a pebble into a still pond and watch the ring of ripples spread. Look closely at one ripple: it is born at the back of the group, runs forward through it, and dies at the front. The ring itself advances at only half that speed.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A wave goes on forever, but a clap or a flash of light is a single short event. To make a localised pulse you must add many waves of slightly different wavelength so they cancel everywhere except one place: a wave packet. The packet’s bump moves at the group velocity, which is not always the speed of the crests inside it. The shorter the packet, the wider the mix of wavelengths it needs.

0 · The story

Try it next time you are by a pond. Throw in a stone and watch the expanding ring. It is not a single ripple but a little group of them. If you fix your eye on one crest, you will see it start at the back of the group, speed forward through the other ripples, and fade out as it reaches the front. A new one takes its place at the back. The group, as a whole, creeps outward at about half the speed of its own crests.

Physicists noticed this in the 19th century. William Rowan Hamilton described such a “group velocity” around 1839. George Gabriel Stokes set it as a puzzle for Cambridge students in 1876, and Lord Rayleigh gave it a proper treatment in his Theory of Sound in 1877. The puzzle for them was the same as for the pond-watcher: which of the two speeds is the speed of the wave? And if the answer is both, how can that be?

1 · The illusion

“A wave has one speed”

The previous chapters used one wave speed for everything. A pure sine wave really does have a single speed: you can follow a crest and measure it. But a pure sine wave is infinitely long, with no beginning or end, so it can carry no message and mark no moment.

A clap or a flash is the opposite: a thing that happens here, now, and not elsewhere. How do you build that out of waves that fill all space?

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1753Daniel Bernoulli shows the motion of a string is a sum of simple waves, the first step toward seeing localised disturbances as sums of extended ones.
  2. 1839William Rowan Hamilton introduces the idea of group velocity.
  3. 1876–77Stokes sets group velocity as a Smith’s Prize examination question (1876). Lord Rayleigh gives it a full treatment in The Theory of Sound (1877).
  4. 1914Arnold Sommerfeld and Léon Brillouin show that even where the group velocity looks faster than light, the very front of a signal never travels faster than light.
  5. 1927Werner Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle uses the same arithmetic: a particle confined to a small region must be made of a wide spread of wavelengths.
  6. 1985–1999Donna Strickland and Gérard Mourou invent chirped pulse amplification (1985, Nobel Prize 2018) to make very short intense light pulses; Lene Hau’s team slows light to 17 m/s in a cold atomic gas (1999).

3 · The model

A packet of waves starts at the left and travels right. The teal curve is the packet. The dashed amber line is its envelope, the outline of the group. The red dot rides on one crest and the amber triangle follows the middle of the group. The first slider sets how wide a mix of wavelengths goes into the packet, shown as bars at the bottom. The second slider sets the medium: at 0% every wavelength travels at the same speed, and at 100% the waves behave like deep-water waves.

group velocity vg = dω / dk crest (phase) velocity vp = ω / kω is the wave’s angular frequency and k its wave number (2π divided by the wavelength). For deep water, vg is exactly half of vp. If every wavelength travels equally fast, the two are the same.

Try this

  1. With the medium at 0%, the red crest stays at the middle of the packet all the way across. Crest and group move together.
  2. Slide the medium to 100%. The red dot now races ahead of the packet and leaves it. The packet moves at half the crest speed, just like a pond.
  3. Make the mix of wavelengths narrower (left). The packet gets longer, more like a steady hum. Make it wider and the packet shrinks to a single short burst.
  4. Compare the bars with the length of the packet. A narrow mix gives a long packet; a wide mix, a short one. You cannot have both short and narrow.

4 · The reveal

Cancel everywhere but here

Take two waves of nearly the same wavelength. As in beats, they go in and out of step, so their sum is a series of bumps with gaps between them. Add a third wave of another nearby wavelength and the bumps get narrower and further apart. Add a smooth spread of waves and the repeats move away altogether, leaving a single bump. The waves still fill all space, but they cancel everywhere except where they happen to line up.

This has a price. A bump of length L needs a spread of wavelengths of at least about 1/L in the “waves per metre” scale. A short packet needs a wide spread; a long packet can make do with a narrow one. It cannot be avoided: it is true of sound, of light and of quantum particles, where the same rule is the uncertainty principle.

Two speeds, one wave

The crests move at the speed of the individual waves. The bump moves at the speed at which the waves’ pattern of lining up moves. If all wavelengths travel equally fast, the pattern of lining up and the crests move together. If long waves outrun short ones, as in deep water, the lining-up pattern lags behind the crests. In some materials and for certain colours the group velocity can even exceed that of light without breaking the rules, because the bump is then a distorted smear and not a signal.

The surprise

The speed of a wave’s crests and the speed of the thing the wave carries are different things. For a narrowband packet in an ordinary medium, the speed of the packet’s bump, the group velocity, is the speed of its energy and its message. In strongly dispersive or absorbing media the picture is subtler, and a signal’s front never outruns light.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now an AM radio station is sending a carrier wave of about a million cycles a second whose size rises and falls with the music. The music is the packet’s envelope, and it is the envelope that carries the information. Light pulses carrying your internet traffic through undersea glass fibres travel at their group velocity, about 200,000 km/s, and engineers must keep the pulses from spreading. Ultrashort laser pulses lasting a few femtoseconds are being made in a lab, each one a packet of a wide mix of colours.

  • AM radio and communicationsA steady carrier is shaped by the message; the envelope is the signal.
  • Ultrashort laser pulsesFemtosecond pulses need a very wide band of colours mixed in; they cut eye tissue and fabricate tiny structures.
  • Optical fibrePulses are packets; their group velocity and spreading limit how fast data can go.
  • Sonar and radar pingsA short ping contains a wide mix of frequencies, which sharpens range measurements.
  • Quantum particlesAn electron is described by a packet; its group velocity is the particle’s speed.
  • Music and hearingA short note cannot have a perfectly sharp pitch; a long one can.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The packet is a computer-made sum of 121 pure waves whose sizes follow a smooth bell shape. Time loops every five seconds and the picture covers only a short stretch of one-dimensional space. The “medium” slider blends two simple rules for how wave speed depends on wavelength (all equal, and deep water) to show the difference. In real media, the group velocity is a good guide only if the mix of wavelengths is narrow enough that the packet keeps its shape; for very short packets in a dispersive medium the spreading described in the next chapter takes over. The readout of the packet’s speed follows the highest point of the envelope.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. A short event is built from many waves. Waves of slightly different wavelength cancel everywhere except one place.
  2. Short packet, wide mix. The shorter the packet, the wider the range of wavelengths needed.
  3. The packet has its own speed. The group velocity can differ from the crest speed: in deep water it is half.

Glossary and sources

Wave packet
A localised wave group formed by adding waves of slightly different wavelength.
Envelope
The smooth outline that follows the peaks of a packet.
Phase velocity
The speed of a single crest of a pure wave.
Group velocity
The speed of the envelope of a narrowband packet (one made from a narrow range of wavelengths) that keeps its shape. In most media it is also the speed of the energy, but in strongly dispersive or absorbing media it can differ from the speed of a signal’s front, which never exceeds the speed of light.
Wave number (k)
2π divided by the wavelength: how many radians of the wave fit in a metre.
