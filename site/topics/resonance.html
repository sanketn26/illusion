Resonance

A child’s swing is a pendulum. A small push at the right moment each time adds up until the child is soaring. The same push at a different rhythm does almost nothing. That is resonance, and it explains everything from radios to collapsing bridges.

Reading time ≈ 11 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Every oscillator has a natural rate at which it likes to swing. Push it at that rate and each push arrives just as the swing is going your way, so the energy adds up, and the swing grows enormous, limited only by friction. Push at a different rate and part of each push works against the motion, so the energy does not build up. Resonance turns a small push into a large effect.

0 · The story

On a playground, a mother pushes her child on a swing. At first she pushes at random moments, now too soon, now too late, and the swing barely moves. Then she gets the rhythm: a gentle push every time the swing comes back towards her, always at the same point. With each push the swing goes a little higher. After a dozen pushes, the child is soaring.

Her pushes are no stronger than before. What has changed is their timing. Pushing at the swing’s own rhythm, each push finds the swing moving in the direction of the push, so the work adds up, swing after swing. Push out of rhythm and half your pushes slow the swing instead.

Galileo noticed this four centuries ago: a heavy pendulum can be set swinging by just blowing on it in time with its swing. A very small push, repeated at exactly the right rate, builds a big effect.

1 · The illusion

“A big effect needs a big push”

Everyday experience says that to move something far, you push it hard. Resonance breaks that rule. A tiny push, repeated at the right rate, can move something far more than a single big push ever would.

The catch is the rate. The simulation lets you set the rate of the push and see how big the swing gets, and plots every rate on one picture.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1638Galileo describes how a heavy pendulum can be set swinging by repeated tiny puffs of breath in time with its swing.
  2. 1831The Broughton Suspension Bridge near Manchester collapses when soldiers marching in step set it vibrating. Armies are afterwards ordered to break step on bridges.
  3. 1863Hermann von Helmholtz uses hollow “resonators” tuned to a single note to pick individual tones out of a sound.
  4. 1897Oliver Lodge describes “syntonic” radio, tuning a receiver circuit to the transmitter’s frequency by resonance.
  5. 2000London’s Millennium Bridge opens, wobbles sideways as crowds fall into step, and is closed after two days. It is fitted with dampers and reopens two years later.

3 · The model

A mass on a spring has a natural rate of exactly one swing a second (1 Hz). A pusher (the teal arrow) pushes it back and forth at the rate you choose. The right-hand graph shows how big a swing every pushing rate would produce in the end, and the white dot shows what this simulation has actually reached. It takes a few seconds to build up. The numbers show the swing as a multiple of what a steady push would give.

size of swing = push ÷ √( (1 − r²)² + (2ζr)² ) r = pushing rate ÷ natural rate at r = 1: swing = push ÷ 2ζFor light friction the tallest swing is almost exactly at r = 1 (strictly it is at r = √(1 − 2ζ²), a little lower for heavier friction). ζ measures the friction. At resonance (r = 1) the swing is 1 / (2ζ) times what a steady push would give: with ζ = 0.06 that is about 8 times.

Try this

  1. Start at 0.60 Hz. The swing is small, because the push is not matched to the oscillator’s natural rate: part of each push works against the motion. After a few swings it settles into following the push at 0.60 Hz with a fixed lag. Note the “× a steady push” reading.
  2. Slide the pushing rate towards 1.00 Hz. Each push now arrives with the swing and the amplitude builds up to about eight times larger.
  3. Reduce the friction to 1. The peak becomes tall and sharp: at 1 Hz the swing reaches about 25 times larger. Move the pushing rate by just 0.05 Hz and it falls away.
  4. Raise the friction to 10. The peak flattens out: heavy friction makes resonance weak and broad.

4 · The reveal

Why the pushes add up

The key is timing. A swing’s position and velocity are a quarter-cycle apart: it is moving fastest as it passes the middle, and stopped at the ends. A push gives the most energy when it acts in the direction the swing is already moving. At resonance, the push arrives exactly in step with the velocity, every time, so every push adds energy. Below or above resonance the push is out of step with the motion: after the first few swings the oscillator settles into following the push at the push’s own rate, lagging by a fixed amount, and part of each push now works against the motion, so the average effect is small. (In the first moments you see a mix of the two rates, which dies away with friction.)

The energy cannot grow for ever. Friction removes more of it the faster the swing moves, so the swing settles where the energy put in by each push equals the energy lost to friction. That is why the height of the peak is set by the friction, 1 / (2ζ). Engineers call that number the “quality factor,” Q: a good bell, a good radio circuit, or a good laser cavity has a high Q and a sharp, tall peak.

Helpful and harmful

Resonance is a tool when you want it: a radio picks one station out of thousands by tuning its circuit to that station’s frequency, ignoring the rest. It is a hazard when you don’t: a bridge, building or aircraft wing has natural rates of its own, and any regular push near one of them can build up dangerously.

The surprise

A tiny push can have a huge effect if it is timed to the natural rhythm. And a huge push can have almost none if it is not. When it comes to oscillators, timing matters more than force.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now your phone and every radio nearby are using resonance. Tuning a radio means adjusting a circuit whose natural frequency matches the station’s, so that it responds to that signal and ignores the thousands of others. In a hospital, an MRI scanner is pushing hydrogen nuclei in your body at their resonant frequency, about 64 million times a second in a 1.5-tesla magnet, and listening to the echo. And in Mexico City, the old lake bed that the city sits on still shakes at about a two-second rhythm, the very rate that in the 1985 earthquake made many mid-height buildings sway dangerously.

  • Radio and Wi-Fi tuningA receiver circuit resonates at the chosen channel’s frequency.
  • Musical instrumentsThe body of a guitar or violin resonates, making the string’s sound louder.
  • MRI scannersNuclei absorb energy only at their own resonant frequency.
  • TimekeepingQuartz crystals vibrate at a very sharp resonance, setting the rate of a watch.
  • Earthquake engineeringBuildings are designed so that their natural sway rates avoid the dominant frequencies of ground shaking.
  • Bridges and crowdsFootfalls at about 2 Hz can excite the sideways sway of a footbridge.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is the simplest case: one mass on one spring, pushed by a perfectly regular force. Real structures have many natural rates at once, and their springs are not perfectly linear, so at large swings the natural rate itself shifts. The friction here is simple and proportional to speed. The simulation runs at double speed, and the pictures of the Millennium Bridge, the swing and the radio circuit are explanations, not part of the model. The curve on the right is the exact steady-state answer; the dot takes a few seconds to arrive.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Match the natural rate. Push at the oscillator’s own rhythm and the energy adds up.
  2. Friction sets the peak. The swing is about 1/(2ζ) times a steady push at resonance.
  3. Timing beats force. A small push at the right rate beats a large one at the wrong rate.

Glossary and sources

Resonance
A large response when a system is driven at its natural frequency.
Natural frequency
The rate at which a system oscillates when left alone.
Quality factor (Q)
A measure of how sharp a resonance is, equal to 1/(2ζ).
Driving frequency
The rate of the regular push applied to the system.
