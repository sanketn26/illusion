MEC13 · Motion & forces

Torque and levers

A crowbar slid under a car’s corner lets one person lift something much heavier than they could ever raise by hand. Archimedes is said to have claimed that, given a long enough lever and somewhere to stand, he could move the world.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–9 min

The 30-second version

A force’s power to turn something depends on both how big it is and how far from the pivot it acts. That twisting effect is the torque: force × distance. A lever balances when the twists on its two sides are equal, so a small force far from the pivot can balance a large force close to it.

0 · The story

Try opening a heavy door by pushing on it close to the hinge. It barely moves, and you have to lean on it with all your weight. Now push at the handle, as far from the hinge as it goes. A finger will do.

You are pushing with the same force in both cases. The difference is where you apply it. The further from the hinge, the more effect the same push has. Builders discovered this a long time before anyone wrote it down. Egyptian stone-movers used long wooden levers to shift blocks too heavy to lift. Archimedes, around 250 BCE, was the first to say exactly how much advantage a lever gives.

His answer is one of the neatest rules in physics, and it is still how every wrench, seesaw and crane works.

1 · The illusion

“A small force can’t balance a big one”

If force is all that matters, a 100 N push could never hold up a 1000 N weight. And it can’t, if both act at the same distance from the pivot. But a pivot adds a second ingredient: distance. A weight close to the pivot has little leverage; a push far from it has plenty.

The simulation sets up a seesaw. Your job is to find where a light weight balances a heavy one.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 250 BCEArchimedes, in On the Equilibrium of Planes, proves the law of the lever: two weights balance when they are inversely proportional to their distances from the pivot.
  2. 4th century CEThe mathematician Pappus records the famous boast: “Give me a place to stand and I will move the Earth.”
  3. 1687Pierre Varignon and Isaac Newton extend the idea: the turning effect (“moment”) of a force is the force multiplied by its perpendicular distance from the pivot.
  4. 1884James Thomson, brother of Lord Kelvin, proposes the word torque (from the Latin for “twist”) for the turning effect of a force.

3 · The model

A plank rests on a pivot. On the left, a 40 kg weight sits 2.0 m from the pivot. On the right, you choose the weight and its distance. Each weight twists the plank by its weight × distance. If the left twist is bigger, the left side goes down; if the right is, the right does. When they are equal, the plank balances.

torque = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot balance: m_L · d_L = m_R · d_RTorque is measured in newton-metres (N·m). For weights, the force is mass × 9.81, so equal mass × distance means equal torque.

Try this

  1. Start with 20 kg at 3.0 m. The left side goes down: 40 × 2.0 = 80 beats 20 × 3.0 = 60.
  2. Set the right weight to 40 kg at 2.0 m. The plank levels and the display says Balanced.
  3. Now try 80 kg at 1.0 m. An 80 kg weight balances a 40 kg weight, because it is half the distance.
  4. Finally try 30 kg at 3.0 m. The small weight at the long distance beats the heavier one on the left.

4 · The reveal

Distance is a multiplier

The rule that comes out of the seesaw is simple: the twist is force times distance, and a lever balances when the twists match. A 100 N push at 2 metres makes the same twist as 1000 N at 0.2 metres. The lever has traded distance for force. This is the entire idea behind a crowbar, a wrench, a pair of scissors, a nutcracker and a wheelbarrow.

It isn’t free. When you push the long end of the crowbar down 10 centimetres, the short end rises by only one. The work you put in (force times distance moved) equals the work you get out. A lever can’t give you energy; it only lets you swap a large force over a small distance for a small force over a large one.

Levers that give away strength

Your forearm is a lever with the muscle attached very close to the pivot. To hold a 3 kg load at your hand, the biceps must pull about nine times harder than the load. That seems a poor design, but what you gain is speed and reach: a small movement of the muscle swings the hand a long way, which is why you can throw.

The surprise

Strength and distance can be swapped, but never created. A lever doesn’t make energy, it trades. Every machine, from a screw jack to a bicycle’s gears, is a way of making that trade.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now your forearm is acting as a lever, if you are holding a phone, with a muscle pulling about nine times the phone’s weight. A tower crane rising over a building site is balanced by a huge concrete counterweight on its short arm, so that the long arm can carry a load without toppling over. And every door handle you touch has been put as far from the hinge as it will go for exactly the reason in this chapter.

  • Hand toolsWrenches, crowbars, scissors, nutcrackers and pliers are all levers.
  • Cranes and balancesA counterweight on a short arm balances a load on a long one.
  • The human bodyMuscles and bones form levers, usually trading strength for speed and reach.
  • Doors and handlesHandles go far from the hinge to give maximum torque.
  • VehiclesBrake pedals, gear levers and steering wheels multiply force by leverage.
  • WeighingA beam balance compares two weights by their equal twists.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The plank is weightless and perfectly rigid and the weights are points that sit exactly on it. The pivot has no friction, the plank is stopped at 14° from level as if it hit the ground, and the weights always push straight down, so at this small tilt “distance” simply means distance along the plank. Real planks bend and have their own weight. The animation is damped so that the plank settles quickly.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Torque = force × distance from the pivot. It measures turning power.
  2. Balance means equal torques. A small force far away can match a big force close in.
  3. Levers trade, they don’t create. What you gain in force you lose in distance.

Glossary and sources

Torque
The turning effect of a force: force multiplied by its perpendicular distance from the pivot.
Pivot (fulcrum)
The point a lever turns about.
Mechanical advantage
The ratio of the load force to the effort force; for a lever, the ratio of the two arm lengths.
Moment
Another word for torque, common in engineering.
