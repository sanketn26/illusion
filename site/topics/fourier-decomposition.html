OSC13 · Oscillations & waves

Fourier decomposition

In December 1807 a prefect from the French provinces told the greatest mathematicians of Paris that any shape you like, even one with sharp corners, can be built by adding smooth waves. One of them said it could not be true. He was wrong, and the idea went on to run the modern world.

Reading time ≈ 11 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 9–13 min

The 30-second version

Add enough sine waves of the right sizes and you can build any repeating shape, even a square wave with sharp corners. Each wave contributes one frequency. The list of sizes (with the timing of each wave) is the shape’s spectrum. Going from the shape to the list, or back, is the Fourier transform, and it is behind compression, radio, medical imaging and much more.

0 · The story

On 21 December 1807 Joseph Fourier stood before the Institute of France in Paris to read a memoir on heat. He was a long way from the centre of the scientific world. As a young man he had been part of Napoleon’s expedition to Egypt, and since 1802 he had been the prefect of the Isère département in the Alps, a government administrator who did mathematics in his spare time. He wanted to know how heat spreads along a metal bar.

To solve it he made a bold claim: you can write any starting temperature pattern as a sum of sine waves, including patterns with jumps and corners. The committee, which included Lagrange and Laplace, was not convinced. Lagrange in particular objected that sums of smooth curves could never produce a sharp edge. Fifty years earlier Daniel Bernoulli had made the same claim about vibrating strings, and the great Euler and d’Alembert had rejected it.

Fourier was ignored for years, but he was right, and for a deeper reason than he knew. Whether the claim is believable, and just how a smooth wave can build a corner, is what the simulation shows.

1 · The illusion

“Smooth things can only make smooth things”

A sine wave is as smooth as a curve can be: no corners, no jumps, the gentlest wiggle there is. It seems obvious that adding smooth waves must give another smooth wave, and that a square wave with its sharp vertical edges lies outside their reach.

Add up enough of them, with the right sizes, and the sharp edge appears. The simulation draws each wave as a spinning wheel and chains the wheels together: the height of the tip is the sum.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1753Daniel Bernoulli proposes that a vibrating string can move in a sum of its simple modes. Euler and d’Alembert doubt it can describe every motion.
  2. 1807Fourier presents his memoir on heat to the Paris Institute. Lagrange, among others, objects to the claim that arbitrary shapes can be written as sine series.
  3. 1822Fourier’s book Théorie analytique de la chaleur is published and the method spreads quickly through physics.
  4. 1848–1899Henry Wilbraham (1848) and, independently, Josiah Willard Gibbs (1898–99) explain the overshoot that appears at a jump. Albert Michelson and Samuel Stratton’s 1898 mechanical “harmonic analyser”, a machine with 80 springs, had shown it in metal.
  5. 1965James Cooley and John Tukey publish the fast Fourier transform (FFT), which made the calculation practical on computers. Carl Friedrich Gauss had found a similar trick around 1805 and never published it.

3 · The model

Each spinning wheel is one pure wave. The first (amber) is the biggest and spins slowest. The next spins three times as fast and has a third the radius, then the one five times as fast with a fifth the radius, and so on. The tip of the last wheel traces the white curve to the right: the sum of all of them. Drag the slider to add waves and press the button to switch between a square, a sawtooth and a triangle wave. The bars at the bottom are the recipe.

square wave = (4/π) [ sin x + (1/3) sin 3x + (1/5) sin 5x + (1/7) sin 7x + … ]Only odd multiples appear, each a little smaller than the one before. Other shapes use different recipes: a sawtooth uses every multiple, a triangle uses odd ones that shrink much faster (1/9, 1/25, 1/49 …).

Try this

  1. Start with 1 wave: a plain sine. Add waves one at a time and watch the corners sharpen.
  2. Press Shape to switch to the sawtooth and then the triangle. The triangle needs very few waves. Why? Its recipe shrinks much faster, because a triangle has no jump, only a corner.
  3. Back on the square wave, push to 25 waves. Watch the little bumps near each jump. The overshoot reads about 9% of the jump, and it will not go away.
  4. Look at the recipe bars: every shape has its own, and the sharper the shape, the more slowly the bars shrink.

4 · The reveal

A recipe for any shape

The key is that sine waves of different frequencies are completely independent: you can ask “how much of the 5th wave is in this shape?” and get an answer that does not depend on the others. To find it, you multiply the shape by that wave and average. Wherever the shape and the wave agree, the product is positive and adds up. Wherever they disagree, it cancels. If the shape has no 5th wave in it, the average comes to zero. Do this for every frequency and you have the recipe, the spectrum.

A shape with sharp features needs a long recipe, because only fast wiggles can make fast changes. A smooth shape needs only a few. That is why a square wave needs dozens of waves for crisp corners, and a triangle only a handful.

The stubborn bump

Near a jump the sum always overshoots, by about 9% of the jump, however many waves you add. The bump gets narrower and moves closer to the edge, but its height does not shrink. Mathematicians call it the Gibbs phenomenon. It is not an error in the method. It is what happens when you try to make a vertical edge out of curves, and you can hear it as ringing around sharp edges in compressed images and audio.

The surprise

Any repeating shape, even one with corners and jumps, is a stack of smooth sine waves. Looking at a signal as a list of frequencies instead of a line over time is one of the most useful changes of viewpoint in all of science.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now your Wi-Fi router and phone are sending data by splitting it across many carefully spaced sine waves (OFDM), and they use a fast Fourier transform to put the signal together and take it apart thousands of times a second. The JPEG photos on your phone are stored as lists of how much of each spatial wave a picture contains, with the faint ones thrown away. And an MRI scanner somewhere is measuring the Fourier recipe of a person’s body, then computing the image from it.

  • Compression: JPEG and MP3Store the strong waves, drop the ones you cannot see or hear.
  • Wi-Fi, 4G and 5GData is spread over many sine waves and separated again with an FFT.
  • MRI scannersThe scanner measures spatial frequencies; a Fourier transform turns them into a picture.
  • X-ray crystallographyA crystal’s diffraction spots are set by the Fourier transform of its electron density, but a detector records only their brightness, not their phase, so the phases must be worked out. It revealed the shape of DNA.
  • Equalisers and noise removalSee the spectrum, then turn down the frequencies you do not want.
  • Tide predictionTides are fitted as a sum of waves tied to the Moon and Sun; Kelvin’s 1870s tide-predicting machines added them with pulleys.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The simulation only builds three repeating shapes from a short list of sine waves, using the exact textbook recipes. A real signal such as speech does not repeat forever, so it needs a continuous spread of frequencies, not a neat list. The wheels all start with exact whole-number speeds and fixed starting angles; a general shape also needs a starting offset for each wave. The overshoot readout is measured on a grid of points, so it is slightly rough for large numbers of waves. And the picture hides the deep part of the theorem: the sum converges to the shape only in a particular averaged sense, and at a jump it lands halfway.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Any repeating shape is a sum of sine waves. Even sharp corners, if you add enough of them.
  2. The spectrum is the recipe. It lists each wave’s size and its timing (phase), plus the average level. With both it describes the shape completely; sizes alone are not enough.
  3. Sharp features need many waves. And a jump always overshoots by about 9%: the Gibbs phenomenon.

Glossary and sources

Fourier series
A repeating shape written as a sum of sine and cosine waves of whole-number multiples of a base frequency.
Spectrum
The list of how much of each frequency a signal contains, together with the phase of each.
Fourier transform
The operation that turns a signal over time into its spectrum, or back again.
FFT
The fast Fourier transform, an efficient algorithm for computing it.
Gibbs phenomenon
The persistent overshoot of about 9% of the jump when a sharp edge is built from a finite number of waves.
