OSC12 · Oscillations & waves

Harmonics and timbre

In the 1630s a French friar plucked a string and heard something no one had thought to listen for: faint, higher notes ringing along with the one he had played. Every musical note you have ever heard has a hidden crowd like that inside it.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A real note is never one pure tone. It is a stack of pure tones, the harmonics, at 1×, 2×, 3× … the base frequency. The pitch is the base frequency. The timbre, which is what makes a violin sound like a violin, is how much of each harmonic is in the mix. Same repeat rate, different shape of wave, different sound.

0 · The story

Imagine a violinist and a flautist standing side by side, each playing the A below middle C. Put a tuner in front of either and it reads the same: 220 vibrations a second. Close your eyes and you would never confuse them. One is warm and rough around the edges, the other is airy and smooth.

That puzzled people for a very long time, because pitch seems to be the whole story of a note. In the 1630s Father Marin Mersenne, a Parisian friar who spent years measuring music with strings, weights and stopwatches, noticed something while listening closely to a long plucked string. Along with the main note, he could pick out higher notes sounding faintly above it, an octave and a fifth up, and two octaves and a third up. One string was playing a chord, and nobody had told it to.

What was that crowd of ghost notes, and could it be what makes one instrument sound different from another?

1 · The illusion

“A note is just its pitch”

We name notes by a single letter and talk about them as if each were one thing: an A is an A. A tuner agrees. It reports one number, how many times a second the sound repeats.

But that number says nothing about what is repeating. Two sounds can repeat at exactly the same rate and have completely different wave shapes. The shape is what you hear as the character of the sound, and it is made of harmonics. The simulation lets you build the shape yourself.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1636Marin Mersenne, in Harmonie universelle, reports hearing several higher notes sounding together with a single plucked string.
  2. 1701Joseph Sauveur names the fundamental and its harmonics and proposes the word acoustique for the study of sound.
  3. 1843Georg Ohm argues that the ear sorts a sound into its pure-tone parts, and mostly hears how much of each is there, not how they line up in time.
  4. 1863Hermann von Helmholtz publishes On the Sensations of Tone. He picks harmonics out of real sounds with resonators and builds vowel sounds from electrically driven tuning forks.
  5. 1935The Hammond organ goes on sale. Its nine “drawbars” add harmonics together, so the player mixes the recipe by hand.
  6. 1964Experiments by Saldanha and Corso find that listeners have trouble naming an instrument once the start of its note is cut off. The first fraction of a second carries part of the timbre too.

3 · The model

Each bar is one pure tone. Bar 1, in amber, is the fundamental at 220 Hz. Bar 2 is a tone at 440 Hz, bar 3 at 660 Hz, and so on. The Brightness slider sets how quickly the higher bars fade away. The white curve underneath is what actually reaches your ear: all the tones added together. The dashed lines mark the length of one repeat, and they do not move.

Try this

  1. Move Brightness from 0 to 100%. The wave goes from nearly a smooth sine to a sharp saw. The dashed repeat lines never move.
  2. Press Odd harmonics only. The bars at 2, 4, 6 … vanish and the wave becomes hollow and symmetrical, like a clarinet. Press Play the real sound and press the odd button again while listening.
  3. Drag across the bars to leave only bar 1. You get a pure tone, like a tuning fork. Now add bar 2, then bar 3, and listen as the sound fills out.
  4. Try the strangest one. Drag bar 1 to zero and leave bars 2 to 6. Press play. The fundamental is gone, yet you still hear a note at 220 Hz.

4 · The reveal

The shape of the wave is the character of the sound

A guitar string, a violin string and a piano string are all fixed at both ends, so they can all vibrate in the patterns of a standing wave: one hump, two humps, three humps, and so on. Each pattern has its own frequency, exact multiples of the lowest. A real string vibrates in all of them at once, and how much of each depends on how it is excited. Pluck a string near its middle and the even harmonics are weak, because the middle is a node of the even patterns. Bow it near the end and the higher ones come out strongly. A wooden body then boosts some frequencies more than others.

All of those harmonics repeat together at the base rate, so the combined wave still repeats 220 times a second, but its shape is different. That shape is the timbre. Your ear does the sorting: the snail-shaped cochlea in your inner ear responds to different frequencies at different places along its length, so it hands your brain a spectrum, the bar chart, rather than the wiggly line.

The same idea shapes your voice

Your vocal cords make a buzz rich in harmonics. The shape of your mouth and throat then boosts some frequencies and mutes others. Change the shape and you change which part of the recipe is loud, and that is what separates the vowels. In the classic 1952 measurements by Peterson and Barney, the average male “ee” has strong regions near 270 and 2,300 Hz, while “ah” has them near 730 and 1,090 Hz. Same buzz, different recipe.

The surprise

The pitch you hear does not need the lowest tone to be there. Your brain works out the repeat rate from the spacing of the harmonics. That is why a tiny phone speaker, which cannot produce a deep male voice’s base frequency of about 100 Hz, still sounds like a deep voice.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the speech-recognition system on a phone or smart speaker is turning your words into a picture of how the recipe changes over time, called a spectrogram, and matching it to words. A streaming service is squeezing music by throwing away harmonics your ear would not notice. And your own cochlea, the spiral in each inner ear, is doing the same sorting job in fluid and hair cells.

  • Musical instrumentsPlayers change timbre by changing where and how they excite a string, tube or reed.
  • Speech and singingVowels are different mixes of the same buzz, shaped by the mouth and throat.
  • Synthesisers and organsAdditive synthesis builds a sound by adding harmonics, as the Hammond organ’s drawbars do.
  • Speech recognition and music identificationApps compare the recipe of a sound over time with stored patterns.
  • Small speakers and “virtual bass”Headphones and phones add harmonics of a low note so the brain fills in the missing fundamental.
  • Machine healthEngineers listen to the spectrum of a motor or bearing: a new bar means something has changed.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The simulation uses exactly sixteen harmonics at exact whole-number multiples of 220 Hz and keeps them fixed in time. Real instruments differ in several ways: the harmonics of a piano string are slightly out of tune (stiff strings), bells have partials that are not harmonics at all, and every note has a start, middle and end that change. Real sounds also contain noise (breath, bow scrape). The brightness rule (each harmonic is 1/n to some power) is a convenient shape of my choosing, not a measurement of any instrument. The “real sound” button makes a steady organ-like tone, not a violin.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. A note is a stack of pure tones. The harmonics at 1×, 2×, 3× the base frequency add together.
  2. Pitch is the repeat rate. Timbre is the recipe. Same repeat rate, different mix, different shape, different sound.
  3. The ear and brain work with the recipe. They can even rebuild a missing base tone from the spacing of the rest.

Glossary and sources

Fundamental
The lowest tone in a note; its frequency sets the pitch.
Harmonic
A tone at a whole-number multiple of the fundamental.
Timbre
The quality that distinguishes two sounds of the same pitch and loudness. Set mostly by the harmonic recipe and by how the note starts.
Spectrum
The bar chart of how much of each frequency a sound contains.
Formant
A band of frequencies boosted by the shape of the vocal tract or an instrument’s body.
