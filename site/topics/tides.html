GRV09 · Gravity & orbits

Tides

The Moon pulls the sea towards it, and so you would expect one bulge and one high tide a day. But coasts have two. The answer says as much about stretching as about pulling, and the Moon is drifting away as a result.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

The Moon’s pull is stronger on the near side of the Earth than the far side. Take away the average, which just moves the whole Earth, and what is left stretches the Earth along the Moon’s direction: two bulges, one on each side. As the Earth spins under them, on many coasts you meet two high tides a day (the ideal pattern; some coasts have one a day or a mixture). The Sun adds a smaller pair, which gives spring and neap tides.

0 · The story

At Burntcoat Head, on the Bay of Fundy in Nova Scotia, the sea rises and falls by about sixteen metres, the height of a five-storey building. Twice a day the water retreats so far that boats are left stranded on the mud flats, and then within hours it comes rushing back. People have recorded it for centuries and named the highest tides on Earth.

Everyone knows the cause: the Moon. And it is easy to picture how: the Moon’s gravity pulls the ocean towards it, heaping the water up on the side facing the Moon. One bulge, one high tide per day.

Except that is not what happens. Coasts nearly everywhere have two high tides a day, about twelve and a half hours apart. If the Moon pulled the water towards it, why would there be a second bulge, on the side facing away?

1 · The illusion

“The Moon pulls the water into one bulge”

It sounds so natural that for centuries even clever people believed it. It is also the reason the second bulge looks like a paradox: on the far side, the Moon is pulling the water away from the bulge, not towards it.

The resolution is that tides are not caused by the Moon’s pull but by differences in its pull from place to place.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 325 BCEThe Greek explorer Pytheas, travelling to Britain, is reported to have noticed that tides are linked to the Moon’s phases.
  2. 1632Galileo rejects the Moon as the cause of tides and argues, wrongly, that they come from the sloshing of the oceans as the Earth rotates and orbits.
  3. 1687Newton explains tides as the result of the Moon’s and Sun’s gravity varying across the Earth, giving two bulges.
  4. 1770sPierre-Simon Laplace builds the first dynamic theory of the oceans’ response, explaining why tide times and heights vary from coast to coast.
  5. 1969Apollo 11 leaves a mirror on the Moon. Bouncing lasers off it shows that the Moon is moving away at about 3.8 cm a year, because of the tides it raises.

4 · The reveal

Why a difference, and why two bulges

Gravity pulls on every part of the Earth, but not equally. Because the Moon is only about 60 Earth-radii away, the side of the Earth nearer to it feels a pull about 7% stronger than the far side. The Earth as a whole falls towards the Moon (that is its orbit), and everything on it falls along with it. What the ocean feels is the pull relative to the Earth’s average: slightly more on the near side, slightly less on the far side. Relative to the centre, the near water is pulled towards the Moon and the far water is left behind, as if pulled away from it. Both are stretched outward. The water at the sides is squeezed in.

So there are two bulges, and as the Earth turns once a day under them, every place passes through both. The Moon moves a little in its orbit each day, so the gap between successive high tides is about 12 hours 25 minutes.

Real coasts

This “equilibrium” picture gives only about half a metre of rise in the open ocean. The huge tides in places like the Bay of Fundy come from the shape of the coast and the sea floor, which can funnel and resonate with the tide. That is why tide tables are measured locally and not calculated from the sky.

The surprise

Tides are not caused by the Moon’s pull, but by the way that pull changes across the Earth. And they make the Moon slowly retreat: the tidal bulge is dragged ahead of the Moon by the Earth’s spin, and its pull adds orbital angular momentum to the Moon, which moves it outward (and, in a bigger orbit, more slowly).

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the Moon is about 3.8 centimetres farther from the Earth than it was a year ago, a fact measured by firing lasers at reflectors left by the Apollo astronauts. The same friction is making the Earth’s day longer by about two milliseconds per century. And tidal power stations, such as La Rance in France and Sihwa Lake in South Korea, are using the sea’s rise and fall to produce electricity from the Moon’s pull.

  • Coastal navigation and flood warningHarbours, ports and flood forecasts depend on tide tables.
  • Tidal powerBarrages and turbines generate electricity from the tide’s rise and fall.
  • The Moon’s historyTidal friction has been moving the Moon away since it formed, slowing the Earth’s spin.
  • EcologyTidal zones are rich habitats; many animals time their lives by tides.
  • Other worldsTides heat the moons of Jupiter and Saturn, and may keep oceans liquid under their ice.
  • Earth’s solid bodyThe ground also rises and falls by tens of centimetres each day.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is the simplest “equilibrium” tide on a planet entirely covered by deep ocean, with no continents, no friction and no delay. The bulges in the picture are hugely exaggerated, and the real open-ocean tide is only about half a metre. The Sun and the Moon are in one plane, and the Moon’s distance does not vary. Real tides depend heavily on coastlines and the shape of the sea floor, which is why the picture’s graph is neater than any real tide gauge’s.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Tides come from differences in pull. Not from the pull itself.
  2. Two bulges, so two high tides a day in the ideal picture. Stretched on both sides of the Earth.
  3. Sun and Moon team up or oppose. Spring tides at new and full Moon; neap at the quarters.

Glossary and sources

Tidal force
The difference in gravitational pull across an object.
Spring tide
The extra-large tide when the Sun, Earth and Moon are lined up.
Neap tide
The smaller-than-average tide when the Sun and Moon are at right angles.
Tidal friction
The drag that tidal bulges exert, slowing the Earth’s spin and driving the Moon outward.
