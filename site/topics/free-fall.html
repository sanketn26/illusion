MEC02 · Motion & forces

Free fall: heavy and light together

In 1971 an astronaut stood on the Moon, held a hammer in one hand and a feather in the other, and let go. They landed at exactly the same moment, and a two-thousand-year-old belief fell with them.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

Without air, every object falls with the same acceleration, whatever its mass. A heavier object is pulled harder by gravity, but it is also harder to get moving, and the two effects cancel exactly. Air spoils the race for light, fluffy things. Take the air away and the feather keeps pace with the hammer.

0 · The story

On 2 August 1971, on the dusty floor of the Moon, Apollo 15 commander David Scott held up a geologist’s hammer in his right hand and a falcon’s feather in his left. “Well, in my left hand I have a feather; in my right hand, a hammer,” he said to the cameras watching back on Earth. “And I guess one of the reasons we got here today was because of a gentleman named Galileo, a long time ago, who made a rather significant discovery about falling objects in gravity fields.”

He let go. The two objects fell side by side, the hammer and the feather, and struck the ground at the same instant. “How about that!” said Scott. “Mr Galileo was correct in his findings.”

In ordinary air on Earth you will not see this, because drag gets in the way (in a vacuum chamber the two do fall together). But how did anyone know it was true before there was a Moon to try it on?

1 · The illusion

“Heavier things fall faster”

Everyday experience says so. A brick drops faster than a leaf. A bowling ball hits the ground before a balloon. It was the accepted wisdom for roughly two thousand years, and it was taught by Aristotle, the greatest authority of the ancient world.

The trouble is that the everyday evidence is contaminated: leaves and balloons are slowed by air, not by being light. The experiment is to separate the two effects. The simulation does it with a slider: turn the air down and watch the evidence change.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 350 BCEAristotle teaches that heavier bodies fall faster, in proportion to their weight.
  2. c. 517John Philoponus of Alexandria argues that this is wrong: dropped weights of very different size land almost together.
  3. 1586Simon Stevin and Jan de Groot drop two lead balls, one ten times heavier than the other, from a church tower in Delft. They land together with a single sound.
  4. 1638Galileo, in Two New Sciences, gives a famous argument: tie a heavy stone to a light one. If heavy falls faster, the pair should fall slower than the heavy one alone (the light stone drags) and yet faster (the pair is heavier). Nonsense. The only way out is that they fall at the same rate. (The story that he dropped balls from the Tower of Pisa is a later legend.)
  5. 1687Newton tests pendulums with bobs of gold, silver, lead, glass, sand, salt, wood, water and wheat and finds no difference to about one part in a thousand.
  6. 1971Apollo 15: the hammer and the feather on the Moon.
  7. 2017–22The French MICROSCOPE satellite compares two metal masses in orbit and finds that they fall alike to about one part in 10¹⁵, the most precise test yet.

3 · The model

A hammer (amber) and a feather (teal) are released together from about 17 metres. The Air slider sets how thick the air is. White ticks mark every quarter second of the fall, so you can compare how the two are speeding up.

a = F / m = (m · g) / m = g with air: a = g − (air drag per unit mass)The mass in the weight cancels the mass in “force ÷ mass”. Drag does not cancel: for a feather it is large compared with its tiny weight.

Try this

  1. Start with the air at 100%. The hammer lands first and the feather drifts down more slowly, speeding up less and less.
  2. Drag Air down to 50%, then 20%. The feather keeps catching up.
  3. Set it to none (vacuum) and press Drop again. The two land together and the tick marks are at identical heights.
  4. Compare the tick spacing in vacuum: the gaps get steadily wider, in the 1, 3, 5, 7… pattern from the previous chapter.

4 · The reveal

Weight pulls harder, inertia resists harder

Gravity pulls on a mass m with a force proportional to m: weight = mg. Ten times the mass, ten times the pull. But Newton’s second law says acceleration is force divided by mass. A heavier object is harder to accelerate in exactly the same proportion that it is pulled harder. The mass cancels, and everything accelerates at g, about 9.8 m/s² near the Earth’s surface.

That the two “masses”, the one that is pulled by gravity and the one that resists acceleration, match so exactly is not obvious at all. It could have been a coincidence. It is why Einstein later took it seriously: it suggested that gravity is not an ordinary force but a property of space and time itself (see the equivalence principle).

What air does

Air pushes back harder the faster something moves. The feather is so light that air’s push quickly matches its tiny weight, and it stops speeding up: its terminal velocity. A hammer’s weight is so large that the same push is negligible over a short drop. So on Earth the heavier object often falls faster, but only because the lighter one is being held back by something else.

The surprise

The old belief was not stupid, it was based on real observation. What was missing was the idea that two things were happening at once. Galileo’s step was to ask what would happen with only one of them, gravity, switched on.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now in Bremen, Germany, a 110-metre-tall evacuated tube called the ZARM drop tower gives experiments about 4.7 seconds of genuine free fall, long enough to study flames, fluids and crystals without the effect of weight. Above you, the International Space Station is also in free fall, which is why everything inside floats. And around the world skydivers are using the other half of this chapter: in a belly-down position they stop speeding up at about 55 m/s, around 200 km/h, because air drag has caught up with their weight.

  • Microgravity researchDrop towers, parabolic flights and the ISS make free fall into a laboratory.
  • Skydiving and parachutesEverything about a jump is the balance between weight and air drag.
  • Precision measurementFalling-corner-cube gravimeters measure g to a few parts in a billion, and detect underground structure.
  • Testing relativitySatellites such as MICROSCOPE check whether all materials fall alike.
  • Vehicle and equipment safetyDrop tests set how well a phone, helmet or package survives falling.
  • Astronaut trainingAircraft flying arcs make short bouts of weightlessness, as in free fall.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Drag is modelled very simply, growing with the square of speed with one constant for each object, and ignores shape, tumbling, and the way a real feather flutters. The air is the same density at all heights (it isn’t, but over 17 metres it hardly matters). The fall is shown at half real speed, and both objects start from rest at exactly the same instant. The Moon version is the clean one: there really is no air, which is why the feather behaves.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Without air, everything falls alike. Whatever the mass, a = g.
  2. The mass cancels. Heavier means more pull, and exactly as much more inertia.
  3. Air is the troublemaker. Light, broad things are held back; that is why the old belief seemed true.

Glossary and sources

Free fall
Motion under gravity alone.
Weight
The gravitational force on an object, m × g.
Terminal velocity
The speed at which air drag balances weight, so that speed stops increasing.
Equivalence principle
All objects fall alike; gravity’s effect on motion is independent of what an object is made of.
