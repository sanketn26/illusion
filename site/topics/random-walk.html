THM01 · Heat, entropy & randomness

Random walk vs directed walk

If every step goes in a random direction, how far do you get? Not nowhere, and not far. The answer is a square root, and it quietly runs through physics, finance, and the web.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–12 min

The 30-second version

A walker who picks a fresh random direction each step drifts away only like √n: 100 steps take you about 10 step-lengths from home. A walker with even a small lean in one direction travels like n: 100 steps take you about 100 step-lengths. Steady bias beats randomness, and the gap widens the longer you go.

0 · The story

Take a coin and stand in the middle of a long straight corridor. Heads, step one pace forward. Tails, one pace back. Flip a hundred times.

Where do you expect to be? Most people say “back where I started, more or less”, and some say “who knows.” Both are partly right. You are probably within ten paces of the start, not exactly on it and not a hundred away. And ten is not an accident. It is the square root of a hundred.

That small fact, a distance that grows like a square root, will turn out to describe how perfume crosses a room, how a stock price jitters, and how heat leaves your coffee.

1 · The illusion

Random means “going nowhere”… doesn’t it?

Picture someone leaving a pub with no sense of direction. Every few paces they stumble off at a fresh angle. Intuition offers two opposite verdicts: they will never get anywhere, or they will wander off forever.

Both are half right. The wanderer does drift away from the door, but far more slowly than someone walking with purpose, and in a way you can predict with a single formula. This chapter makes that “slowly” precise, and shows why the same arithmetic explains how perfume crosses a room and how a stock price jitters.

2 · Where the idea came from

A letter to Nature about a drunk man

Gamblers got there first. In the 1650s, Pascal, Fermat and Huygens were already working out the odds of games that move you up or down one coin at a time, which is a one-dimensional random walk in disguise. But the walk became a physics idea in 1905.

  1. 1900Louis Bachelier models price changes on the Paris exchange as a random process, an early random walk for markets, mostly ignored for decades.
  2. 1905 · JulyKarl Pearson writes to Nature asking the “problem of the random walk”: a man walks a fixed distance, turns through any angle, walks again, repeating n times. How likely is it that he ends within a given distance of the start? The name “random walk” comes from this letter.
  3. 1905 · AugustLord Rayleigh replies that he solved the same problem decades earlier for combining sound waves of random phase. Pearson’s reply is the famous line: in open country the most likely place to find a drunken man is somewhere near his starting point.
  4. 1905 · same yearEinstein shows that jittering grains in water (Brownian motion) follow exactly this kind of statistics, with the spread growing as the square root of time.
  5. 1908–09Jean Perrin measures the jitter and uses the result to pin down the size of atoms, turning a toy walk into evidence that atoms exist.

The idea in one line

Many small, independent, unbiased pushes don’t cancel to zero. They leave a spread that grows with the square root of how many there were.

3 · The model

Two walkers, one rule difference

Both tracers start at the centre and take steps of the same length. The teal one chooses any direction at random. The amber one chooses a direction within a cone pointing right, so it is noisy but leaning.

distance from start ≈ step × √n (random)
distance from start ≈ step × n × lean (directed)n = number of steps. “lean” is the average rightward share of each step (about 0.87 here).

  2. Raise Step size. Both walkers speed up, but the amber one pulls away much more.
  3. Watch the amber walker hit the right wall. Real directed motion would keep going; the edge is a limit of the picture, not of the idea.

4 · The reveal

Why the square root?

Each random step is a push in some direction. Some pushes line up and add; others point the opposite way and cancel. Pushes that point opposite are just as likely as pushes that agree, so a long run of agreement is rare. The typical outcome is that they partly cancel.

Counting carefully (square the displacement, which turns the sign-cancelling into something you can add) shows that the squared distance grows in step with n. Take the root and the distance grows as √n. Quadruple the walk and you are only twice as far away.

The directed walker has no such cancellation. Every step contributes the same small bit forward, so distance simply adds up: double the steps, double the distance. Even a gentle lean overtakes pure randomness. After n steps the lean wins once the steady drift outgrows the random spread.

The surprise

One random walk tells you almost nothing. The teal path above could wander left, right or loop. The √n law only appears when you picture many walkers at once. That is why scientists simulate thousands of paths, not one.

5 · Where it shows up

The same walk, different disguises

Happening right now

Right now supercomputers are running billions of random walks to design nuclear reactors, plan radiotherapy doses, and price financial risk. These “Monte Carlo” methods follow imaginary particles, each taking random steps, and average the results, using exactly the √n behaviour from this chapter to know how many walks they need.

  • Diffusion of moleculesPerfume, ink in water, oxygen crossing a membrane. Each molecule is a tiny random walker; the crowd spreads like √time.Next chapter idea: Heat diffusion
  • Brownian motionA pollen grain buffeted by unseen molecules. A collision-based model is planned as episode 01; this chapter’s walker is the simplified cousin.
  • MarketsBachelier’s 1900 insight underlies modern option pricing: short-term price changes behave like random steps. Real markets add fat tails and volatility changes.
  • PolymersA long molecular chain, each link pointing somewhere new, is a random walk in space, and its size grows like √(links).
  • Search and rankingThe “random surfer” behind PageRank clicks links at random; where the walker spends its time ranks the pages.
  • Monte Carlo methodsRandom walks estimate quantities that are too hard to compute directly, from radiation transport to Bayesian statistics (MCMC).

6 · Where the simulation lies

What this toy model leaves out

Honest limits

Steps are all the same length and strictly independent. Real motion has memory, inertia, and variable step sizes. A grain in water isn’t “stepping”: it is pushed continuously by collisions, which is the point of episode 01. The walls clamp the walkers, which no real open-field wanderer would feel. Two walkers are anecdotes; the √n law is a statement about averages over many.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Random isn’t zero. Unbiased steps leave a spread that grows as √n.
  2. Bias beats noise. A steady lean grows as n, so given time it dominates.
  3. Think in crowds. The law lives in the average of many walks, not in any single path.

Glossary and sources

Random walk
A path made of steps whose directions are chosen at random.
Bias / drift
A consistent average push in one direction.
Diffusion
The large-scale spreading that results from many random walkers.
Ensemble
A large collection of independent runs of the same experiment.
