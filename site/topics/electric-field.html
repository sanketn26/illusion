EMG02 · Electricity & magnetism

The electric field

Rub a comb on your hair and it lifts scraps of paper off the table without touching them. Something reaches across empty space. Michael Faraday gave that something a name and a picture, and the picture is still how we think.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A charge doesn’t push other charges directly. It changes the space around it, giving every point an arrow that says how hard, and which way, a small positive charge would be pushed there. That map of arrows is the electric field. Draw lines that follow the arrows and you get Faraday’s pictures: lines start on positive charges, end on negative ones, and pack tightly where the field is strong.

0 · The story

Comb your hair on a dry day. Hold the comb an inch above some scraps of tissue paper. They leap up and cling to it, with nothing between the comb and the paper but air.

This puzzled people for more than two thousand years. The Greeks knew that amber rubbed with fur would pick up feathers (the Greek word for amber is elektron). But how does one object affect another it isn’t touching? Newton, who faced the same riddle with gravity, wrote that it was inconceivable that matter could act on distant matter “without the mediation of something else.”

Michael Faraday, a self-taught experimenter with little mathematics, had the idea that dissolved the problem. Perhaps the “something else” is space itself.

1 · The illusion

“Charges act directly on each other across empty space”

Textbook pictures of two charges with an arrow of force between them suggest each charge somehow knows where the other one is. It is a tempting picture, and it works for calculation. But it leaves the puzzle: how does the information cross the gap?

Faraday’s answer is more physical. The first charge alters the space around it. The second charge responds to what is there, at its own location, and not to the first charge directly. That map of “what is there” is the field.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 600 BCEThales of Miletus is traditionally credited with noticing that rubbed amber attracts light objects.
  2. 1600William Gilbert separates electric attraction from magnetism and coins electricus, from the Greek for amber.
  3. 1747–52Benjamin Franklin introduces positive and negative charge, and shows that lightning is electrical.
  4. 1785Charles-Augustin de Coulomb uses a delicate torsion balance to show that the force between two charges falls with the square of the distance.
  5. 1830s–40sMichael Faraday introduces lines of force: invisible lines filling the space around charges and magnets, which he makes visible with iron filings.
  6. 1861–65James Clerk Maxwell turns Faraday’s pictures into equations, and finds that disturbances in the field travel at the speed of light.

4 · The reveal

What a field picture is telling you

Three rules cover nearly everything you see. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative ones. Lines never cross, because the field has only one direction at each point. And where the lines bunch together, the field is strong. A single charge sends its lines out evenly. A pair makes the familiar dipole pattern, with lines curving from one to the other.

The field is real in a very practical sense. It is there whether or not anything is present to feel it, and it carries energy. A field can even outlive the charge that made it. If you wiggle a charge, the change in the field doesn’t reach far-away points instantly: it travels outward at the speed of light, which is what a radio wave is. That idea, a field as a thing in its own right, is why Faraday’s picture beat the “action at a distance” one.

The surprise

The field is not just a drawing aid. It is a real, energy-carrying thing that fills space. Charges don’t talk to each other. They talk to the field, and the field talks to them.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now your touchscreen is measuring the electric field. A grid of tiny electrodes under the glass maintains a weak field, and your finger, which is conductive, distorts it. The phone finds where the field changed and calls that a touch. In a laser printer, electric fields steer the toner onto the drum, and across every nerve cell in your body, a field of about ten million volts per metre sits across a membrane only a few billionths of a metre thick.

  • TouchscreensCapacitive screens detect how a fingertip distorts the electric field above the glass.
  • Printers and photocopiersToner particles are placed with electric fields.
  • Air and water cleanersElectrostatic precipitators charge particles and pull them out of smoke with a field.
  • Nerves and heartbeatCells use fields across membranes to switch ion channels. ECGs measure the resulting fields at the skin.
  • Spray paintingCharged paint droplets follow the field to coat awkward shapes evenly.
  • Lightning protectionA pointed rod concentrates the field and gives lightning a safe path.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is a 2D drawing of a 3D field. Real field lines spread out in three dimensions, so how closely the lines are drawn here does not measure strength; read strength from the white probe instead. The charges are idealised points with a small smoothing so the field doesn’t blow up, and the numbers are in arbitrary units. Everything is static: nothing moves, and nothing here shows how a changing field ripples outward as a wave.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Charges act on space. Space then acts on other charges. That map is the field.
  2. Lines start on + and end on −. They never cross, and bunch up where the field is strong.
  3. Fields add as arrows. The field of many charges is the sum of the field of each.

Glossary and sources

Electric field
The force per unit positive charge at each point in space.
Field line
A curve that follows the field direction, showing the direction of the force on a small positive charge there (not necessarily the path it would follow).
Dipole
A positive and negative charge close together.
Superposition
The total field is the vector sum of the fields from each charge.
