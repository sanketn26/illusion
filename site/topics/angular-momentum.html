MEC14 · Motion & forces

Angular momentum: the spinning skater

A figure skater starts a slow spin, draws in their arms, and whirls into a blur, with nothing to push on and no one to help. The secret is a conserved quantity that is to rotation what momentum is to straight-line motion.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Spinning things carry angular momentum: how much mass there is, how far it is from the axis, and how fast it turns. With no outside twist, that amount cannot change. So if the skater brings their mass closer to the axis, the spin rate must go up to compensate. Pulling the hands in from full stretch to the chest gives about five times the spin rate.

0 · The story

On the ice, a skater begins to spin with their arms stretched wide, turning about once a second. Slowly, deliberately, they draw their arms in against their chest. The spin quickens. By the time their hands are tight against their body they are turning four or five times a second, a blur of costume. Then they open their arms, and the spin slows again.

Nobody pushed. The ice is nearly frictionless; the skater’s blades are on a tiny patch. Nothing external is giving them extra twist. And yet the spinning is plainly faster. Where did the speed come from?

It came from a quantity that nature keeps constant and that a skater, without knowing the physics, has mastered: angular momentum.

1 · The illusion

“To spin faster, something must push you”

Momentum in a straight line works as expected: to go faster, something has to push you. So it is natural to think the same of spinning. A skater who spins faster must be getting a boost from somewhere.

But for rotation there is a second way to change speed. You can keep the same “amount of spin” and change how it is shared between how fast and how spread out. The simulation lets you slide the skater’s arms and watch the trade.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1609Johannes Kepler announces that a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so it moves fastest when it is closest to the Sun. Nobody yet knows why.
  2. 1687Newton proves, as the very first result in the Principia, that equal areas follow whenever the force points to a fixed centre: it is conservation of angular momentum.
  3. 1750sLeonhard Euler works out the laws of rotation for a solid body, including how a spinning body responds to twist.
  4. 1918Emmy Noether proves that conservation of angular momentum comes from a symmetry of nature: the laws of physics don’t depend on which direction you face.
  5. 1967–68Jocelyn Bell Burnell discovers pulsars, and Thomas Gold explains them: spinning neutron stars, spun up by the collapse of their cores.

3 · The model

You see a skater from above, spinning at a speed set by one rule: the angular momentum, L, stays constant (59.7 in these units). Arms sets how far the hands are from the axis. The bars show the “spread of mass” (moment of inertia), the spin rate, and their product.

angular momentum L = I · ω I = I_body + 2 m r² if L is constant: ω = L / II (moment of inertia) measures how far the mass is from the axis, weighted by distance squared. ω is the spin rate. With no outside twist, L cannot change.

Try this

  1. Start with the arms stretched out. The skater turns about once per second. The “spread of mass” bar is long and the spin bar is short.
  2. Drag Arms down towards 0. The spread of mass shrinks about 5-fold, and the spin rate rises to nearly 5 turns per second.
  3. Look at the white angular momentum bar. It never moves. Everything else may change; this is conserved.
  4. Check the movement energy bar. It rises as the arms come in: the skater is doing work to pull their arms inward.

4 · The reveal

The seesaw of spin

Angular momentum is the product of two things: how spread out the mass is, and how fast it turns. Hold the product fixed and the two are on a seesaw. Bring the mass inward and the spin rate must rise to keep the product the same. Because the “spread” counts the square of distance from the axis, halving the arm radius cuts the arms’ share of it by four. With the body’s share added in, the whole drops about five-fold between arms out and arms in, and the spin rate rises five-fold to match. That is why the effect is so dramatic.

It is also why the energy goes up. The skater has to pull their arms inward against the tendency of their hands to keep moving outward, and that takes effort (see circular motion). The extra movement energy of the spin is exactly the work the skater did.

The same trick everywhere

A diver tucks into a ball to somersault and opens out to slow down for a clean entry. A cat in free fall turns its body by pulling in and extending its legs in turn, landing on its feet while angular momentum stays zero overall. A hurricane spins faster as air is drawn towards its centre. And a star’s core, collapsing from thousands of kilometres across to about twenty, spins up by a factor of tens of thousands, which is why the neutron star at the centre of the Crab Nebula turns about thirty times a second.

The surprise

You can speed up a spin without any outside push, just by reshaping yourself. The conserved quantity is angular momentum, and the shape of the thing decides how that is divided between “how fast” and “how spread out.”

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the International Space Station is using large gyroscopes, “control moment gyroscopes,” to turn itself around without burning any fuel: it tilts the axes of spinning wheels, and the station turns the other way to keep the total fixed. The Crab pulsar, the remains of a star that exploded in 1054, is spinning about thirty times per second. And somewhere a figure skater is pulling in their arms to reach five or six turns a second.

  • Figure skating, diving and gymnasticsAthletes change their spin by changing their shape.
  • Spacecraft and satellitesReaction wheels and control moment gyroscopes turn a spacecraft with no fuel.
  • AstronomyPulsars, planetary orbits and galaxies all obey angular-momentum conservation.
  • WeatherAir drawn inward spins faster, one reason hurricanes and tornadoes get so intense.
  • Cats and animalsA falling cat rotates its body by changing its shape, with zero total angular momentum.
  • Engines and flywheelsA flywheel stores rotational kinetic energy (and carries angular momentum), and smooths out an engine’s pulses.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The skater is modelled as a fixed round body with two point-mass hands on stick-thin arms, and the arms are the only thing that changes. A real skater also tucks their legs and shifts their torso. The ice has no friction, and the skater’s spinning leg is ignored. The display is slowed to a quarter of real speed so the turns don’t blur. The pulsar picture is illustrative: real collapses involve many other changes. The numbers in the bars are in made-up but self-consistent units.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Spin has a conserved amount. Angular momentum = spread of mass × spin rate.
  2. Pull in and speed up. Hands from full stretch to the chest gives about five times the spin rate.
  3. No push needed. Reshaping alone changes the spin rate, though it costs work.

Glossary and sources

Angular momentum (L)
The rotational equivalent of momentum: L = I × ω.
Moment of inertia (I)
How hard something is to spin, set by how far its mass is from the axis.
Angular velocity (ω)
The spin rate, in radians per second.
Torque
A twist that can change angular momentum.
