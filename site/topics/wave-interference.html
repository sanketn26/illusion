OSC10 · Oscillations & waves

Wave interference

Add two disturbances and sometimes you get nothing at all. That one fact turned light from a stream of particles into a wave, and now cancels the engine noise in your headphones.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 5–12 min

The 30-second version

Waves from two sources add wherever they meet. If a peak meets a peak, they reinforce; if a peak meets a trough, they cancel. Which one happens at a point depends only on the difference in distance to the two sources, measured in wavelengths.

0 · The story

You are on a long flight. The cabin roars, a steady, tiring drone from the engines. You put on noise-cancelling headphones and flip the switch. The roar fades to almost nothing.

Here is the odd part. Those headphones don’t block the sound the way earplugs do. They add sound. A tiny microphone listens to the engine noise, and a speaker plays back a carefully made copy of it, upside down.

How can adding more sound make less? That is the question that ended a hundred-year argument about the nature of light.

1 · The illusion

Two disturbances can’t add up to less… can they?

Drop one pebble in a pond and you get ripples. Drop two, and your instinct says: twice the disturbance. Yet along certain lines in the water the surface stays perfectly calm, even though two sets of ripples are crossing it. Adding two things and getting nothing seems like a violation of arithmetic.

It isn’t. A wave isn’t an amount, it is a swing up and down. And swings can be added with signs.

2 · Where the idea came from

The argument about light

  1. 1600sNewton champions light as a stream of particles (“corpuscles”); Huygens argues for waves. Newton’s authority wins for a century.
  2. 1801 – 1803Thomas Young shines light through two narrow openings and sees alternating bright and dark stripes on a screen. Particles landing in two heaps would not do this; overlapping waves would. He explains the stripes with the principle of interference, borrowing the idea from ripples and sound.
  3. 1818Augustin Fresnel develops a full wave theory of light. Simeon Poisson objects that it predicts an absurd bright spot at the centre of a round shadow; François Arago tests it, and the spot is there. The wave theory wins.
  4. 1887Michelson and Morley build an interferometer, splitting a beam and recombining it, to detect motion through the supposed “ether”. Their null result helped open the road to relativity.
  5. 2015LIGO detects gravitational waves with a 4-km interferometer, measuring a change in length thousands of times smaller than a proton by watching two beams cancel or not.

The idea in one line

At every point, add the waves arriving from each source: the result depends on whether they arrive in step or out of step.

4 · The reveal

Where the stillness hides

Pick any point. The wave from the nearer source arrives slightly ahead of the one from the farther source. If the extra distance is a whole number of wavelengths, the two arrive in step: peak with peak, always. If the extra distance is a half wavelength more, they arrive out of step: peak with trough, always.

Those two conditions carve the plane into fans of bright and dark lines, the interference pattern. Because it depends only on distances, the pattern is fixed in space even as the waves themselves move. That is why the cancellation lines are the easiest things to spot: they are the only places where nothing moves.

Energy isn’t destroyed at the dark lines. It is redistributed, so the bright regions are brighter than either source alone would make them.

The surprise

Spacing matters in wavelength units. Move the sources by half a wavelength and every bright place becomes dark. Tiny geometry changes, big pattern changes.

5 · Where it shows up

Cancelling and reinforcing on purpose

Happening right now

Right now the Wi-Fi in your home is full of interference. Signals bounce off walls and furniture and reach your phone along many paths, so there are spots in the room where they cancel and spots where they add. That is why moving a few centimetres can fix a bad connection. Meanwhile the LIGO detectors are running, using the same two-path cancelling trick to sense distortions of space smaller than a thousandth of a proton.

  • Noise-cancelling headphonesA microphone hears the noise, and the speaker plays the inverted wave. At your ear, peak meets trough.
  • Soap bubbles and oil filmsLight reflects from the front and back of a thin film. Their path difference depends on thickness and colour, so different thicknesses show different colours.
  • Anti-reflective coatingsA lens coating is a film of just the right thickness so that reflected light cancels itself.
  • Phased-array radar and 5GMany antennas, each delayed slightly, steer a beam by arranging interference so that signal reinforces in one direction and cancels elsewhere.
  • LIGO and interferometersThe same two-path idea measures lengths to a tiny fraction of an atom’s width.
  • Wi-Fi dead spotsSignals bouncing off walls arrive by different paths and can cancel at some positions. Moving a few centimetres can fix the signal.

6 · Where the simulation lies

What the picture simplifies

Honest limits

This is a 2D scalar wave with a fixed amplitude. Real light is a 3D vector wave, and real water ripples fade with distance and depend on depth, which this model ignores (so does it drop energy loss and reflections from walls). The colours show the instantaneous swing, not a time-averaged intensity, which is what a camera or eye would register. And the sources are idealised points that never run out of energy.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Waves add with signs. Peak + peak is bigger; peak + trough is nothing.
  2. Path difference decides. A whole number of wavelengths reinforces; half-odd numbers cancel.
  3. The pattern stands still while the waves move, so look for what stays flat.

Glossary and sources

Wavelength (λ)
Distance between neighbouring peaks.
Superposition
Where waves overlap, the total is the sum of each wave’s swing.
Constructive / destructive
Interference that increases / decreases the combined swing.
Path difference
The difference in distance from each source to a point.
