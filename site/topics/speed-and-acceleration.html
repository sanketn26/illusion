MEC01 · Motion & forces

Speed versus acceleration

At 900 km/h your coffee sits perfectly still on the tray. On the takeoff run, at a quarter of that speed, you are pressed into your seat. Speed is invisible to your body. What it feels is something else.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

Speed is how fast your position changes. Acceleration is how fast your velocity changes (speeding up, braking or turning; on this chapter’s straight road, how fast your speed changes). They are different things, and you can only feel the second. Motion at constant velocity, however fast, feels like rest. A steady acceleration makes your distance grow with the square of time: twice as long, four times as far.

0 · The story

You are on an airliner, cruising at 11,000 metres. Outside it is moving at about 900 kilometres an hour, fast enough to cross a country in an afternoon. Inside, you pour a cup of coffee. It pours perfectly straight. A pencil dropped on the floor falls straight down. Nothing in the cabin tells you that you are moving at all.

Twenty minutes earlier, on the takeoff run, the aircraft was moving at a quarter of that speed. But you were shoved hard back into your seat. The coffee, had you tried to pour it, would have slid.

Same body, same plane, wildly different feelings. The difference is not speed. It is change of velocity, and giving it its own name, and its own picture, is what turned the study of motion into a science.

1 · The illusion

“Fast is the same as ‘being pushed hard’”

In everyday language, “speed” and “power” and “force” blur together: a fast car feels powerful. But the body feels pushing, not moving. Constant velocity is silent. Only when your velocity changes (speeding up, braking, turning) do you feel it.

To think clearly about motion you have to separate three things: where something is, how fast that is changing, and how fast that is changing. The two cars in the simulation will make it concrete.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1330sAt Merton College, Oxford, a group of scholars known as the Calculators separate speed from its rate of change (along a straight line), and prove the “mean speed theorem”: steady acceleration covers the same distance as steady motion at the average speed.
  2. c. 1350Nicole Oresme in Paris draws it: time along one axis, speed along the other. It is one of the first graphs ever made.
  3. 1604–38Galileo rolls balls down gentle slopes and times them with water clocks. Distances grow as the square of the time, the “odd-number rule” (1, 3, 5, 7…), published in Two New Sciences (1638).
  4. 1680sNewton, with calculus, makes “rate of change” precise. Velocity is the rate of change of position; acceleration is the rate of change of velocity.

3 · The model

Two cars race along a straight road. Car A cruises at a steady 15 m/s (54 km/h). Car B starts from rest and speeds up steadily. You choose how fast. The graphs record each car’s speed and distance as the run goes on.

speed: v = a · t distance: s = ½ · a · t² (steady acceleration from rest)a = acceleration, in metres per second per second (m/s²). For steady motion at speed v: distance = v · t. The run is shown at double speed.

Try this

  1. Watch the speed graph. Car A’s line is flat: it never changes speed. Car B’s is a rising slope. The slope is the acceleration.
  2. Look at the distance graph. A’s line is straight. B’s curves upward, slowly at first, then faster and faster.
  3. Notice the instant B overtakes A (marked on the graph). Is B faster than A at that moment? Yes, much faster: it has already caught up and passed A’s speed.
  4. Raise the acceleration. B overtakes sooner, but at the moment of overtaking it is going exactly twice as fast as A.

4 · The reveal

Slopes and areas

The two graphs hold the whole idea. On a position graph, the slope is the speed. On a speed graph, the slope is the acceleration, and the area under the line is the distance travelled. Oresme’s insight in 1350 was that you could see the distance as an area: for steady acceleration from rest it is a triangle, with area ½ × base × height, and that is where the half in ½at² comes from.

The overtaking moment shows a nice fact. Car B passes A when the two distances are equal, which happens at t = 2v/a. At that moment B’s speed is a × t = 2v, exactly double A’s. To catch a cruiser you have to be going faster than it by the time you get there, because you started behind in speed as well as in position.

What you feel

Your inner ear and the pressure of your seat sense one thing: acceleration. That is why steady motion feels like sitting still, why a smooth lift feels like nothing until it starts or stops, and why the moment you press the accelerator you are pushed back. “g-force” is just acceleration measured in units of the pull of gravity.

The surprise

The most basic quantity we feel, acceleration, has no unit of its own in daily speech. Once you can draw it, as the slope of a speed graph, motion stops being a blur and becomes a shape you can read.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the accelerometer in your phone is measuring acceleration, not speed. It cannot tell if you are standing still or cruising at a steady 900 km/h on a plane: a steady velocity gives no signal. What it measures is the push of its own case on its inner parts, which is about 1 g even when the phone lies on a table (a freely falling phone reads about zero). That steady reading is how it knows which way is down for screen rotation, and the changes in it are what step counters count. A jet airliner on its takeoff run is typically accelerating at about 2 to 3 m/s² for 25 to 40 seconds; an elevator’s start is gentler, around 1 m/s². Formula 1 cars brake at around five times the pull of gravity, which is why the drivers train their necks.

  • Cars and trainsComfort is mostly about acceleration: smooth starts and stops, gentle bends.
  • FlightPilots feel and control “g” by pulling out of dives and in turns.
  • Smartphones and fitness trackersAccelerometers measure motion and detect steps, falls and orientation.
  • Safety designSeats, airbags and crash tests care about peak acceleration, not speed.
  • Space launchRockets limit acceleration so that astronauts and cargo can survive the climb.
  • Amusement ridesRoller coasters are built to deliver controlled changes in acceleration, which is what thrills.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Real cars don’t accelerate steadily: drag grows with speed and engines change gear, so acceleration shrinks as speed rises. Here Car B keeps accelerating at the same rate, and at the highest settings it reaches speeds that no road car could hold for that long. The road is perfectly straight and the run is shown at double speed. The graphs describe motion along one line only, so they leave out turning, which also involves acceleration.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Speed is not acceleration. Speed: how fast position changes. Acceleration: how fast velocity changes, by speed, direction or both.
  2. You feel acceleration only. Constant velocity, however large, feels like rest.
  3. Steady acceleration means distance ∝ t². Twice as long gets you four times as far.

Glossary and sources

Speed
How quickly position changes, in metres per second.
Acceleration
How quickly velocity (speed and direction) changes, in metres per second per second. Along a straight road it is the rate of change of signed speed.
g
The acceleration of free fall at the Earth’s surface, about 9.8 m/s².
Mean speed theorem
Steady acceleration covers the same distance as moving steadily at the average speed.
