GRV03 · Gravity & orbits

Orbits: falling around the Earth

In 1965 an astronaut tried to catch up with an object ahead of him in orbit by firing his thrusters towards it. The object drifted away. The lesson reshaped how every spacecraft meets another.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

An orbit is a fall that keeps missing. Speed up and you climb to a higher, slower orbit with a longer lap, so you fall behind. Slow down and you drop to a faster, shorter one, so you get ahead. To catch something in front of you, you slow down first.

0 · The story

On 3 June 1965, Gemini 4 astronauts Jim McDivitt and Ed White were circling the Earth when McDivitt spotted something ahead of them: the spent upper stage of their own Titan II rocket, tumbling along in nearly the same orbit. It was a perfect chance to test the thing NASA would need for the Moon: meeting another object in space.

McDivitt did what any pilot would do. He pointed his spacecraft at the stage and fired his thrusters to speed up towards it. The stage promptly moved away, sliding up and off and out of reach. He tried again. The same thing. He was burning precious fuel and getting nowhere.

The trouble was not his flying. It was that in orbit, the obvious move is the wrong one. Orbital mechanics was about to teach NASA a lesson, and engineers such as Buzz Aldrin, whose doctoral thesis at MIT was on exactly this problem, were ready to explain it. Six months later, Gemini 6 and 7 met in orbit within a few metres of each other.

1 · The illusion

“Orbiting things have escaped gravity”

Pictures of satellites, with no engines running and no visible support, easily suggest that gravity has let go. Many of us also carry a second idea: to move faster, you push harder in the direction of travel, which sounds obvious.

Neither is true in orbit. Gravity is fully in charge, and “faster” does not mean what it means on the road. The simulation lets you do the experiment McDivitt could not.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1680sNewton’s cannon: fire a shot faster and faster from a mountain and it eventually never lands. An orbit is a fall that keeps missing the Earth.
  2. 1925Walter Hohmann works out the most fuel-efficient way to move between two circular orbits: two short burns joined by a half-ellipse, now called a Hohmann transfer.
  3. 1957Sputnik 1 becomes the first object to orbit the Earth.
  4. 1963Buzz Aldrin completes his MIT doctoral thesis on manned orbital rendezvous; his nickname becomes “Dr. Rendezvous”.
  5. 1965Gemini 4’s failed chase of its rocket stage teaches NASA the counter-intuitive rules. In December, Gemini 6A and 7 rendezvous successfully.

3 · The model

A target and a chaser start side by side on the same circular orbit. At the start, the chaser fires its engine to change its speed by the amount you choose. Then both coast. The picture shows each one’s path, how long a lap takes, and how far ahead or behind the chaser ends up.

circular-orbit speed: v = √(G M / r) lap time: T = 2π r / v ∝ r^(3/2)A bigger orbit is slower, and its lap takes longer, by more than just the extra distance. Speeding up from a circular orbit moves you to a bigger ellipse.

Try this

  1. Choose +10%. The chaser swings out to a bigger loop. Its lap takes about 11 seconds against the target’s 8, so it falls steadily behind.
  2. Choose −10%. The chaser drops to a lower, tighter orbit with a lap of about 6 seconds, and pulls steadily ahead.
  3. With 0% the two stay together. Only a change in speed changes the orbit.
  4. Watch the amber path at the point where the burn happened. That point is always the lowest or the highest of the new orbit, never the middle.

4 · The reveal

Energy decides the orbit

Speeding up adds energy. Energy in a gravity field means you can climb higher before turning back. So after a forward burn the spacecraft swings outwards to a higher point, and as it climbs it slows down, just as a ball thrown upwards does. At the top it is moving slower than the circular speed there. The bigger loop also has a longer lap, from Kepler’s third law. The net effect is that you arrive back at your starting point later than the target does.

Slowing down does the reverse. The spacecraft sinks, speeds up on the way down, and completes its smaller loop in less time. So to catch something that is ahead, you drop below it, gain on it, and later burn again to rise and match its orbit. That is the Hohmann idea.

The four shapes

The speed at a given height sets which curve you follow. At exactly the circular speed, a circle. Faster, an ellipse that extends further out. At 41% above circular speed (√2 times), the orbit becomes a parabola and never returns; this is escape speed, the next chapters’ subject. Beyond that, a hyperbola. They are all “conic sections”, curves you get by slicing a cone, and one law of gravity generates all four.

The surprise

In orbit, your intuition about speed is reversed. Speeding up makes you slower overall; slowing down makes you quicker. The reason is simply that speed trades against height, as with a ball thrown into the air.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the International Space Station is slowly sinking, because a trace of air still reaches it at 400 km up, and every few months its crew or visiting craft must fire thrusters to climb back. Cargo and crew vehicles reach it by exactly the rendezvous method in this chapter, typically in anywhere from a few hours to two days of careful orbit-shaping. And thousands of satellites in orbit adjust their height with small thrusters, raising and lowering orbits to avoid collisions.

  • Space stations and dockingEvery rendezvous is a plan of burns that shapes the chaser’s orbit relative to the target.
  • Satellite constellationsSatellites raise, lower and phase their orbits with small speed changes.
  • Interplanetary travelA transfer to Mars is a Hohmann-style ellipse around the Sun.
  • Space debrisDropping into a lower orbit speeds up a satellite’s re-entry; boosting avoids it.
  • Planning missionsMission designers think in speed changes (“delta-v”), which is the true fuel budget.
  • AstronomyBinary stars and exoplanets follow the same rule: smaller orbits have shorter periods.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Everything is flat (two dimensions), the planet is a smooth ball with no air, and both craft are points. The target and chaser start together and the chaser’s burn is instantaneous and exactly forward. Real burns take time, point in slightly different directions, and lose accuracy, and real orbits are disturbed by the Earth’s bulge, the Moon and the Sun. The planet’s size and the orbit’s scale are not to scale: the picture is shown at an easy-to-watch speed.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Higher orbits are slower. And their laps take longer.
  2. Speed up and you fall behind. Slow down and you get ahead.
  3. To catch something, change the orbit. Not the straight-line distance.

Glossary and sources

Orbit
The path of an object falling freely around another, fast enough to keep missing it.
Apsis
The highest or lowest point of an orbit.
Hohmann transfer
A two-burn move between circular orbits along a half ellipse.
Delta-v
The total change in speed a mission needs, the practical fuel measure.
