FLU06 · Fluids & flight

How airplanes fly

A wing doesn’t hold a plane up by magic or by a clever shape alone. It pushes air downward, and the air pushes back. The shape and the angle just decide how well that goes.

Reading time ≈ 9 minIllustration: qualitative diagramVideo: 5–12 min

The 30-second version

A wing tilted into moving air turns the air downward. By Newton’s third law the air pushes the wing up: that is lift. More tilt gives more lift, up to a point; beyond it the air can no longer follow the wing’s surface and lift collapses. That’s a stall. Lift does not need the engine: a glider is held up the same way.

0 · The story

On 23 July 1983, Air Canada Flight 143, a Boeing 767 with 69 people on board, was cruising over Canada at 41,000 feet when its engines fell silent. The fuel had been loaded in the wrong units. There was none left.

A 767 weighs well over a hundred tonnes. With no thrust at all, it didn’t drop out of the sky. The pilots turned it into a glider and flew on for about seventeen minutes, steering for an old airfield at Gimli, Manitoba. They landed. Everyone survived.

What was holding that airplane up, minute after silent minute, with the engines dead?

1 · The illusion

“It stays up because the engines push it forward”

Engines push a plane forward. Gravity pulls it down. Something else has to push it up, and it isn’t the engine. Proof: a glider with no engine at all stays aloft for hours, and an airliner with failed engines can glide to a landing.

The second illusion is the “official” explanation many of us were taught: air going over the curved top must travel farther, so it must go faster to “catch up” with the air below, and faster air means lower pressure. The pressure part is real. But the “catch up” part is not: the air above the wing doesn’t wait for the air below, and in reality it arrives earlier. A flat plate or even a wing flying upside down can still fly, provided it is tilted into the air.

2 · Where the idea came from

From curious pressure to controlled flight

  1. 1738Daniel Bernoulli publishes Hydrodynamica, relating the speed of a flowing fluid to its pressure: faster flow, lower pressure. It’s true, and it is one half of the story.
  2. 1799 – 1810George Cayley sketches the modern airplane: a fixed wing for lift, separate propulsion for thrust, and a tail for control. Later gliders built to his designs carried a person aloft (1853).
  3. 1891 – 1896Otto Lilienthal flies hundreds of glider flights and publishes measured tables of lift against wing angle.
  4. 1901 – 1903The Wright brothers build a wind tunnel, find that the published lift data was wrong, and design their own wings. In December 1903 they make the first powered, controlled flights.
  5. 1902 – 1906Martin Kutta and Nikolai Zhukovsky show how a wing’s shape creates circulation around it, linking the pressure story and the “turn the air downward” story in a mathematical theory of lift. Around 1904, Ludwig Prandtl’s boundary-layer idea explains why air sticks to a wing and then separates at stall.

The idea in one line

Lift is the reaction to turning the air downward, which can equally be described as a pressure difference between the top and bottom of the wing. Both are true at once.

3 · The model

Four arrows and one slider

This is a qualitative diagram. Drag the wing angle and watch the lift arrow. Weight, thrust and drag stay fixed so you can see what the angle alone does.

Lift ≈ ½ · ρ · v² · S · CL(angle)ρ = air density, v = airspeed, S = wing area, CL = lift coefficient. For a simple wing CL rises almost in a straight line with angle, then falls at the stall.

Try this

  1. Start at 0°: a little lift, and the streamlines hardly bend.
  2. Increase the angle to about 12°. The wing turns the air more and the lift arrow grows.
  3. Push past about 15° into the stall region. The lift arrow turns red and shrinks, even though the wing is tilted more.
  4. Keep in mind: that threshold is typical for this teaching diagram, not a universal number.

4 · The reveal

More angle isn’t always more lift

Tilting a wing deflects more air downward, so lift grows with angle. But the air has to bend around the top surface to stay attached. Past a certain angle it can’t, and the flow separates, leaving a messy wake over the top. The smooth downward turning is lost, lift drops sharply, and drag jumps. That is the stall, and it has nothing to do with the engine stopping.

The v² in the equation explains something else: speed matters enormously. Double the airspeed and you get four times the lift at the same angle. That’s why aircraft need a long runway to reach flying speed, and why they must fly faster when the wings are small or the air is thin.

The surprise

Pilots recover from a stall by lowering the nose, which reduces the angle. The fix for “not enough lift” is less tilt, not more.

5 · Where it shows up

Wings are everywhere

Happening right now

Right now thousands of airliners are in the air, each one held up by exactly the mechanism in this chapter. Pilots watch an “angle of attack” indicator because the stall in the simulation is a real limit; modern jets even have a stick-shaker that warns the crew when the wing is near it. Wind turbines turning in the next county are doing the same trick, using blades as rotating wings.

  • Airliners and glidersWing area, shape and angle are chosen so the plane makes enough lift at takeoff speed without stalling.
  • Wind turbines and propellersEach blade is a rotating wing. Its lift is what turns the rotor, or what pushes the aircraft or boat forward.
  • HelicoptersThe rotor is a spinning wing. Tilting it changes the lift direction, so the aircraft can move sideways.
  • SailingA sail is a wing standing upright. Sailboats can make progress upwind because the sail produces a sideways lift force.
  • Race carsAn upside-down wing makes “downforce,” pressing the car into the road for grip.
  • Birds and dronesFlapping or rotating wings change angle continuously. The same stall limit applies, and they work around it.

6 · Where the simulation lies

What the diagram simplifies

Honest limits

This is a teaching diagram, not a flow solver. The arrow lengths are not measured forces, and the airflow lines are decorative rather than computed. Real lift depends on airspeed, air density, wing geometry, and the state of the flow around the wing, and the stall angle varies between wings and conditions. A complete aircraft also needs control surfaces, a tail, and stability, none of which are shown. Thrust and drag are held fixed, so the picture can’t show flight performance.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Lift comes from turning air downward. Engines supply thrust, not lift.
  2. More tilt gives more lift… until it doesn’t. Past the stall angle the flow detaches.
  3. Speed counts squared. Twice the airspeed means four times the lift.

Glossary and sources

Lift / weight / thrust / drag
The four forces on an aircraft in steady flight.
Angle of attack
The angle between the wing and the oncoming air. The “wing angle” here.
Stall
Sudden loss of lift when airflow separates from the wing’s upper surface.
Circulation
A net swirl of air around a wing, linked mathematically to lift.
