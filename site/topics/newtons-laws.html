MEC04 · Motion & forces

Newton’s laws: force changes motion

Slide a puck on an air-hockey table and it glides on and on. Slide it on carpet and it stops dead. For two thousand years the carpet taught the wrong lesson.

Reading time ≈ 11 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A force doesn’t keep something moving. It changes its motion. With no net force, an object keeps going at the same speed in a straight line. With a net force, its speed changes by force ÷ mass every second. Things stop on Earth not because the push ran out but because friction pushed back.

0 · The story

Picture an air-hockey table. Flick the puck with a finger and it skims across, barely slowing, bouncing off the walls. Now switch off the little fans that blow air up through the surface and flick it again. This time it travels a few centimetres and stops.

Same puck. Same flick. The only difference is how much the surface rubs against it.

For about two thousand years, clever people, including Aristotle himself, watched carts and rocks and arrows and concluded that things come to rest unless something keeps pushing them. It matched every observation in a world full of friction. It was also wrong in a way that took a very particular thought experiment to expose.

1 · The illusion

“Motion needs a continuing push”

Push a shopping trolley and it moves. Stop pushing and it stops. So surely a force is what keeps it going. Aristotle built a whole physics on that: the speed of a body is proportional to the push on it, divided by the resistance.

The trap is that friction is also a force, and an invisible one. When you stop pushing the trolley, something else is still pushing it: the ground, gently, backwards. Remove friction and the story changes completely. The simulation lets you turn friction down to zero.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. c. 350 BCEAristotle: the speed of a body is proportional to the force on it. No force, no motion.
  2. c. 517John Philoponus proposes that a thrower gives a body an “impetus” that keeps it going. Jean Buridan refines it around 1340. Better, but still a thing that is used up.
  3. 1632–38Galileo imagines a ball rolling down one slope and up another. It always reaches the same height. Make the second slope gentler and it rolls farther. Make it flat and it would roll on for ever.
  4. 1644René Descartes states the law of inertia clearly: a body in motion keeps its speed and direction unless something acts on it.
  5. 1687Isaac Newton collects it into three laws in the Principia: (1) inertia, (2) F = ma, (3) every action has an equal and opposite reaction.

3 · The model

A puck sits on ice. Push! gives it a fixed shove (20 newtons for 0.3 seconds). You control the friction of the surface and the mass of the puck. The speed graph shows what happens during and after the push.

net force = mass × acceleration F = m · a (no net force → no change of speed)Friction is a force too, pointing opposite to motion, of size μ·m·g. It makes the puck decelerate by μ·g, whatever its mass.

Try this

  1. With friction on none, press Push!. The speed rises during the push, then stays exactly constant. The distance counter keeps rising. Nobody is pushing it.
  2. Now raise Friction to about 0.15 and push again. After the push ends, the speed drops steadily to zero. The only difference is a force pushing back.
  3. Change Mass from 2 kg to 10 kg with no friction and push. The same shove gives a much smaller speed. That is F = ma: bigger mass, smaller change.
  4. Try mass 10 kg with friction 0.30. The push is not strong enough to overcome friction. The puck doesn’t move at all.

4 · The reveal

Force changes motion; it does not maintain it

Newton’s first law is Galileo’s thought experiment turned into a rule: with no net force, an object’s velocity (speed and direction) does not change. His second law says how much it changes when there is one: the acceleration is the net force divided by the mass. A push of 20 newtons on a 2 kg puck changes its speed by 10 m/s every second. On a 10 kg puck, by 2 m/s every second. The same push, a fifth of the effect.

The shopping trolley is explained too. When you stop pushing, the net force is not zero: friction is still there, pointing backwards, so the trolley decelerates. Remove friction and the same trolley would roll on.

One more surprise: friction doesn’t care about mass

Friction pushes with a force proportional to the weight, μ·m·g. Acceleration is that force divided by the mass, so the mass cancels again, just as it did in free fall: every sliding object slows down at the same rate, μ·g, however heavy it is. A heavy puck given the same push ends up moving more slowly, so it slides a shorter distance. A heavy puck given the same speed would slide exactly as far.

The surprise

Rest is not the natural state of things. Constant motion is just as natural. What needs an explanation is any change, and the explanation is always a force.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now Voyager 1, launched in 1977, is more than 24 billion kilometres from the Sun, travelling at about 17 kilometres per second. Its engines have been silent for decades. It is simply coasting, with nothing to slow it, exactly as Newton’s first law says. Back on Earth, a hybrid or electric car coasting in neutral is losing speed only because of friction and air drag, which is why designers work so hard to reduce them.

  • SpacecraftProbes fire engines briefly to change velocity, then coast for years.
  • TransportEverything from bearings to maglev trains is about reducing friction so that coasting lasts longer.
  • SportIce hockey, curling and bobsleigh all exploit very low friction.
  • SafetySeat belts exist because a car stops quickly, but you, obeying the first law, do not (see impulse).
  • MachinesSizing a motor means working out the force needed for the acceleration you want: F = ma.
  • Everyday intuitionNearly every “it needs a push to keep going” belief is friction in disguise.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Friction here is the simplest possible kind: a constant force, independent of speed, and the same whether the puck is at rest or sliding (real surfaces cling harder when still than when moving). The push is a perfect square pulse. There is no air drag. The track moves past the puck to give a sense of speed. Real motion is also not confined to a line, and real pushes cannot be set to an exact number of newtons.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Force changes motion. With no net force, speed and direction stay the same.
  2. F = ma. Bigger mass, smaller change from the same push.
  3. Friction is a force. Things stop because something pushes back, not because the push ran out.

Glossary and sources

Inertia
The tendency of a body to keep its velocity unless acted on by a net force.
Net force
The sum, as arrows, of all forces acting on a body.
Newton (N)
The force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².
Friction
A force opposing sliding between surfaces in contact.
