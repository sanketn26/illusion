MEC09 · Motion & forces

Impulse: seat belts and airbags

In 1954 a US Air Force doctor strapped himself into a rocket sled, hit 632 miles an hour, and stopped in under two seconds. He wanted to know how much a human body can take. His answer changed every car on the road.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

To stop, you must lose all your momentum, and that amount is fixed. Force × time = change in momentum (the impulse). So the longer the stop takes, the smaller the force. A seat belt, airbag or crumple zone does not remove the crash. It stretches it out, turning a violent blow into a firm push.

0 · The story

On 10 December 1954, in the New Mexico desert, Colonel John Paul Stapp lay strapped into a rocket-powered sled. Behind him was a bank of rockets. A few seconds after ignition he was moving at 632 miles an hour, about 83% of the speed of sound. Then the sled’s scoops hit a water brake and he came to rest in about a second and a half.

His eyes filled with blood; his wrists were broken; he was temporarily blind. But he walked away. Doctors of the time were sure that a human being could not survive more than 18 times the force of gravity. Stapp had just taken about 46.

The lesson was bigger than the stunt. It was not the speed that nearly killed him; it was how quickly the speed had gone. And that is something engineers can design.

1 · The illusion

“A crash is as dangerous as the speed”

People say “it was the sudden stop that killed him,” but are often unclear what that means. Speed alone doesn’t hurt: you are moving at 100 km/h on a plane and nothing happens. What matters is the force in the stop, and that depends on how quickly your speed is removed.

The same speed, the same change of momentum, can be absorbed gently or violently. The simulation makes you choose how.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1687Newton states his second law in terms of “motion” (momentum): the change of motion is proportional to the force and the time it acts. We call force times time the impulse.
  2. 1953John Hetrick receives a US patent for an inflatable cushion to protect car occupants. It is decades ahead of its time.
  3. 1954–55John Paul Stapp’s rocket sled runs show that humans survive far greater decelerations than expected, if properly restrained. He starts the annual Stapp Car Crash Conference.
  4. 1959Nils Bohlin of Volvo introduces the three-point seat belt. Béla Barényi’s crumple zone, patented in 1952, appears on a Mercedes-Benz the same year.
  5. 1981–90sAirbags reach production cars and become mandatory in the United States for new cars by the late 1990s.

3 · The model

A 70 kg person is travelling at the speed you choose and must be brought to rest by a cushion of the thickness you choose. The graph shows the deceleration during the stop. Notice that the shaded area (change in speed) is the same every time.

impulse = F · Δt = m · Δv average force F = m v² / (2 d) (stopping distance d)For constant deceleration, stopping time Δt = 2d / v and deceleration a = v² / (2d). The force is mass times deceleration.

Try this

  1. At 50 km/h with a half-metre cushion the stop takes about 72 ms. The average force is about 13.5 kN, twenty times the body weight.
  2. Shrink the cushion to 5 cm, like hitting a dashboard. The stop takes 7 ms, and the force is ten times larger: nearly 200 times the body weight.
  3. Go back to 50 cm and raise the speed to 100 km/h. Doubling the speed quadruples the force, because the stop must remove four times the energy over the same distance. The shaded area also doubles, because it measures the speed lost.
  4. Now compare cushions at one speed: the graph’s shaded area stays the same. A tall thin bar and a low wide bar contain the same change in momentum.

4 · The reveal

You can’t reduce the impulse. You can stretch it.

To go from 50 km/h to zero, your body must lose a fixed amount of momentum, 70 kg × 13.9 m/s ≈ 970 N·s. There is no way around it. What you can choose is how that impulse is delivered. Divide 970 N·s by 7 milliseconds and you need a force of 135,000 newtons. Divide it by 72 milliseconds and you need 13,500. The person is the same, the crash the same, the force a tenth.

That is all a seat belt (which stretches a little and spreads the load across the strong bones of the chest and pelvis), an airbag (which cushions the head over tens of milliseconds) and a crumple zone (which lets the car’s front crush progressively, so that the cabin stops over half a metre or more) are doing. They are time-extending devices.

The same trick everywhere

A cricket fielder pulls their hands back as they catch a hard ball. A person landing from a jump bends their knees. A parachutist rolls on landing. A boxer rolls with a punch. A foam helmet liner crushes. Every one of these takes the same momentum and delivers it over a longer time.

The surprise

Speed alone doesn’t decide how hard a crash is. The stopping distance does. A car that crumples over a metre is kinder than one that stops over five centimetres, even if both are going the same speed.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now every car you can see parked outside is carrying a stack of time-stretching devices: belts, bags, crumple zones and head restraints, all tuned by crash tests run in laboratories every day. Federal estimates credit seat belts with saving on the order of fifteen thousand lives a year in the United States alone. And every time you bend your knees getting off a step, you are doing the same thing with your own legs.

  • CarsCrumple zones, seat belts, airbags and padded dashboards are designed from the impulse equation.
  • Helmets and sports padsFoam liners crush over a few centimetres to lengthen an impact.
  • PackagingFoam and air cushions protect fragile goods by extending the time of a drop’s stop.
  • AthleticsLanding mats, soft knees and shoe cushioning all work the same way.
  • Aircraft and spacecraftEnergy-absorbing seats and landing gear; capsules land with crushable structures.
  • Crash investigationImpulse links crash damage and speed change for reconstruction.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The deceleration here is perfectly constant (a flat bar), but real crashes are spiky. Peak forces are well above the averages shown. The person is a 70 kg point, so the model says nothing about which body part takes the load or how long the human body can tolerate it, which depends on direction, duration, body part and the person. “Stopping distance” bundles belt stretch, crush and bag together. The averages here are for explanation, not a prediction of injury.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Impulse = force × time = change in momentum. The change is fixed by the speed and mass.
  2. Longer stop, smaller force. Ten times the distance, a tenth of the force.
  3. Safety devices stretch time. Belts, bags, crumple zones, foam and bent knees all do the same job.

Glossary and sources

Impulse
Force multiplied by the time it acts; equal to the change in momentum.
Crumple zone
A part of a vehicle designed to deform progressively in a crash.
g
The pull of gravity; a deceleration of 10 g is ten times it.
Average force
The total impulse divided by the duration of the stop.
