MEC11 · Motion & forces

Energy conservation: the roller coaster

On almost every roller coaster, the first hill is the highest. It is not an accident or a design quirk. It is the most important rule in physics, written in steel.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

Energy can be passed between forms, height energy, movement energy and heat, but the total never changes. A coaster car carried up the first hill by a chain has all its energy stored in height. Every later hill must be lower, because friction only ever turns some of it into heat. Energy isn’t used up, it is moved.

0 · The story

The ride begins with a long, slow climb. A chain clanks you up a steep slope, tooth by tooth, higher and higher. It is the dullest part of the ride and the most important. When you reach the top, a motor has done all the work it will ever do. Everything that follows, every drop, every swoop, every whoop of the crowd, is paid for out of the height you were hauled to.

Look at the track ahead. Every following hill is lower than the first. Any engineer can tell you why: the car can never rise above where it started, because it has nothing to lift it except what it already has.

This is the principle behind the lowest of the low and the highest of the high. It is called conservation of energy, and a coaster is its cleanest picture.

1 · The illusion

“Energy gets used up”

In everyday talk, energy runs out: we “use up” petrol, we are “out of energy,” a battery goes “flat.” It sounds as if energy is a thing that is consumed and then gone. And at the end of a coaster ride, when the car has slowed to a stop, the energy it had at the top seems to have vanished.

It hasn’t. It has gone into places we don’t easily see: tiny amounts of heat in the wheels, the rails and the air. Add that in, and the books balance exactly. The simulation lets you track it.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1638Galileo notes that a pendulum released from a height swings up to the same height on the other side, even if a peg catches the string partway.
  2. 1686Gottfried Leibniz argues that the quantity mass × speed² (“vis viva”) is what a moving body carries and what is conserved in perfectly bouncy collisions.
  3. 1740Émilie du Châtelet, building on dropped-ball experiments in clay by Willem ’s Gravesande, shows that the dent depends on speed squared, not speed, and champions Leibniz’s view in her Institutions de physique.
  4. 1807Thomas Young introduces the word energy in its modern scientific sense.
  5. 1843–47James Joule uses a falling weight to turn a paddle wheel in water, and measures the heating precisely. Helmholtz states the general law in 1847: the total of all forms of energy is constant.

3 · The model

A 500 kg car is released from rest at a height you choose on the track. The long bar at the top is always full: height energy, movement energy and heat add up to the same total. The dashed line shows the release height. Add friction and watch heat grow.

½ · m · v² + m · g · h + (heat) = constant speed from a drop: v = √(2 g Δh)A drop of Δh turns height energy into movement energy. With no friction, speed depends only on the height drop, not on the shape of the path.

Try this

  1. Release the car at 28 m with no friction. It swoops down and rises to the dashed line on the far side again, never above it. Watch the bar: amber and teal trade places.
  2. Lower the release height to about 20 m. The car swings back and forth in the valley and never makes it over the big middle hill. It doesn’t have the energy.
  3. Add friction. Now each swing is a little lower, and the red heat segment slowly grows. The total is still the same.
  4. Note the speed whenever the car passes a given height. With no friction it is always the same, whichever hill it is on or which direction it’s going.

4 · The reveal

The ledger

Think of the energy in the car as money in three pockets. At the top of the first hill, all of it is in the “height” pocket. As the car drops, money flows steadily out of height and into movement: the car speeds up. At the bottom of a valley, the height pocket is nearly empty and movement is full. Climbing again moves it back. A frictionless car would do this for ever, and could never rise above its starting point, since that would need more money than the account holds.

Friction doesn’t destroy energy. It quietly moves a little from the movement pocket to a third one, heat, which spreads into the wheels, track and air and can’t be taken back. After many hills, almost all the energy has gone there. That is why the ride ends.

Why only the drop matters

Height energy depends only on how high you are, not how you got there. So the speed you gain falling from a given height is the same down a steep slope, a gentle curve or a flight of steps (though the time taken differs greatly). It is one reason engineers can design the whole ride from a plain height-versus-position profile.

The surprise

Nothing is ever used up. What we call “using energy” is really moving it somewhere we can’t get it back from, usually into heat. The law is not a restriction. It is a guarantee that the books always balance.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now power stations in many countries are running huge versions of this chapter. In pumped-storage hydropower plants, spare electricity is used to push water up a mountain into a reservoir, and later the water is let down through turbines to make it back. Pumped storage accounts for the great majority of the world’s grid-scale energy storage. In electric cars, regenerative braking turns movement energy back into stored electricity instead of heat.

  • Roller coasters and ridesThe profile is a height-energy budget: every hill after the first is lower.
  • Hydropower and pumped storageWater held high is stored energy; turbines turn it into electricity.
  • Regenerative brakingElectric and hybrid vehicles recover movement energy that brakes would waste as heat.
  • Pendulum clocks and swingsEnergy sloshes between height and movement, with a little added back each swing to replace friction.
  • Skiing and cyclingEvery descent buys speed with height; every climb pays for it.
  • Energy accountingBuildings, engines and power grids are designed by adding up where every joule went.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The car is a single point following a fixed track, with a simple constant friction force and no air drag, wheel spin, loops or bumps. Real coasters also have brakes and sometimes launches. The energies are for a 500 kg car, in kilojoules. The track shape is made up for the picture, with tall walls at each end to keep the car in. The three-pocket picture is an accounting convention: “heat” here means all energy that ends up as random motion of atoms.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Energy is conserved. Height + movement + heat is a constant.
  2. You can’t go higher than you started. Not without something adding energy: a chain, a motor, a launch.
  3. “Used up” means turned into heat. It spreads out and can’t easily be recovered.

Glossary and sources

Kinetic energy
Energy of motion, ½ m v².
Gravitational potential energy
Energy stored by height, m g h.
Conservation of energy
The total of all forms of energy in an isolated system never changes.
Joule (J)
The unit of energy: the work done by a force of one newton over one metre.
