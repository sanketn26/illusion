OSC15 · Oscillations & waves

The Doppler effect

An ambulance races past, siren wailing, and as it goes the note drops: “neee-yowww.” The driver hears one steady note the whole way. So who is right, and where does the change come from?

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

When a source moves toward you, the waves it sends arrive closer together and you hear a higher pitch. When it moves away, they arrive stretched out and the pitch is lower. The source’s own frequency never changes; only the spacing of the waves reaching you does. The effect works for sound, light, radar and any other wave.

0 · The story

In 1842 Christian Doppler, a young professor in Prague, read a paper to the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences with a title about the coloured light of double stars. His idea was that a wave source moving toward an observer should look different from one moving away, because the waves reach the observer at a different rate. He suggested that the colours of stars come from their motion. That part turned out to be mostly wrong, because stars move far too slowly compared with light.

But the central idea was right, and sound was the quickest way to test it. In June 1845 the Dutch meteorologist Christophorus Buys Ballot organised a test on the railway near Utrecht. An open carriage hauled by a locomotive carried a group of trumpeters sounding one steady note. Along the track stood listeners with perfect pitch, writing down what they heard as the train sped toward them and away. The note was higher on the way in and lower on the way out, just as Doppler had predicted.

That train of trumpeters is the siren scene, a hundred and eighty years earlier.

1 · The illusion

“The siren changes as it passes”

We hear the pitch fall, so we say the siren’s pitch fell. It feels like a fact about the siren. But inside the ambulance the sound is steady. Nothing about the source changes. What changes is the relationship between source and listener, and so how often the crests reach your ear.

The simulation shows that directly: a steady 700 Hz siren, circles for each crest, and a graph of what a listener hears.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1842Christian Doppler proposes that motion changes the frequency of waves received, in a paper on the colour of double stars.
  2. 1845Buys Ballot tests it with trumpeters on a moving train near Utrecht and confirms it for sound.
  3. 1848Hippolyte Fizeau points out that for light the effect should shift the lines in a star’s spectrum, and so can measure its speed.
  4. 1868William Huggins uses the shift of spectral lines to measure the speed of the star Sirius along our line of sight.
  5. 1957After Sputnik’s launch, William Guier and George Weiffenbach at Johns Hopkins track its radio beeps by their Doppler shift. This leads to the first satellite navigation system.
  6. 1973–1995Doppler weather radar first sees the rotation inside a tornado-producing storm (1973), and Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz find the first planet around a Sun-like star from the Doppler wobble of its light (1995).

3 · The model

A siren drives along a road, sending out one wave crest every half second. Each crest spreads out as a circle at the speed of sound, centred on where the siren was when it was sent. You stand beside the road. The graph underneath is what you hear: a pitch computed from how often crests actually reach you. Siren speed is given as a fraction of the speed of sound.

pitch heard = siren pitch ÷ (1 − (v / c) × cos θ)v is the siren’s speed, c the speed of sound, and θ the angle between the siren’s direction of travel and the line from it to you. Heading straight at you, cos θ = 1; heading away, −1; passing at its closest, 0.

Try this

  1. With the speed at 0.4, watch the circles bunch up in front of the siren and spread out behind. The amber line shows a high note falling to a low one.
  2. Drop the speed to 0. The circles become evenly spaced rings and the pitch is a flat 700 Hz.
  3. Raise the speed toward 0.9. The circles ahead crowd together and the pitch jump gets enormous.
  4. Go beyond 1.0. The circles can no longer keep ahead of the siren, and they pile into a cone. The cone sweeps over a listener as a sudden bang, a sonic boom, rather than as a sliding pitch. (The cone here is drawn from simple circles; a real shock wave is a sharp jump in pressure.)

4 · The reveal

It is all about spacing

Think of someone throwing tennis balls at you at a steady rate, one a second. If they stand still, you catch one a second. If they run toward you while throwing, each ball starts a little closer than the last, so it arrives sooner after the previous one. You catch more than one a second. If they run away, each ball has farther to go, and you catch fewer. The throwing rate never changed. The siren does exactly this with wave crests.

For an ambulance at 25 m/s (about 90 km/h), sending 700 Hz, you hear about 755 Hz as it comes straight at you and about 652 Hz as it moves straight away, a drop of roughly two and a half semitones. It does not jump between them, because the angle between its path and you sweeps smoothly from head-on, through sideways, to directly away. The graph shows why a passing siren slides.

When the source overtakes its own waves

At exactly the speed of sound, the siren keeps pace with its own crests and they stack up in front of it. Faster still, it outruns them, and the crests overlap into a cone-shaped shock front. Everything on the cone reaches you at once: a sonic boom. Chuck Yeager’s Bell X-1 first made one on purpose on 14 October 1947.

The surprise

The source never changes. Whether you hear a high note, a low note or a bang depends only on how the crests are spaced by the time they reach you. The same idea measures the speed of a distant star, a storm, a baseball and the blood in your artery.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now a GPS receiver is correcting for the Doppler shift of each satellite signal. Those satellites move at nearly 4 km/s, which shifts the 1.5 GHz signal by up to about 5 kHz. A police radar gun or a weather radar is comparing the frequency of a reflected pulse with the one sent. And astronomers are tracking the tiny back-and-forth wobble in a distant star’s light, a few metres per second, to find planets orbiting it.

  • Police radar and speed camerasThe reflected signal’s frequency shift gives the speed.
  • Weather radarThe shift in echoes from raindrops shows wind toward or away from the radar, and reveals rotation inside storms.
  • Medical ultrasoundDoppler scans show the speed and direction of blood flow.
  • AstronomyShifts in starlight reveal the motion of stars, the wobble caused by planets and the spin of galaxies.
  • Satellite navigation and trackingDoppler shift was the basis of the first satellite navigation system, Transit, and is still part of tracking spacecraft.
  • Supersonic flightThe cone that forms when a source outruns its own waves is the sonic boom.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The speeds are exaggerated so the circles are visible: a real ambulance moves at about 0.07 of the speed of sound, not 0.4. The crests are drawn as circles in a flat plane; real sound spreads as spheres and gets quieter with distance, which the simulation ignores, so it shows pitch only and not loudness. The air is perfectly still (wind would change the answer), the source moves at a constant speed and the listener stays put. The pitch is calculated from the arrival times of successive crests, so it updates twice a second. For light the effect has the same idea but a different formula, with relativity included.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. The source’s pitch never changes. What changes is how closely the crests are spaced when they reach you.
  2. Toward: higher. Away: lower. A passing siren slides smoothly because the angle changes smoothly.
  3. Past the speed of sound, the rule changes. The crests pile into a cone, and its passage is heard as a boom.

Glossary and sources

Doppler shift
The change in received frequency caused by relative motion of source and observer.
Wavefront
A surface joining all the points of a wave that are at the same stage, such as all the crests sent at one instant.
Mach number
A speed divided by the speed of sound; above 1 is supersonic.
Shock cone (Mach cone)
The cone formed by the crests of a source moving faster than its own waves.
