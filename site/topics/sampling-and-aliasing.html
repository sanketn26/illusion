Sampling and aliasing

A stagecoach thunders across the screen at full gallop, the horses a blur, the driver whipping, and its wheels slowly turning backward. In 1824 a London doctor named Peter Mark Roget, the man who later made the thesaurus, saw something like it through a picket fence.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Moderate (M)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

A film or a digital recording is a series of snapshots. If something changes faster than half the snapshot rate, the snapshots can no longer tell the fast change from a slow one, and the record shows a false, slower motion: aliasing. A fast wheel looks like it is turning backward; a high note on a recording turns into a lower one. The fix is to sample at least twice as fast as the fastest thing you want to keep, or to remove the fast things before sampling.

0 · The story

Peter Mark Roget was a London physician and, in his spare time, a keen observer. He noticed that when he looked through a fence of vertical bars at a carriage wheel rolling past, the spokes seemed to stand still, or bend, or even turn the wrong way. The fence let him see the wheel only in brief slices, a flicker of glimpses. In 1824 he reported to the Royal Society that the wheel was being seen at intervals, not continuously. His explanation, that the eye was getting snapshots, was right, and it is one of the earliest descriptions of what we now call aliasing.

A hundred years later the movies made the effect famous. A film camera takes 24 snapshots every second. A spoked wheel at the right speed will turn by almost, but not quite, one spoke’s gap between pictures. The spoke that has moved on looks the same as the one it replaced, and a little behind it, so the eye sees the wheel creeping backward. Nothing is wrong with the camera or the wheel. The snapshots simply do not contain enough information to tell the difference.

1 · The illusion

“Enough snapshots always show the truth”

If you took a photograph of everything that happened, you would of course see everything. But we never record everything. We sample: frames at 24 a second, sound at 44,100 numbers a second. It seems that as long as the snapshots are quick, nothing is lost. The question is how quick is quick enough, and what happens when it is not.

What happens is worse than blur. The record shows something calm and coherent that never occurred. The simulation lets you spin a wheel and film it.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1824–25Peter Mark Roget describes how a wheel seen through the gaps of a fence appears to behave strangely; he correctly blames the glimpses.
  2. 1831–32Michael Faraday studies the effect with rotating toothed wheels. Joseph Plateau’s phenakistoscope (1832), the first animation toy, uses the same trick.
  3. 1915–1933E. T. Whittaker (1915) and Vladimir Kotelnikov (1933) show that a signal can be rebuilt exactly from snapshots taken at more than twice its fastest rate; Harry Nyquist (1928) shows the same limit on telegraph signalling.
  4. 1926–1930Sound movies make 24 frames a second the film standard.
  5. 1949Claude Shannon gives the sampling theorem its place at the heart of communication theory.
  6. 1982The compact disc stores music at 44,100 samples a second, a little over twice the 20,000 Hz limit of human hearing.

3 · The model

In each frame the eye assumes each spoke moved to the nearest new spoke, the shortest hop (amber arc) rather than the real, longer turn (red dashes). The graph underneath is the same story as a wave: how often a spoke passes the top (teal), the snapshots (amber dots), and the slow wave that fits all of them (red).

  2. Raise the speed slowly toward 3 turns/s. Past 1.5 turns/s, the wheel on the right begins to turn backward. At 3, it stops dead.
  3. Press Find the frozen speed. The wheel on the left is spinning at full gallop but the camera shows it standing still.
  4. Raise the camera’s frames per second to 60. The backward turning vanishes at speeds that were wrong before: faster snapshots push the false effect to higher speeds.

4 · The reveal

Why too few snapshots lie

Between two snapshots you see nothing. So when you see the second picture, you have to guess what happened between. The simple, natural guess is the smallest change that could explain it, the nearest spoke. That guess is right as long as the real change was smaller than half the gap between spokes. If the wheel really moved more than half a gap, the guess is wrong, and the wheel appears to move the other way, or too slowly, or not at all.

A spoked wheel is a special case, because every spoke looks the same, so the pattern repeats eight times a turn. In the language of waves, the wheel makes a wave that repeats once per spoke gap, and the camera samples it 24 times a second. Any wave faster than 12 per second is too fast to be sampled safely. Its snapshots fall on the same dots as a slower wave, and the slower one is the one the recording keeps.

The same trap in sound, pictures and scanners

Digital audio takes tens of thousands of snapshots of air pressure a second. A tone too high for the sample rate would fold down to a false lower note, so recorders filter out everything above half the sample rate before sampling. For a CD, a 30,000 Hz tone, if it were not filtered, would reappear as 14,100 Hz. In pictures, a tight pattern such as a striped shirt, finer than the camera’s pixels, produces false wavy bands called moiré. In an MRI scanner, aliasing can fold the far side of a patient’s body onto the other side of the image.

The surprise

A recording cannot show you what it missed. It shows you a different, slower thing that fits the same snapshots. The only cure is to sample at more than twice the fastest rate, or to remove the fast part first.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the microphone in your phone is measuring air pressure 48,000 times a second, after an electronic filter has removed everything above 24,000 Hz so that nothing folds back as a false note. The screen in front of you is drawing 60 or more pictures a second, and a camera pointed at a helicopter somewhere is recording rotor blades that look frozen. And an MRI scanner is covering its field of view with enough samples to keep the picture from wrapping around.

  • Digital audioCD and phone audio sample at 44,100 or 48,000 times a second and filter out anything higher before sampling.
  • Film, video and gamesFrame rate limits the fastest motion shown truthfully; graphics cards smooth jagged edges with extra samples (anti-aliasing).
  • Cameras and scannersOptical filters blur a picture just enough that fine patterns cannot form false moiré bands.
  • Oscilloscopes and measuring instrumentsA signal must be sampled at more than twice its fastest component.
  • Medical imagingMRI and ultrasound must sample finely enough to avoid wrap-around and false patterns.
  • Strobe tricksA strobe light flashing at the right rate makes a fast motion look slow or still: a deliberate use of the same effect.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The camera in the simulation takes perfectly instant snapshots at exactly regular times. A real film or video camera keeps its shutter open for part of the frame and blurs fast motion, which softens but does not remove the effect. The rule “the eye picks the nearest spoke” is a simple model of perception; real viewers sometimes see other motion. The wheel turns at a perfectly steady rate with eight identical spokes. The real wheel on the left is drawn 20 times slower because a screen drawing 60 pictures a second cannot show a wheel at 3 turns a second without aliasing on its own. The graph shows only a half-second window.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Recording means sampling. We keep snapshots, and nothing about what happened between them.
  2. Keep it under half the rate. Anything changing faster than half the sampling rate appears as something slower.
  3. False motion is convincing. An alias looks like a real slow signal, so it cannot be removed afterwards. Filter first, then sample.

Glossary and sources

Sampling
Measuring a quantity at regular instants, turning a continuous signal into a list of numbers.
Aliasing
The appearance of false lower frequencies when a signal is sampled too slowly.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sampling rate; the highest frequency that can be recorded faithfully.
Sampling theorem
A signal containing no frequency above B is completely determined by samples taken more than 2B times a second.
Moiré
The false wavy pattern that appears when a fine pattern is sampled by a grid of similar spacing.
