OSC14 · Oscillations & waves

Beats

Pluck two guitar strings that should be playing the same note. If they are slightly out of tune, you do not hear two notes. You hear one, throbbing: wah-wah-wah, louder, softer, louder. Tune one and the throbbing slows and stops.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 5–9 min

The 30-second version

Add two tones of nearly the same pitch and they drift in and out of step. When their peaks line up the sound is loud; when peak meets trough it is silent. The result is one tone with a loudness that rises and falls at a rate equal to the difference between the two pitches. Tune the strings until the throbbing stops, and they match.

0 · The story

A guitarist tunes the top string. First she plays the open string. Then she presses the string below it at the fifth fret, which should play the same note. She plucks them together and listens. There is a slow wobble in the sound: loud, soft, loud, soft, about once every second. She turns the tuning peg a little. The wobble slows to once every two seconds, then every four. Finally it stops. The two strings are now exactly the same pitch.

Piano tuners do the same with the three strings that sound together for each note: they listen for the slow throbbing and adjust until it dies away. Orchestras do it when the oboe sounds an A and everyone tunes to match.

Why does a small difference in pitch make a slow wobble in loudness? And how can the loudness change at all if both strings are just playing at steady volume?

1 · The illusion

“Two tones make two notes”

If you play two notes you expect to hear two notes. When they are far apart, you do. When they are very close, something else happens: you hear one note, in between, whose loudness pulses.

That slow pulse, much slower than either pitch, is a beat. It is a pattern that arises purely from adding two fast oscillations. Try it in the simulation.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1701Joseph Sauveur names the effect “beats” (battements) and uses the beats between organ pipes to work out the frequency of a note, one of the first such measurements.
  2. 1714Giuseppe Tartini notices that two loud notes can produce a third, lower tone not present in either.
  3. 1863Hermann von Helmholtz explains beats and the roughness of dissonant notes in On the Sensations of Tone.
  4. 1901Reginald Fessenden patents the “heterodyne” principle: mixing two radio frequencies to produce a beat at the difference.
  5. 1918Edwin Armstrong invents the superheterodyne receiver, used in nearly every radio since.

Try this

  1. With the difference at 1 Hz, the sum swells and fades once each second: one beat a second.
  2. Raise it to 2.5 Hz. The beats come more than twice as fast. The beat rate always equals the difference between the two tones.
  3. Set it to 0. The beats vanish and the sum is a steady tone: the two are in tune.
  4. Press Play the real sound (turn your volume down first) and hear the throb. Try a difference of 0.5 Hz, then 3 Hz.

4 · The reveal

Why the sum throbs

At one instant, the peaks of the two waves line up, and the sum is twice as big as either. Because one wave is a little quicker than the other, the pair slowly drift apart. A short time later, a peak of one meets a trough of the other, and they cancel: the sum is almost silent. Later still, they line up again. The time between two lined-up moments is exactly the time it takes the faster wave to gain one full swing on the slower, which is one second divided by the difference in their frequencies.

That explains the tuner’s trick. A difference of just 1 Hz is easy to hear as a one-per-second throb, even though both notes are at hundreds of cycles a second. Counting beats lets a tuner measure a tiny difference in pitch with just a clock and an ear.

Beats inside every radio

The same mathematics is used to move signals around. A radio receiver mixes the incoming signal, say at 100.0 MHz, with a signal it generates itself, say at 99.3 MHz. The mixture contains beats at the difference, 0.7 MHz, which is a much easier frequency to process than the original. That is the “heterodyne” idea, and the “superheterodyne” receiver based on it is inside almost every radio and television made in the last century.

The surprise

Two steady, fast tones can combine into a slow throb you could count on a stopwatch. The slow pattern isn’t in either tone. It is created by adding them, and its rate is their difference.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now inside every FM radio, a stage of the circuit is mixing the station you chose with a locally made signal and listening to the beat at the difference. Many cars with adaptive cruise control carry a radar that works by beats: it sends out a signal whose frequency is steadily sweeping, and the echo, returning a little later, beats with the signal being sent. The beat rate tells the car how far away the vehicle ahead is. And in an orchestra pit, an oboist is sounding an A at 440 Hz while a hundred players listen for beats against their own.

  • Instrument tuningMusicians and tuners listen for beats to fade to zero.
  • Radio receiversHeterodyne mixing shifts a signal to a convenient frequency.
  • Radar and speed gunsThe beat between the sent and reflected signal reveals distance or speed.
  • Acoustics and hearing testsBeats are used to test how finely the ear can distinguish pitch.
  • Laser and atomic clocksBeats between two lasers measure tiny differences in optical frequency.
  • Geology and engineeringBeats between vibration modes can show a structure has changed.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

The picture uses slow tones of 8 to 11 swings a second, so that the waves can be seen, while the button plays real audio tones around 440 Hz. Both tones have exactly the same strength, which gives a perfect fade to silence; if one were louder, the dips would not reach zero. Real instruments make a mix of overtones, each of which beats too, and beats faster than about 15 to 20 a second sound like a rough buzz rather than a slow throb. The sound depends on your speakers and the room.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Two nearby tones make a throb. You hear one tone with a pulsing loudness.
  2. Beat rate = difference in pitch. Tune until the beats slow to nothing.
  3. A slow pattern from fast waves. It shows up in sound, radio, radar and light.

Glossary and sources

Beat
A periodic swelling and fading of loudness caused by two tones of nearly equal pitch.
Beat frequency
The number of loud-soft cycles per second, equal to the difference of the two frequencies.
Envelope
The slowly varying outline of the loudness of a wave.
Heterodyne
Mixing two signals to produce one at the sum or the difference frequency.
