GRV04 · Gravity & orbits

Weightlessness in orbit

Astronauts on the space station float around the cabin, and most people assume that gravity is faint up there. It is not. They float for a better reason, and Einstein found it while sitting in a patent office.

Reading time ≈ 9 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 6–10 min

The 30-second version

What you feel as weight is the floor pushing up on you, not gravity itself. If you and the floor fall together, the floor has nothing to push against, and you feel weightless. The space station is in free fall around the Earth, with gravity still at about 89% of its surface value. They float because they are falling.

0 · The story

In 1907, Albert Einstein was working as a clerk in the Swiss patent office in Bern when an idea struck him. He later called it “the happiest thought of my life.” It was this: if a man falls freely from a roof, he does not feel his own weight.

Picture it. You are standing in a lift when the cable snaps. You and the lift fall together. The floor no longer presses your feet, because the floor is falling just as fast as you are. If you let go of a coin, it floats beside you. Inside the lift, gravity seems to have vanished.

That small image became the foundation of general relativity. And it explains something every child asks of a space-station video: why are the astronauts floating?

1 · The illusion

“Astronauts float because gravity is weak up there”

The photographs of astronauts drifting around, with no “down,” make it feel as if they have escaped Earth’s pull. And it is true that gravity gets weaker with height. But at the altitude of the space station, about 400 km, it has dropped only by about 11%. If you could stand on a very tall tower that high, you would weigh almost the same as on the ground.

So gravity is there. What has gone is the thing that makes you notice it. The simulation lets you take that away, a bit at a time.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1687Newton explains that an orbiting body is in free fall: it falls continuously, but moves sideways fast enough to keep missing the planet.
  2. 1907Einstein’s “happiest thought”: a person in free fall does not feel their own weight. It leads to the equivalence principle and general relativity.
  3. 1959NASA begins flying aircraft along parabolic arcs to give trainees about 20 to 25 seconds of weightlessness at a time.
  4. 1961Yuri Gagarin orbits the Earth. Weeks later Alan Shepard makes a short flight with about five minutes of weightlessness.
  5. 2000The International Space Station receives its first long-term crew; people have lived in free fall there continuously ever since.

3 · The model

A person stands on a bathroom scale in a lift. You choose how fast the lift falls, as a share of the pull of gravity. Then you press Release the ball to let go of a ball from hand height and see what it does inside the lift. The sliding background lines show the lift speeding up as it falls.

scale reading ∝ g − a (lift accelerating downward at a) free fall: a = g, so the reading is 0The scale measures the push between you and the floor. If the floor accelerates away at g, the push is zero. The ball, falling at g as well, stays beside you.

Try this

  1. At 0% (at rest) the scale reads 70 kg. Release the ball: it falls to the floor in about half a second.
  2. Set the lift to 50%. The scale now reads 35 kg, and the ball takes longer to land, as if gravity were half as strong.
  3. Set it to 100% (free fall). The scale reads zero. Release the ball and it just hovers, exactly where you let go.
  4. Notice that nothing in the lift reveals that gravity is still there. Inside a small closed lift, there is no way to tell this from floating in empty space. (Over a big enough room, the slightly different pull at different heights, the tide, would give it away.)

4 · The reveal

You never feel gravity

On the ground you feel your weight because the floor is pushing up on you, stopping you from falling. It is the floor’s push that you sense in your feet, your spine and your inner ear. Gravity itself pulls every part of you equally, so no part of you is squeezed against another, and nothing registers. That is why we feel light in an elevator that starts to descend and heavy as it starts to rise: the floor’s push changed.

Orbit takes this to the limit. The space station moves sideways at about 7.7 km/s, fast enough that as it falls, the Earth’s curve falls away beneath it. It is in permanent free fall. The station, the astronauts and everything inside all fall together, with no floor pushing on anything. It is not an absence of gravity, but an absence of support.

An idea with consequences

Einstein’s step was to say that this isn’t a coincidence. If falling cancels gravity so completely, then a laboratory in free fall is, as far as any experiment inside can tell, the same as one floating in empty space. Gravity and acceleration are, locally, the same thing. That is the equivalence principle, and from it Einstein built a new theory of gravity.

The surprise

Weightlessness is not the absence of gravity. It is the absence of anything holding you up. You are weightless any time you fall freely, in a lift, on a rollercoaster drop, or in orbit.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now astronauts aboard the International Space Station are in free fall, as people have been continuously since November 2000. Because their bodies get none of the loading that standing gives, they lose bone density at roughly 1% a month in weight-bearing bones, and spend about two hours a day exercising to fight it. And somewhere, an aircraft is flying a steep arc that gives its passengers about 20 seconds of the same free fall.

  • Space stationsLiving and working in continuous free fall is the ISS’s everyday reality.
  • Microgravity researchFlames, fluids, crystals and cells behave differently without weight, which lets scientists see what weight normally hides.
  • Astronaut trainingParabolic flights and drop towers give short bursts of free fall for practice.
  • RelativityThe equivalence principle, born here, underlies GPS corrections and black-hole physics.
  • MedicineStudies of astronauts reveal how bones, muscles and balance depend on gravity.
  • Everyday lifeThe brief “lightness” at the top of a swing or in a drop ride is a small free fall.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

This is an idealised lift in a uniform gravity field, with no air, so the ball falls only by gravity. The person is a rigid figure and the scale a perfect instrument. A real orbiting station is not perfectly weightless: tiny differences in gravity across it, drag from the thin air and rotation produce “microgravity” of about a millionth of g, not exactly zero. The release is instantaneous and the animation runs at half speed.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Apparent weight is the floor’s push. Not gravity itself.
  2. Free fall means no apparent weight. Whether in a lift, a drop, or an orbit.
  3. Gravity is still strong up there. About 89% of its surface value at the space station.

Glossary and sources

Free fall
Motion under gravity alone, with nothing else pushing on you.
Apparent weight
The force a scale or the floor exerts on you, which you perceive as weight.
Microgravity
The tiny, near-zero apparent gravity inside an orbiting spacecraft.
Equivalence principle
Free fall in a gravitational field is locally indistinguishable from floating in empty space.
