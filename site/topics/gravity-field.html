GRV01 · Gravity & orbits

Gravity as a field

Newton asked what would happen if you fired a cannonball from a mountain top, faster and faster. His answer explains the Moon, the International Space Station, and why astronauts float: they are all falling, and missing.

Reading time ≈ 10 minSimulation: Starter (S)Video: 8–12 min

The 30-second version

Every mass fills the space around it with a gravitational field, an arrow at each point showing which way, and how strongly, things fall. An orbit is just a fall that is so fast sideways that the ground curves away as quickly as the object drops. The astronauts aren’t beyond gravity. They are in constant, free fall.

0 · The story

Picture a very tall mountain, taller than the atmosphere, so that air can’t slow you down. On its summit sits a cannon, pointing sideways. You fire. The ball arcs and lands a mile away. You load more powder and fire again: it lands ten miles away. Again, harder: a hundred miles.

Now something strange happens. The Earth is round. As the ball flies farther, the ground curves away beneath it. At some speed the ball falls just as fast as the Earth bends away, so it never gets any closer to the ground. It keeps falling around the world.

Isaac Newton drew this thought experiment in the 1680s. It said something shocking: the Moon is not a different kind of thing from a cannonball. Both are falling. The Moon is just very fast sideways.

1 · The illusion

“Astronauts float because there is no gravity up there”

At the height of the International Space Station, about 400 km above us, gravity is still roughly 90% as strong as on the ground. If you could build a tower that tall and stand on it, you would weigh almost what you do now.

The astronauts float because they and the station are both falling around the Earth together. Fall in a lift whose cable has snapped, and you would float too, until the bottom. They simply never reach the bottom. The simulation below lets you fire your own cannon.

2 · Where the idea came from

  1. 1609–19Johannes Kepler finds that planets move on ellipses, sweeping equal areas in equal times. He can describe the motions but not explain them.
  2. c. 1680sIsaac Newton connects falling apples to orbiting planets with a single law: every mass attracts every other, with a force falling as the square of distance. He pictures a cannon on a mountain to make the point.
  3. 1687The Principia. With one law, Newton explains tides, comets, and Kepler’s ellipses.
  4. 1798Henry Cavendish measures the tiny attraction between lead balls and, from it, “weighs” the Earth.
  5. 1800sMathematicians such as Laplace and Poisson describe gravity as a field filling space, the same idea Faraday used for electricity.
  6. 1915Einstein recasts gravity as the curvature of space and time, but Newton’s field remains accurate for most everyday and orbital purposes.

3 · The model

You fire from the top of the tower on the right-hand side of the planet. Launch speed sets how fast the shot leaves, in kilometres per second, using real values for the Earth. The faint arrows around the planet are the gravitational field: they always point towards the centre and shrink quickly with distance.

g = G · M / r² (a field: an arrow at every point, towards the mass)Orbit speed at the surface ≈ 7.9 km/s. Escape speed = √2 × that ≈ 11.2 km/s. A faint trail marks every earlier shot so you can compare.

Try this

  1. Start at 4 km/s. The shot curves and hits the ground. Gravity pulled it down faster than the planet curved away.
  2. Raise to about 7.9 km/s and press Fire again. The path becomes a circle: the shot keeps falling and keeps missing.
  3. Try 9 km/s. You get a long egg-shaped ellipse that swings out and comes back. It moves slowest when it is farthest away.
  4. Go to 11.5 km/s. The shot doesn’t come back. Gravity keeps weakening faster than it can pull the shot round.

4 · The reveal

Falling around the world

The shot always falls toward the centre. What changes is the sideways speed. At 7.9 km/s, in each second it drops about five metres, and in that second the curve of the Earth drops away by about five metres too. It is falling the whole time and never gets any nearer. As Douglas Adams once put it, the trick to flying is to throw yourself at the ground and miss.

That’s also what makes weightlessness in orbit. If you and everything around you fall together, nothing presses on anything else. The scales read zero. You haven’t escaped gravity; you’ve removed everything that would let you feel it.

What “field” adds

Why call gravity a field and not just a force between two things? Because the arrows exist at every point in space, whether or not anything is there to feel them. The field of the Earth is already there around the planet, waiting; a satellite just samples it where it happens to be. This idea lets you ask “what is gravity here?” without naming a second object, and it is the doorway to Einstein’s picture in which the field is the curvature of space itself.

The surprise

There are not two laws, one for earthly falling and another for the heavens. The same rule governs an apple and the Moon. Newton unified them, and the test is that the Moon’s measured acceleration matches the apple’s, shrunk by the square of its distance.

5 · Where it shows up

Happening right now

Right now the International Space Station is racing round the Earth at about 7.7 km/s, once every 92 minutes, in continuous free fall. Satellites in the GPS constellation orbit at about 20,000 km up, going round twice a day, and communications satellites in geostationary orbit sit 35,786 km above the equator, circling once every 24 hours so that they hang over the same spot.

  • Satellite navigationGPS satellites’ orbits are computed from exactly this law.
  • Weather and communication satellitesGeostationary satellites hover above one spot because their orbit takes exactly one day.
  • Space missionsMission planners use the field to get probes to other planets using the least fuel.
  • Space stations and astronaut trainingOrbit means constant free fall, which is also reproduced by parabolic aircraft flights.
  • Geology and resourcesTiny variations in the field reveal underground structure, from oil to ice sheets.
  • TidesThe Moon’s field is slightly different on each side of the Earth, which is what stretches the oceans.

6 · Where the simulation lies

Honest limits

Earth is shown as a smooth ball with no atmosphere, so there is no drag, and no mountain on Earth is anywhere near tall enough to fire from above the air. The shot is fired exactly horizontally, instantly, at a given speed, with no rotation of the planet. The Earth is treated as a point-like source of gravity, and the orbit time is sped up so you can watch it. Real orbits are also disturbed by the Moon, the Sun, and the Earth’s lumpiness.

Remember

Three things to take away

  1. Orbit is falling and missing. Enough sideways speed keeps the ground curving away.
  2. Weightless is not gravity-free. Astronauts feel no weight because everything around them falls together.
  3. A field is an arrow everywhere. It’s there before anything arrives to feel it.

Glossary and sources

Gravitational field
The acceleration a small mass would feel at each point.
Orbital speed
The sideways speed at which falling matches the curve of the ground.
Escape speed
The speed above which an object never returns, √2 times the circular speed.
Free fall
Motion under gravity alone. You feel weightless in it.
